Brake Bedding Procedure — Why It Matters

Bedding lays a thin, even film of pad material across the rotor face. Until that film exists and is uniform, you have less braking than the parts you paid for can deliver. Do it wrong — most often by stopping and holding your foot on the pedal while the rotors are hot — and you lay the film down in patches instead, which is what the majority of people are actually feeling when they say their rotors are warped. The whole procedure takes about fifteen minutes, and the instruction sheet in your pad box outranks everything on this page.

Why this matters

A fresh set of pads and rotors does not brake at its rated capability on the first stop. New friction material is full of resins and binders left over from manufacturing, and the rotor face is bare cast iron with nothing on it. Stopping power climbs as those resins cook out of the pad surface and as pad material begins depositing onto the iron. Bedding is the deliberate, controlled version of a process that otherwise happens at random over your next few hundred miles of driving — including, possibly, during the one hard stop you needed.

The stakes are higher than feel. Skipped or botched bedding produces three specific failures: short-term fade on the first hard stop because the pad outgasses all at once under heat it has never seen; glazing, where the pad surface vitrifies into something glassy and slick that will not generate friction; and uneven deposits on the rotor, which produce a vibration through the pedal and steering wheel that most shops diagnose as a warped rotor and fix by selling you more rotors.

On a rig that tows, carries weight, or descends grades in low range, this is not an enthusiast detail. The first time you need the brakes to work at full capability on a loaded vehicle going downhill should not be the first time they have ever been hot.

Key point: Bedding is not about wearing the pad in. It is about transferring pad material onto the rotor evenly, and driving the volatiles out of the pad surface under controlled heat. Everything in the procedure — the speeds, the cool-downs, the rule against holding the pedal — serves one of those two goals.

What is actually happening

Brake friction works two ways at once. Abrasive friction is the pad mechanically scrubbing against cast iron, and it wears both parts. Adherent friction is pad material sliding against a layer of the same pad material already bonded to the rotor — the transfer layer. Most street compounds rely on a mix; semi-metallic pads lean abrasive, ceramic and many performance compounds lean heavily adherent and will not reach their rated friction coefficient without that layer in place.

During bedding you heat the pad face enough that its binder softens and deposits a microscopically thin coat onto the iron, then you keep heating and cooling it so that coat anneals into something tough and uniform. Done right, the rotor face ends up an even gray or gray-blue across its full swept width, with no bright iron and no dark patches.

The second job is outgassing. A green pad holds uncured resin. Heat it gradually and the resin bakes off the working surface in a controlled way. Heat it violently on the first stop and it gases off all at once, forming a film of vapor between pad and rotor that the pedal reads as sudden, frightening fade. Bake the surface past its limit and the resin fuses into glaze instead of burning off, which is permanent — a glazed pad does not recover, it gets replaced or resurfaced.

Before you bed anything

A bedding run means repeated hard braking at speed in a vehicle whose brakes were apart an hour ago. Confirm the brake job before you confirm the bed-in.

  1. Pump the pedal before you move. New pads sit with the caliper pistons retracted. Pump until the pedal comes up firm and stays firm. A pedal that sinks slowly under steady pressure means a leak or air — see bleeding brakes before you go anywhere.
  2. Check for leaks at every fitting you touched, with the pedal held down hard.
  3. Verify lug nut torque with a torque wrench, in the correct pattern, to the factory spec. See reading torque specs.
  4. Roll at walking pace in the driveway and stop. If the brakes do not bite, nothing further in this guide applies.
  5. Drive the first half mile gently, testing the pedal at 10 and 25 mph, before you make the first real stop.
  6. Pick the location. You need a stretch where you can brake hard, repeatedly, without traffic behind you: an empty industrial park, a closed business frontage road on a weekend, a long quiet rural road with good sight lines in both directions. Not a public arterial. Check your mirrors before every single stop.

The procedures, and why they disagree

There is no single correct bed-in, because the right amount of heat depends on the compound. A pad designed to work cold needs far less than one designed for track temperatures, and some pads arrive already burnished at the factory and need nothing at all. Published procedures from the people who make the friction material:

Source Procedure Afterward
ADVICS (the “30-30-30” rule) 30 moderate stops from 30 mph, with at least 30 seconds of cool-down between each. No hard or severe braking until 400–500 miles.
Brembo OE At least 30 stops of roughly 3 seconds each, light-to-medium deceleration, from varying starting speeds, with at least half a mile between stops. Several miles with little or no braking to cool.
Brembo Gran Turismo At least 20 stops from 60 mph down to 20 mph at about 60% of maximum deceleration. Do not come to a stop. 20–30 seconds between. At least 5 minutes of driving with little or no braking.
Centric / StopTech Ten partial stops from 60 mph down to 10 mph at 80–90% of the deceleration that would lock the wheels or trigger ABS, back to back, without letting the brakes cool between them. Cool without holding the pedal.
Hawk (street compounds) 6–10 stops from about 35 mph at moderate pressure, then 2–3 hard stops from 40–45 mph. Do not come to a complete stop. Park and let the brakes cool completely. Do not set the parking brake until cooling is finished. Race compounds use a different procedure.
Bosch No stop-count procedure. 400–500 miles of moderate driving. Avoid heavy braking through that window.
Akebono None — the pads ship pre-burnished. Drive moderately, avoid heavy braking for 400–500 miles.

Notice what the aggressive procedures and the gentle ones have in common: a defined number of decelerations, a rule about cooling, and a restriction on heavy braking for several hundred miles afterward. Notice also that two of them tell you to do nothing in particular. If your pad box contains a sheet, follow the sheet. If it does not, the generic street procedure below is a reasonable default for an ordinary semi-metallic or ceramic street pad on a stock-caliber brake system.

A generic street procedure

  1. Make 5 or 6 stops from roughly 30 mph, decelerating at moderate pressure — firm, but nowhere near panic braking. Let the vehicle roll a few hundred feet between stops rather than stopping dead.
  2. Make 4 or 5 stops from roughly 45 mph at noticeably harder pressure, down to about 10 mph. Do not come to a full stop. Accelerate back up between each one; that rolling time is the cool-down.
  3. Make 2 or 3 near-threshold stops from 50–55 mph down to about 10 mph — hard enough that you feel the nose load up and the pad work, light enough that ABS does not intervene. Still do not stop.
  4. Expect the brakes to smell. A hot, sharp, slightly acrid smell at this point is the resin outgassing and is normal. Visible smoke, a soft pedal, or the pedal going long means stop the procedure and let everything cool.
  5. Drive for 5 to 10 minutes using the brakes as little as possible. Highway or an open road is ideal. The rotors need airflow at speed, not a parking space.
  6. Park, and leave the brakes alone. Transmission in gear or in Park, chock a wheel if you are on any grade, and leave the parking brake off until the rotors are cool enough to touch. An infrared thermometer takes the guessing out of this.

Stop. Do not come to a complete stop and sit with your foot on the pedal during or immediately after the bedding run, and do not set the parking brake on hot rotors. A stationary pad clamped against a hot rotor transfers a thick slug of material into that one footprint — pad imprinting. The result is a raised patch on an otherwise even transfer layer, which the caliper then reads as a thickness change once per revolution: a judder through the pedal and steering wheel under braking. That symptom is almost universally sold as a warped rotor, and almost never is one. Plan the end of your bedding run so the vehicle is still rolling when the hard stops finish.

Prep that decides whether bedding works at all

Bedding deposits pad material onto whatever surface is there. If that surface is contaminated, you bed the contamination in.

  • New rotors ship coated. Manufacturers apply oil or a rust preventive for shipping. It has to come off the friction surfaces completely with brake cleaner and a clean rag or shop towel — both faces, until the towel comes away clean. Oil left on the iron soaks into the new pad and contaminates it permanently. Work in ventilation and read chemical handling first; brake cleaner is not a benign solvent.
  • Reused rotors carry the old pad’s transfer layer. You cannot bed a new compound cleanly on top of another compound’s film, and mixing deposits is a reliable way to produce judder on brand-new pads. Scuff the faces with medium emery or a sanding disc in a cross-hatch until the old glazing and deposits are gone and the surface is uniform, or have the rotors turned. If a reused rotor is below the minimum thickness cast into its hub, it gets replaced, not resurfaced.
  • Do not touch the friction faces with bare hands after cleaning. Skin oil is contamination.
  • Make sure the caliper slides and pins move freely before you start. A pad that cannot retract drags, overheats, and glazes itself during the bedding run.

How to tell whether it worked

Look at the rotor after it cools. A correctly bedded face is an even, dull gray to blue-gray across the whole swept area, with the machining finish still faintly visible under it. Check for these instead:

  • Bright bare iron in patches — the transfer layer did not form there. Usually too little heat, or a contaminated surface.
  • A dark, slightly raised patch or several evenly spaced ones — pad imprinting. This is the one that causes judder.
  • A glassy, shiny pad surface — glazing. The pad got too hot too fast. Light glaze sometimes scuffs off with emery; heavy glaze means new pads.
  • A blue heat band on the rotor hat or vents — the rotor itself got far hotter than bedding requires. Inspect for cracking before you trust it.

By feel, a properly bedded brake has a firmer, more immediate pedal than it did on the first stop of the run, bites consistently from cold, and is quiet. A brake that squeals, needs more pedal travel than it did before the job, or vibrates under moderate braking is telling you something went wrong — and the diagnosis is worth doing before you drive it loaded or towing.

The first few hundred miles

Bedding starts the transfer layer; normal driving finishes it. Most compounds need 300 to 500 miles of ordinary use to even the film out fully. Through that window, avoid repeated hard stops, avoid long steady drags down grades, and do not tow or load the vehicle heavily if you can avoid it. If you must descend a long grade in that period, use engine braking and a lower gear rather than riding the pedal — good practice afterward too.

Bedding is also not a one-time event. If you change pad compounds later, the new compound needs its own bed-in on a resurfaced or scuffed rotor. If a brake system has sat unused long enough to surface-rust the rotors, a few moderate stops clean the rust off and re-establish the film before it matters.

Common mistakes

  • Holding the pedal at a stop while the brakes are hot. The single most common cause of post-brake-job judder, and the most avoidable.
  • Setting the parking brake on hot rotors. Same mechanism at the rear, plus the risk of a drum-in-hat shoe bonding to the drum surface.
  • Treating one procedure as universal. The Centric near-threshold procedure on a soft OE-replacement pad will glaze it. The gentle 30-30-30 on an aggressive performance compound will not get it hot enough to work.
  • Bedding on a public road with traffic. Repeated hard braking in front of drivers who do not expect it is how a bedding run becomes a collision report.
  • Skipping the rotor cleaning. Shipping oil in the pad is permanent, and no amount of bedding fixes a contaminated pad.
  • New pads on unscuffed used rotors. The old compound’s film is still there, and the new pads will deposit over it unevenly.
  • Panic-stopping to “bed them quickly.” ABS intervention is not bedding — it is the pad skipping across the rotor. Threshold braking without ABS activation is the target.
  • Assuming judder means warped rotors. Measure runout and thickness variation with a dial indicator and a micrometer before replacing anything. Genuinely warped rotors exist, but uneven deposits are far more common and are often correctable by scuffing and re-bedding.
  • Bedding brakes that were not verified first. A bedding run amplifies every mistake in the brake job at the worst possible moment.

When to hand it over: If the pedal will not come up firm, if you cannot find the source of a leak, if judder persists after a correct re-bed on clean rotors, or if the rotors show cracking or heat damage, this is reasonable ground to stop and use a shop. Brakes are the one system on the vehicle with no redundancy you can improvise.
