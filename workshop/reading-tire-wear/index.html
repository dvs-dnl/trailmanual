Reading Tire Wear Patterns — What They Tell You

A tire is a twenty-thousand-mile recording of what your alignment, air pressure and suspension actually did — not what the printout said they would do. Three tread-depth readings across each tire and one pass of your fingertips across the blocks tell you whether you are looking at a pressure problem, an angle problem, a worn-parts problem or normal wear that needs rotating more often. Read it before you buy the next set, because the cause you do not find wears the new tires the same way on the same schedule.

11 min readApplies to any vehicleHow we source guides

Why this matters

Every other diagnostic tool in your garage tells you what is happening right now. A tire tells you what has been happening for twenty thousand miles. The rubber has been averaging your alignment, your air pressure, your damper condition and your driving over the whole life of the tread, and the shape it wore into is the readout.

That matters for two reasons. The first is money: a set of 33-inch all-terrains is several hundred dollars, and a misaligned front end or a soft shock can take half the life out of it. The second is that replacing tires without finding the cause means the new set wears the same way. People do this repeatedly — three sets in sixty thousand miles, each one scrubbed off the inside edge, each one replaced without anyone measuring a toe angle.

On a lifted rig the stakes go up again. Lifting a vehicle moves the control arms and the steering linkage through a different part of their arc, which changes camber, toe and caster whether or not anyone adjusted anything. Larger, heavier tires then amplify every geometry error they are handed.

Key point: Read the whole tread width, not the part of the tire you can see while it is on the vehicle. The inside shoulder is where the expensive problems hide, and it is the one face you cannot see standing next to the truck.

Measure first, then interpret

Wear patterns are often misread from a photograph and rarely misread from numbers. Before you decide what a tire is telling you, collect four things.

  1. Cold tire pressures, all five. Measure before driving — NHTSA defines cold as parked at least three hours, and a warm tire reads high. Compare against the placard on the driver's door jamb or B-pillar, not the number molded into the sidewall. The sidewall number is the tire's maximum, not your vehicle's spec. Write down what you found before you correct anything; the pressures that produced the wear are evidence.
  2. Tread depth at three points across each tire. Outer shoulder, center, inner shoulder, with a depth gauge rather than a glance. Record all twelve numbers. A tire that reads 7/32 outer, 6/32 center, 3/32 inner has told you more in three measurements than an hour of staring will.
  3. The feel of the tread block edges. Run your fingertips across the tread from the outside of the tire toward the inside, then back the other way. Smooth one direction and sharp or ramped the other is feathering. On aggressive tread patterns with large blocks this is nearly invisible and entirely obvious to your hand.
  4. A look at the inside shoulder. Turn the steering fully to one lock and look at the back of the front tire on that side, then the other lock for the other side. For the rears, get the vehicle safely on stands or use a mirror and a flashlight.

While you have the gauge out, check the two baselines. Tread is legally and practically done at 2/32 of an inch, which is where the molded wear bars sit flush with the surrounding tread, and which is what the upside-down penny shows you when Lincoln's whole head is visible. Age is the second baseline: the last four digits of the DOT code give the week and year of manufacture, and tire and vehicle makers commonly call for replacement somewhere between six and ten years regardless of remaining tread.

The patterns and what each one means

These are the patterns you will actually find, with the cause that explains most cases and the first thing worth checking.

What you see What it usually means Check first
Center band worn, shoulders good Over-inflation. The center of the tread carries the load and scrubs off first. Cold pressure vs. the door placard. On a lifted rig, also whoever last set the pressures by the sidewall number.
Both shoulders worn, center good Under-inflation, or chronic overloading. The edges carry the load the center should. Cold pressure, TPMS history, and whether the vehicle regularly carries weight the pressures were never raised for.
Inside shoulder only Negative camber, toe-out, or both. Very often worn control arm or trailing link bushings, sagged springs, or lost ride height rather than a maladjusted alignment. Ride height against spec, then bushings and ball joints for play. Align after the parts are right, never before.
Outside shoulder only Positive camber under load, or toe-in. On tall vehicles it is frequently body roll the dampers and sway bars are no longer controlling, which pushes the outside wheel into positive camber through every corner. Sway bar links and bushings, damper condition, then alignment. Hard cornering on a high center of gravity does this on its own.
Feathered or sawtoothed block edges Excessive toe, scrubbing the tire sideways as it rolls. Feathering on the inner edges points to toe-in; on the outer edges, toe-out. Toe. It is the last angle set in an alignment because camber, caster and ride height all move it.
Cupping or scalloping — dips around the circumference The tire is bouncing instead of staying loaded. Worn shocks or struts are the most likely cause; loose or bent suspension parts and out-of-balance assemblies also do it. Dampers first. An out-of-balance assembly tends to produce a more regular, concentric pattern; worn damping produces irregular scallops.
Heel-and-toe wear — each block ramped front to back Normal wear accelerated by missed rotations, and much more visible on open, aggressive all-terrain and mud-terrain tread. The leading edge of each block wears faster than the trailing edge. Rotation interval. This is the pattern most often mistaken for an alignment problem on off-road tires.
One flat patch A skid — locked brakes, a seized caliper, or a wheel that stopped turning under hard braking. A vehicle parked for a long time can also take a set. That corner's brakes. A dragging or locking caliper leaves other evidence too, including heat you can read with an infrared thermometer.
Diagonal patches, several evenly spaced Usually a rear tire that has never been rotated on a vehicle with a thrust angle or rear toe problem, sometimes a tire defect. Rear alignment, including thrust angle. Rear wear that mirrors itself left to right points at the rear axle, not the front.
Bulge, lump, or separation in the sidewall or tread Not wear. Structural damage, typically from a pothole or curb strike that broke the inner liner. Nothing. Replace the tire. See the warning at the end of this guide.

Patterns that look like alignment and are not

Alignment shops sell alignments, and a reader who walks in holding a tire worn on one edge will usually be sold one. Sometimes that is the right answer. Often the alignment is a symptom.

Air pressure outranks everything. Center or both-shoulder wear is a pressure story almost every time, and no alignment corrects it. Check all four cold before you spend money on angles.

Worn parts move the angles. A control arm bushing with play, a loose ball joint, a sagged coil, or a leaking air spring changes camber and toe continuously as the suspension loads and unloads. An alignment performed on worn parts measures the angles at rest and holds nothing in motion — the numbers come back in spec and the tire keeps wearing. Inspect for play and check ride height against spec before anyone puts the vehicle on an alignment rack.

Dampers control tire loading. A shock that has lost its gas charge cannot keep the tire planted, which produces cupping, and cannot control body roll, which produces outside shoulder wear. Neither one is an angle problem.

Load changes geometry. Weight in the back of a truck or trailer tongue weight shifts front caster and camber and rear toe. A rig that tows or carries gear can wear the outside front and inside rear while the alignment printout from the unloaded vehicle looks perfect.

Rotation hides and reveals. Rotating tires distributes wear and keeps a pattern from running away; skipping rotations lets heel-toe and cupping develop fully. NHTSA puts the interval at 5,000 to 8,000 miles where the manufacturer calls for rotation, and sooner if uneven wear appears. Staggered sizes front to rear, and some directional fitments, change or prevent rotation — check your manual.

What is different on a modified rig

Most published wear-pattern guidance assumes a stock passenger car. Four things change on a lifted truck or SUV running oversized tires.

  • The lift changed the geometry whether or not it came with parts to correct it. Raising ride height moves the control arms through a different arc, which usually adds camber change and almost always moves toe. A lift installed without an alignment afterward is one of the most common causes of inside shoulder wear on a built rig.
  • Aggressive tread wears louder. Large, widely spaced blocks on an all-terrain or mud-terrain show heel-toe wear far earlier and more visibly than a highway tread, and they get noisy as they do. The tire is often fine; it wants rotating more often than a street tire does.
  • Airing down is not the same as running low. Trail pressures are a deliberate, temporary choice for traction and sidewall compliance. Driving home on them is how you get both-shoulder wear and a hot, overworked sidewall. Air back up before pavement, every time.
  • Heavier tires punish worn parts faster. A 35-inch tire loads a ball joint, a bushing and a damper harder than the 31 the vehicle shipped with. Wear patterns that would have taken 40,000 miles to appear on a stock rig appear in 15,000 on a built one, which makes a tire inspection a genuinely useful early warning on a modified vehicle.

If the wear is on the front of a solid-axle rig and you also feel a shimmy at highway speed, read the pattern alongside the death wobble diagnostic. Worn track bar and tie rod end hardware shows up in both places.

What to do with what you found

Order matters. Working through it backwards is how people pay for an alignment twice.

  1. Set pressures to the placard, cold, all four plus the spare. If the wear is a pressure pattern, stop here and watch it over the next few thousand miles.
  2. Inspect for play and ride height. With the vehicle safely supported — see working under a vehicle — check ball joints, tie rod ends, control arm and link bushings, and wheel bearings for movement. Measure ride height side to side and against spec.
  3. Check the dampers. Leaking, loose, or dead shocks explain cupping and body-roll shoulder wear, and replacing them is a prerequisite to any alignment holding.
  4. Replace what is worn, then align. An alignment is the last step, not the first. Ask for a printout of before-and-after numbers, including rear toe and thrust angle; a front-only alignment on a vehicle with rear wear fixes nothing.
  5. Rotate, and start a record. Note mileage and the three depth readings per tire each time you rotate. Two rotations in, you will see which corner is losing tread faster, while there is still tread left to save.
  6. Torque the lug nuts properly on reassembly — to spec, in the correct pattern, with a torque wrench. See reading torque specs.

Common mistakes

  • Reading only the outside of the tire. The inside shoulder carries the expensive diagnosis and is invisible from where most people look.
  • Setting pressure from the sidewall. That number is the tire's maximum. Your vehicle's number is on the door jamb placard.
  • Checking pressure warm. A tire driven on reads several psi high, so you bleed air out of a correctly inflated tire and create the shoulder wear you were trying to prevent.
  • Aligning before inspecting. Angles measured on worn bushings and sagged springs are angles that do not exist once the vehicle is moving.
  • Treating heel-toe wear on an aggressive tire as a defect. It usually means the rotation interval is too long for that tread pattern.
  • Rotating a badly worn pattern onto the other axle without diagnosing it. That spreads the evidence across four tires and loses the information, which is why the measurements come before the rotation.
  • Replacing two tires and leaving the cause in place. The new pair wears the same way on the same schedule.
  • Driving home on trail pressures. A full day of highway miles at 18 psi overheats the sidewalls and scrubs both shoulders.
  • Trusting TPMS as a pressure gauge. It warns at significant underinflation, well past the point where wear begins. A monthly check with an actual gauge is what the system is there to back up.

Stop. Some of what you find on a tire is not a wear pattern to diagnose — it is a reason to take the vehicle out of service now. Exposed steel belts or cords, a bulge or lump in the sidewall or tread face, tread separating from the carcass, cracks deep enough to see into, or a tire worn to or past the wear bars are all failure in progress. A sidewall bulge is a broken inner liner holding pressure against nothing but the outer rubber, and it fails without warning at speed. Put the spare on and replace the tire; do not rotate it to the rear, and do not plan to get a few more months out of it. If you find the same damage on multiple tires, or wear you cannot account for after working through this guide, that is reasonable ground to hand it to a shop with the measurements you collected in hand.
