Thread Locker — Blue vs. Red vs. None

Blue is medium strength and comes apart with hand tools — it belongs on fasteners that vibrate loose and that you will service again. Red is high strength and needs roughly 250°C (480°F) of localized heat to break loose — it belongs on fasteners that are meant to stay put permanently. Most bolts on your vehicle need neither: the factory torque spec and a clean, correctly stretched fastener is the locking mechanism. Use thread locker where the service manual calls for it, where a fastener has already backed out on you, or where there is no other retention.

9 min readApplies to any vehicleHow we source guides

Why this matters

A properly torqued bolt is a spring. Tightening stretches it, and the tension it holds is what clamps the joint and keeps the threads from walking. A joint that stays clamped does not need adhesive. The bolts that back out are the ones where something defeats that clamp — high-frequency vibration, a short grip length that stores very little stretch, thermal cycling that relaxes the joint, or a soft or shifting mating surface.

That is the problem thread locker solves. It is an anaerobic acrylic adhesive: liquid in the bottle, where air keeps it from reacting, and cured once it is confined between metal threads where the air is excluded and metal ions start the reaction. Cured, it fills the clearance between the male and female threads, so the fastener cannot rattle in that clearance and work its way out.

Used in the wrong place it causes the failure it was supposed to prevent. Red on a bolt that has to come out at the next brake job turns a twenty-minute job into a torch-and-extractor job. Any grade in a blind hole in aluminum, over-applied, hydraulically loads the bottom of the hole and can crack the casting. And since the stuff is a lubricant until it cures, it changes what a given wrench reading actually means.

Key point: Thread locker is a retention device, not a substitute for correct torque, and not a fix for damaged threads. If a fastener keeps loosening, find out why before you reach for the bottle — a stretched bolt, a crushed spacer, or a hole with half its threads left will keep failing with adhesive on it.

The grades, by color

Color coding is broadly consistent across brands, but it is a convention, not a standard. Read the label. The numbers below are Loctite part numbers because they are the reference everyone else is matching; Permatex, Vibra-Tite, and the house brands have equivalents at each strength.

Grade Strength Use it on Removal
Purple
Loctite 222		 Low. About 6 N·m (50 lb-in) breakaway on an M10 test joint. Fasteners up to 1/4 in / 6 mm. Set screws, adjustment screws, small electrical and trim screws, instrument fasteners — anything a medium-strength grade would seize or shear. Ordinary hand tools, no heat.
Blue
Loctite 242, 243, 248 stick		 Medium. Roughly 20 N·m (about 180 lb-in) breakaway on an M10 test joint. The default for vehicle work, threads up to about M36. Bracket and accessory bolts, caliper bracket bolts, driveshaft and pinion-yoke bolts, skid-plate and bumper hardware, exhaust hangers, anything that vibrates and will be serviced again. 243 is the oil-tolerant version and the one to keep on the bench. Hand tools. A longer wrench, not a torch.
Green, wicking
Loctite 290		 Medium to high, applied by capillary action. Fasteners that are already assembled and torqued. It wicks into engaged threads from the outside — set screws, electrical studs, fasteners you discovered loose and do not want to disturb. Also seals weeping threads and porous welds. Hand tools if you are lucky; localized heat to about 260°C (500°F) if you are not.
Red
Loctite 262, 263, 271, 272		 High. Roughly 33 N·m (290 lb-in) breakaway on an M10 test joint, and more once pre-torqued. Permanent assemblies. Studs into blocks and heads, ring-gear bolts, suspension and steering fasteners where the manual specifies high strength, anything you do not intend to remove without heat. Localized heat to about 250°C (480°F), then disassemble while hot.
Press-fit / retaining compound
Loctite 638, 680		 High, for cylindrical fits rather than threads. Not a thread locker. Bearing races, worn keyways, slip-fit pulleys. Listed here only because it sits on the same shelf and is the wrong bottle to grab in a hurry. Heat and a press.

Each grade also has an operating temperature ceiling, and under the hood that matters. Purple and green are rated to about 150°C (300°F). Blue 243 runs to about 180°C (355°F). Red is the high-temperature family, and the specialist grades go higher still. Nothing on this list survives on an exhaust manifold or a turbo flange — that is what high-temperature anti-seize and lock washers are for.

When to use none

This is the part most guides skip, and it is the part that keeps you out of trouble.

  • Where the manual gives a dry torque spec and says nothing about thread locker. A torque figure assumes a friction condition. Add a lubricant and the same wrench reading produces more stretch than intended; add a cured adhesive and the next removal reads higher than the spec. Follow the manual — see reading torque specs.
  • Wheel studs and lug nuts. Lug nuts are a dry, tapered-seat, high-tension joint that gets checked and re-torqued by design. Nothing goes on those threads — not thread locker, not anti-seize, not oil.
  • Fasteners that already have retention. Prevailing-torque nuts (nylon-insert or distorted-thread), safety wire, cotter pins and castellated nuts, tab washers, and the thread-locker patch the factory already applied to the fastener. Double up only if the manual says to.
  • Torque-to-yield and single-use fasteners. Head bolts, many rod bolts, many suspension bolts. These are stretched past yield on purpose and replaced on removal. Adding an unspecified lubricant corrupts the torque-angle procedure.
  • Brake hydraulic fittings and any fluid passage. Thread locker is not a thread sealant and can migrate into a bore. Use the specified sealant, or nothing, and keep it out of brake and fuel systems entirely.
  • Plastic threads and plastic housings. Anaerobic adhesive needs metal to cure, so it will not lock the joint anyway, and the acrylic chemistry stress-cracks many thermoplastics.
  • Oxygen sensors, spark plugs, and anything with a specified coating. These ship with their own thread treatment or specify dry threads. Adding product changes the torque and can poison a sensor.

Applying it so it actually works

Most thread-locker failures are application failures, not product failures. The adhesive cannot cure in a gap it never reached, and it cannot cure on a film of oil it never displaced.

  1. Clean both halves. Brake cleaner or an aerosol parts cleaner down the hole and over the bolt threads, then blow dry. Oil, old cured adhesive, and loose rust all keep the product off the metal. Chase damaged threads first — a tap and die for cleanup, and thread repair if the threads are actually gone.
  2. Let it dry completely. Solvent trapped in a blind hole keeps the adhesive from curing and makes a mess of the next removal.
  3. Apply to the male threads, in the engagement zone. One bead along two or three threads, about a third of the way up from the tip, is enough on a typical bolt. The nut or hole carries it the rest of the way as the fastener threads in.
  4. For a blind hole, put it on the bolt, not down the hole. Filling the hole hydraulically loads the bottom when the bolt seats. In an aluminum casting that is how a boss cracks.
  5. For a through-hole with a nut, add a little where the nut will finish. Apply to the bolt threads and also to the few threads the nut ends up on, so the engaged length is wetted.
  6. Assemble and torque to spec right away. Threading in spreads the product; do not let it skin over first.
  7. Leave it alone while it cures. Fixture time on steel is on the order of 10 to 20 minutes; full strength takes 24 hours. Stainless steel, zinc and cadmium plating, aluminum, and anodized surfaces are all slower — they are the "passive" substrates. The modern primerless grades (243, 263) handle them without an activator; older grades (242, 271) may need Activator 7471 or 7649 on those surfaces, or a warmer shop and more patience.
  8. Do not load the joint before it cures. An uncured bolt is a lubricated bolt. If the vehicle has to move, it moves after the cure.

How much: A bead a couple of threads long, not a dipped bolt. Excess does not add strength — it squeezes out, runs onto the mating face, hardens where you did not want it, and in a blind hole it is a hydraulic problem. One drop does more than five.

Getting it back apart

Blue comes apart with hand tools. Expect the first movement to need more than the installation torque, give it a steady pull with a decent-length wrench, and do not snap at it with an impact until you know the fastener is not seized for some other reason.

Red does not come apart cold. The cure is thermoset acrylic, and the plan is thermal: localized heat on the nut or the bolt head to around 250°C (480°F), which softens the adhesive, then break it loose while it is still hot. Let it cool and it grips again. A small propane torch or a decent heat gun will do it on accessible hardware — before you light anything, read propane torch safety and know where the fuel lines, brake lines, wiring, and rubber are. An induction heater does the same job with no flame and is worth renting for work near anything flammable.

Do not confuse adhesive with corrosion. If the fastener is rusted rather than glued, the approach is different — penetrant, dwell time, and shock — and that is covered in breaking rusty bolts.

Afterwards, clean the threads before reassembly. Cured adhesive comes off with a tap or die, a wire brush, and a solvent soak. Reusing a bolt with hardened red still in the threads gives you a false torque reading on the way back in.

Common mistakes

  • Reaching for red because it sounds stronger. Strength is not the goal; the right retention for a serviceable joint is. Red on a caliper bracket bolt or a skid-plate bolt is a trap you set for your future self.
  • Treating it as a cure for a loose joint. A bolt that keeps backing out usually has a real cause — wrong torque, a stretched or wrong-grade fastener, a crushed bushing, or stripped threads. Find it. Fastener grades is often where the answer is.
  • Applying it over oily threads. The standard grades still need reasonably clean metal. Oil-tolerant 243 is forgiving of residual film, not of a dripping bolt.
  • Flooding a blind hole. Hydraulic lock cracks aluminum bosses and leaves you repairing the casting.
  • Ignoring cure time, then driving it. Twenty minutes of fixture strength is not 24 hours of full strength, and an uncured joint is a lubricated one.
  • Using it on plastic. It will not cure and it may craze the part.
  • Torquing to the dry spec with a wet thread, or the wet spec dry. The spec and the thread condition are a matched pair. If the manual specifies thread locker, torque with it applied; if it does not, do not add it and then use the same number.
  • Letting the bottle die. Anaerobic adhesive has a shelf life — roughly 18 to 24 months, less in a hot garage. Keep the cap on, store it cool, and never pour unused product back into the bottle; it has already seen air and will contaminate the rest.

What to actually buy

Two bottles cover almost everything a home shop does: a medium-strength blue in the oil-tolerant, primerless formulation, and a high-strength red for the handful of permanent fasteners. Buy the small bottles. A 10 ml bottle is hundreds of fasteners, and the clock on an opened bottle runs faster than most people use it up.

Add the wicking green if you do electrical or set-screw work, where backing a fastener out to treat it is not an option. Add the purple if you work on small screws — trim, instrument clusters, accessory brackets — where medium strength will shear the head off before it lets go. The stick form of blue is worth having for overhead and vertical work, where liquid runs out of the joint before the fastener goes in.
