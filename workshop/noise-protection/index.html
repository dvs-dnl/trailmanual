Hearing Protection — dB Ratings and When to Wear Them

Noise-induced hearing loss is the injury shop work hands out most reliably, and it never announces itself. There's no pain and no moment you can point to — an impact gun here, a grinding session there, and years later conversations in a noisy room stop making sense. The damage is cumulative and permanent: the inner-ear cells that loud noise destroys do not grow back. It's also the cheapest injury in the shop to prevent. A pair of foam earplugs costs less than a shop rag, and three numbers tell you exactly when you need them.

6 min readApplies to any vehicleHow we source guides
The short answer

85 dBA is the line. NIOSH's recommended limit is 85 dBA averaged over an 8-hour day, and every 3 dB above that cuts the safe time in half. An angle grinder on steel runs around 100 dB — which spends your entire day's safe dose in about 15 minutes. Most air and power tools in a shop sit above the line; most hand-tool work sits below it.

The test you always have with you: if you have to raise your voice to be heard by someone at arm's length, the noise around you is roughly 85 dB or more. Protection goes in before the trigger gets pulled, not after your ears start ringing.

What to use: correctly inserted foam plugs (NRR 28–33) cover nearly everything in a home shop. Earmuffs are better for on-and-off noise and greasy hands. For the loudest work — air hammers, long grinding sessions — wear plugs and muffs together.

Why this matters

Your inner ear converts sound to nerve signals using a few thousand microscopic hair cells, and loud noise physically destroys them. Unlike skin or bone, they never regenerate. Every loud exposure kills a few more, the losses add up across years, and the first symptoms — asking people to repeat themselves, ringing that won't quit, TV volume creeping up — arrive long after the damage that caused them. By the time hearing loss is noticeable, it's permanent. Hearing aids amplify what's left; nothing restores what's gone.

The trap is that noise damage recruits your own adaptation against you. Ears that ring or feel muffled after a loud session usually seem to recover by morning — that's called a temporary threshold shift, and it isn't recovery, it's tissue taking a hit and limping back partway. Repeat it enough times and the shift stops being temporary. "I'm used to loud tools" doesn't describe tolerance. It describes hearing you've already spent.

Tinnitus deserves its own mention: permanent ringing, buzzing, or hissing is a common outcome of shop-noise exposure, it frequently shows up before measurable hearing loss, and there is no cure. Plenty of people who shrugged off earplugs for years would trade a lot to get silence back.

Key point

If you have to raise your voice to be heard at arm's length, you're at roughly 85 dB — the level where protection is warranted. That test works in any shop, with no meter, every time.

How loud shop work actually is

Decibels are logarithmic, which is why the numbers mislead people. Every 3 dB doubles the sound energy reaching your ears; every 10 dB is ten times the energy and sounds about twice as loud. The gap between a 90 dB tool and a 100 dB tool isn't 11% — it's ten times the dose.

  • Normal conversation: ~60 dB. Baseline.
  • Air compressor (motor running, same room): 80–90 dB. Right at or over the line if you're near it.
  • Shop vac: 85–95 dB. Over the line for the length of the cleanup.
  • Angle grinder on steel: 95–105 dB. The workpiece is a speaker — grinding on thin sheet or a resonant frame rail runs louder than the bare tool spec.
  • Cutoff wheel, die grinder, wire wheel: 100+ dB.
  • Impact wrench: 100–110 dB, and it's next to your head when you're under the vehicle.
  • Air hammer / air chisel: 110–120 dB. Minutes of safe exposure, unprotected.
  • Hammer blows on steel: impulse peaks of 120–140 dB. Impulse noise — single sharp cracks — damages hearing out of proportion to how brief it feels.

Anything with a peak above about 140 dB can cause permanent damage from a single event. Hammering hardened steel, and certainly anything explosive like a tire bead seating with ether, lives in that territory.

The exposure math

NIOSH's recommended exposure limit is 85 dBA averaged over 8 hours, with a 3-dB exchange rate — each 3 dB increase halves the allowed time:

  • 85 dB — 8 hours
  • 88 dB — 4 hours
  • 91 dB — 2 hours
  • 94 dB — 1 hour
  • 97 dB — 30 minutes
  • 100 dB — 15 minutes
  • 103 dB — 7.5 minutes
  • 106 dB — under 4 minutes

Two things about that table. First, the doses add across the day — 10 minutes of grinding plus a half hour of impact work plus vacuuming the floor is one combined exposure, not three separate passes. Second, OSHA's legal workplace limit is higher (90 dBA with a more permissive exchange rate), but that's a regulatory compromise, not a safety target. NIOSH's number is the one built from the hearing-damage research. Free phone apps like NIOSH's own sound level meter will read your actual shop within a couple dB — worth doing once, because people reliably underestimate their loudest tools.

What NRR actually means

Every earplug and muff sold in the US carries a Noise Reduction Rating — the attenuation in decibels measured under ideal laboratory conditions with a perfect fit. Real-world performance is lower, sometimes drastically. The standard field estimate is to subtract 7 from the NRR (correcting for how noise is measured) and then halve the result for a casually fitted protector: an NRR 30 plug worn the way most people wear them delivers something like 11–12 dB of real protection.

The gap between label and reality is almost entirely fit. A foam plug rolled tight, inserted deep, and held while it expands performs near its rating. The same plug perched in the bowl of the ear — which is how most people wear them — barely works. A cheap plug worn correctly beats an expensive one worn wrong, every time. The check: with plugs seated properly, your own voice sounds boomy and hollow, like talking with your head in a barrel. If your voice sounds normal, the plugs aren't deep enough.

Choosing your protection

  • Foam plugs (NRR 28–33). The highest ratings available and nearly free. Roll the plug into a thin cylinder, reach over your head to pull the top of your ear up and back, insert deep, hold 20–30 seconds while it expands. Downsides: they take clean hands, and a plug rolled in greasy fingers carries the grease into your ear canal.
  • Pre-molded flanged plugs (NRR ~25). Washable silicone or rubber, insert without rolling — the right answer for shop hands that are already dirty. Slightly lower attenuation, far more likely to be worn correctly mid-job.
  • Earmuffs (NRR 22–31). Consistent protection with no insertion technique, and they go on and off in a second — which makes them the best match for intermittent noise, where plugs tempt you to skip the reinsert. Weakness: anything that crosses the seal. Safety-glasses temples, a hat brim, or a respirator strap under the cushion leaks noise straight through.
  • Electronic muffs. Microphones pass through speech and shop sounds at normal volume and clamp anything loud. Worth it if you work with another person or need to hear an engine while protected.
  • Doubling up (plugs + muffs). Adds roughly 5 dB over the better of the two — the correct setup for air hammers, extended grinding, or any sustained work over ~105 dB.

When to wear them

An honest mapping, tool by tool:

  • Every time, no exceptions: angle grinder, cutoff wheel, die grinder, wire wheel, impact wrench, air hammer, hammering on steel, and running an engine indoors with the exhaust open or disconnected.
  • For the duration of the session: shop vac, air compressor cycling in the same room, orbital or belt sanders.
  • Genuinely fine without: hand wrenching, diagnosis, assembly, fluid changes — most quiet work. Hearing protection all day every day isn't the standard; wearing it whenever a loud tool comes out is.

The habit that makes this work: stage your protection with the tools that need it. Muffs hanging on the grinder's hook, a box of plugs on the compressor — the decision gets made once, not renegotiated every time you're in a hurry.

Common mistakes

  • Foam plugs perched shallow. The most common failure. If most of the plug is visible outside your ear canal, you're getting a fraction of the rating.
  • Muffs clamped over glasses. The temple arms hold the cushion off your head and leak noise under the seal. Use thin-temple safety glasses, or switch to plugs — you'll be wearing eye protection for the same jobs anyway (see eye and hand protection).
  • Earbuds and music as "protection." Standard earbuds block almost nothing, and turning music up loud enough to hear over a grinder means you've added a second noise source inside your ear canal. Total exposure went up, not down.
  • Pulling muffs off "for one quick cut." The loud part of the job is the reason they were on. Fifteen seconds of unprotected cutoff wheel is a real dose — see the impulse numbers above.
  • Trusting brief exposure. Hammer strikes feel too short to matter. Impulse peaks are precisely the exposures your ears handle worst.
  • Treating end-of-day ringing as normal. Ringing is the damage report. A shop routine that ends in ringing ears is a routine that ends in hearing aids.

Ringing ears are the warning

Ringing, muffled hearing, or sounds seeming far away after shop time means you took a dose your ears couldn't handle — a temporary threshold shift. It feels recovered by morning; it is not fully recovered, and repeating it converts temporary loss to permanent loss. And separately: sudden hearing loss in one ear is a medical urgency — treatment works best within the first days. If it happens, see a doctor promptly, not eventually.
