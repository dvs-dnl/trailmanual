Jeep Wrangler JK / Body & Rust

Fender Flare Replacement (JK Wrangler)

Difficulty 2/51–3 hrs$90–4502007-2011, 2012-2018
Short answer

Fender flares on the JK crack, fade, and disintegrate from UV exposure, rock chips, and trail contact. Replacing them is the right move before they delaminate and damage the paint underneath — and aftermarket flat flares provide more tire clearance than the factory arched design.

The factory JK fender flares are a multi-piece plastic assembly: the main flare body, inner liner, and hardware clips. They are held by six to eight push-pin clips and two bolts per corner. After 60,000 miles of UV exposure the plastic becomes brittle and cracks from any trail contact — replacement is maintenance, not optional.

Flat replacement flares (Rugged Ridge, Bushwacker) remove the arched overhang of the factory flare and add 1–1.5 inches of additional tire clearance at the fender edge. This matters on a body-lifted or suspension-lifted JK where the tire has moved outward and the factory arch rubs at full droop. On a stock-height JK, flat flares are an aesthetic and durability upgrade. On a lifted JK with 35s, they are often a functional necessity.

Bushwacker's flat style is the highest-quality OEM-style replacement — thick polyurethane that flexes under trail contact rather than cracking, and a finish that accepts paint or holds the factory-style black. Rugged Ridge at $189 for a set of four is the value choice and fits cleanly. Mopar replacements match factory fit and finish exactly — correct choice if you want stock appearance restored.

Tube fenders — the next tier up

If you're running 37s or wheeling rock trails where sheet-metal-eating impacts are routine, tube fenders (Poison Spyder, RockHard 4x4) trade the flare-and-fender-liner assembly for a welded or bolt-in steel tube frame that clears far more tire and shrugs off impacts that would crack or dent a stock fender. A front pair runs $500-700 and takes 4-6 hours to install correctly since it usually involves cutting the factory inner fender back to the tube's mounting flange — this is a step beyond flare replacement and worth planning as its own project rather than a weekend swap. Tube fenders also drop weight versus the factory fender-and-flare assembly, which matters marginally for approach geometry on steep departure angles.

The procedure

  1. Locate all clip positions by feel along the flare edge — typically six to eight clips per flare.
  2. Use a trim removal tool to pop each clip. Work from the ends toward the center to prevent cracking.
  3. Remove the two retaining bolts at the front or rear edge of the flare (location varies by corner).
  4. Pull the flare free. Inspect the body panel underneath for rust or damage before installing the new flare.
  5. Install the new flare by aligning the clip holes and pressing firmly until each clip seats.
  6. Thread the retaining bolts and torque to the specified value (typically 8–10 ft-lbs — hand-tight is not adequate).
  7. Verify all clips are fully seated — a partially seated clip causes the flare to flex and rattle.

What to watch for

  • Clip condition: If the factory clips are broken or missing, the flare will rattle. Replacement clips are available at any dealer for $1–2 each — replace any that don't seat positively.
  • Paint chip inspection: The area under the flare often has paint chips from rock spray. Treat any rust with a converter before re-covering with the new flare.
  • Two-door vs. four-door fitment: Flare kits are sold as two-door (two front, two rear) or four-door (same) — verify fitment for your specific body before ordering.
  • Fender-coverage laws: A number of states have minimum fender-coverage requirements measured from the tire's centerline — narrow flat flares on tires that stick out past the body can put you out of compliance for street use. Check your state's regulation before going with a minimal-coverage flare on a wide tire and wheel combo.

Bottom line

Aftermarket flares are one of the cheapest ways to both clear bigger tires and protect the body — but match the flare to your tire and lift before buying. Tube or low-profile flares expose more tire (great for big builds, worse for road spray and local fender-coverage laws), while OE-style replacements keep it street-friendly. Decide which problem you're solving first: a daily-driven JK with stock-ish tires wants OE-style replacement for looks and longevity; a trail-focused build on 35s or bigger wants flat flares at minimum and should budget for tube fenders once the sheet metal starts taking real hits.

Tools required

  • socket set
  • trim removal tool
  • heat gun (for stubborn clips)
