Jeep Wrangler JK / Maintenance

Coolant Flush — 3.8L and 3.6L Pentastar

Difficulty 2/51.5–3 hrs$40–902007-2011, 2012-2018
Short answer

Drain the radiator, flush with distilled water until it runs clear, then refill with the correct Mopar coolant for your model year — HOAT for 2007–2012, OAT for 2013 and up. The two chemistries are not interchangeable, and mixing them is the most expensive mistake available in this job.

The JK cooling system holds about 11.4 quarts when completely dry, but a drain-and-fill through the radiator petcock only exchanges roughly 8 of those. The remaining 3-plus quarts sit in the block, heater core, and hoses. That 70 percent exchange is adequate to refresh chemistry on a healthy system every 5 years or 60,000 miles. If you are chasing contamination — sludge, rust color, an oil sheen on the surface — you need a real flush with distilled water, not a drain-and-fill.

2007 through 2012 JKs were filled with HOAT: Hybrid Organic Additive Technology, Mopar's pink-orange coolant to spec MS-9769. 2013 through 2018 JKs switched to OAT to spec MS-12106, dyed purple.

The difference is not marketing. HOAT carries silicate and phosphate inhibitors that plate a protective film onto iron and aluminum surfaces; OAT uses organic carboxylate acids that work by a different mechanism and deliberately contain no silicates. Blend the two and the silicates drop out of suspension as a gel — the gray-green sludge that clogs heater cores and radiator tubes and that no amount of flushing fully removes once it has set. Chrysler's own service literature calls out mixing as a cause of accelerated corrosion and water-pump seal failure.

If you bought the JK used and cannot confirm what is in it, treat the color as a hint and not proof (aftermarket "universal yellow" coolant fades to almost anything after a few years). Flush the system completely with distilled water before adding new fluid. Two extra gallons of distilled water costs $4 and eliminates the question.

Use distilled water, not tap. Tap water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium that precipitate as scale on the hottest surfaces in the system — which is to say, exactly where you least want an insulating layer. This matters more on the 3.6L, whose tighter coolant passages tolerate deposits poorly.

The petcock is plastic and brittle. It sits low on the passenger side of the radiator. Turn it gently, a quarter turn at a time, and expect that a 10-year-old one may weep after reinstall. A replacement is $12 (55056633AA) — have one on the bench before you start rather than discovering you need one with the system empty.

The pressure cap is on the coolant bottle, not the radiator. The JK uses a pressurized degas bottle, so the bottle cap is the system's pressure reference. If the cap is original and the JK is over 100,000 miles, replace it while you are in there. A weak cap lowers the boiling point and produces the intermittent overheat that owners chase for months through thermostats and water pumps.

The 3.6L Pentastar traps air. This is the single most common reason a coolant change goes badly. The 3.6L's coolant crossover sits high enough that an air pocket will hide there through a short idle, then migrate to the temperature sensor a day or two later and spike the gauge. The fix is procedural: leave the cap off, run the engine to full operating temperature with the heater on maximum heat and the fan on high, and keep topping off the bottle as the level drops. The heater core is the highest point in the circuit and running it open is how the trapped air gets a path out. Expect to add half a quart or more during this step.

Watch what comes out. Brown and murky means rust and a system overdue. Milky or chocolate-colored means oil in the coolant — stop, and check the oil for coolant contamination before you refill anything, because you may be looking at a head gasket rather than a service item. A thin oil film on the surface with otherwise clean coolant is usually transmission cooler seepage into the radiator on the automatics, which is a radiator replacement. See the JK radiator upgrade guide if you are already at that point.

Budget two gallons of premix for a drain-and-fill, three for a full flush. Total DIY cost lands at $45–$90. An independent shop charges $150–$250 for the same work, and a dealer machine flush runs $200–$300 — the machine does exchange more of the block volume than a gravity drain, which is the one honest argument in its favor.

Steps

  1. Park on level ground with the engine fully cold. Remove the pressure cap from the coolant bottle to break vacuum.
  2. Place a 12-quart drain pan under the radiator. Open the lower petcock on the passenger side — plastic, so turn it gently.
  3. Drain fully. About 8 quarts will come out over 10–15 minutes. Close the petcock.
  4. Full flush only: disconnect the lower radiator hose at the radiator, run distilled water into the upper hose port with a garden hose until the water running out the bottom is clear. This typically takes 5–10 gallons and several minutes.
  5. Full flush only: reconnect the lower hose, torque the clamp, and drain the residual rinse water out the petcock so you are not diluting the new premix.
  6. Refill the coolant bottle with the correct Mopar 50/50 premix to the MAX line. Do not use concentrate at full strength.
  7. Start the engine with the cap off and the heater set to full hot, fan on high.
  8. Run to operating temperature — roughly 15 minutes, until the upper radiator hose is hot and the electric fan has cycled at least once.
  9. Top off the bottle as the level drops and air burps out. Keep adding until the level holds steady at MAX with the engine running.
  10. Install the cap, drive a full heat cycle, let the Jeep cool completely, and recheck the level the next morning. Top off cold to the MIN–MAX band.
  11. Check the petcock, both radiator hose clamps, and the ground under the Jeep for drips after the first two drives.

Why it works

  • Driveway job with $50 of fluid and no specialty tools
  • Removes the silicate sludge that clogs heater cores and radiator tubes
  • Surfaces failing petcocks, hoses, and pressure caps before they strand you on a trail
  • Correct chemistry protects the aluminum water pump housing and radiator end tanks

Trade-offs

  • A gravity drain-and-fill only exchanges about 70 percent of the fluid
  • The plastic petcock is fragile enough that you should own a spare before starting
  • Mixing HOAT and OAT requires a full flush to undo, and set sludge may never fully clear
  • Pentastar air pockets can hide for a day or two if the bleed step is rushed
  • Used coolant is toxic to animals and requires a recycling drop-off

Tools required

  • drain pan (12+ quart)
  • 5/8" hose
  • funnel
  • channel lock pliers
  • ratchet set
  • hose clamp pliers
  • infrared thermometer (optional)
