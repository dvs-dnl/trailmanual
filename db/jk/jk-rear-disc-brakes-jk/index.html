Jeep Wrangler JK / Brakes

Rear Disc Brake Service

Difficulty 2/51–2 hrs$80–2202007-2018Jump to steps
Short answer

Rear disc brake service on a JK is a one-hour-per-side job: pull the caliper, compress the piston, swap pads, and torque the bracket bolts to your service manual's figure, since one forum list gives 77 ft-lbs and Project-JK gives 55.

Safety-critical system. Brake work decides whether the vehicle stops. Bleed the system, check for leaks, and test at walking speed before you drive it in traffic. Torque values and sequences come from the factory service manual for your year.

The JK runs disc brakes at all four corners from 2007 on, which is one of the upgrades JK owners take for granted until they go back and try to service drums on a TJ. Rear pads on a JK typically last 50,000 to 80,000 miles depending on tire size, towing, and how much you ride the brake in 4-low. Larger tires and aftermarket bumpers shorten that interval because the unsprung weight and total mass climb without a corresponding brake-system upgrade.

The factory rear pad is semi-metallic. Akebono ProACT ceramics (ACT1274) are the most common upgrade for owners who hate brake dust on the wheels. Ceramics also run cooler in stop-and-go driving but bite slightly softer when cold than semi-metallics. For tow rigs or anyone running 35s and up, PowerStop's Z36 truck-and-tow rear kit (K2798 covers fronts and rears) is the more honest answer than chasing exotic pads on stock-size rotors. You probably don't need slotted-and-drilled rotors for a JK that sees pavement and dirt — they look the part but the drilled holes are a stress riser, not a performance gain. And be clear about what pads can't fix: the stock master cylinder is the known weak point on lifted JKs with big tires, and if your pedal is soft or long with healthy pads, the answer lives in the front brake and master cylinder conversation, not another rear pad compound.

The drum-in-hat parking brake

The JK's parking brake is a small drum brake living inside the rear rotor's hat, with its own shoes, springs, and a star-wheel adjuster — completely separate from the hydraulic pads. On Jeeps that live in gear and rarely set the handle, the shoes glaze and the adjusters seize, and you find out when the rotor won't clear the shoes on the way off. Rotate the rotor until its access hole lines up with the star wheel, then back the adjuster off with a flat screwdriver before forcing anything. With the rotor off, inspect shoe lining and the adjuster threads; replacement shoe sets run about $40 and this is the only economical moment to do them. To adjust after reassembly: spin the star wheel out until the shoes drag lightly on the new rotor, then back off six to eight clicks until the rotor spins free. A parking brake that won't hold on a hill after a rotor swap almost always needs this adjustment, not new parts.

Fluid, heat, and water crossings

Brake fluid is the most neglected part of the JK's brake system. DOT 3 is hygroscopic — it absorbs water from the air continuously — and at 3% water content its boiling point drops by roughly a third. A trail JK compounds this with water crossings that soak the system in humidity and long 4-low descents where dragging the pedal cooks the rear brakes. Boiled fluid is that terrifying pedal-to-the-floor moment at the bottom of a grade. Flush the system every two to three years, or after a season with real water crossings; if the fluid in the master cylinder reservoir is dark brown instead of honey-colored, it's overdue. While the wheel is off for pad service, also eyeball the flex hoses at the axle — lifted JKs stretch them — per the brake line inspection guide.

Pulsation and the runout trap

Pedal pulsation after a brake job gets blamed on "warped rotors," but the usual culprits are a rust ridge on the hub face and uneven lug torque. Any scale between hub and rotor hat tilts the rotor a few thousandths — enough to produce runout that wears into thickness variation within a few thousand miles. Wire-brush the hub face to bare, flat metal before the new rotor goes on. Then torque the lugs with an actual torque wrench in a star pattern; an impact gun hammering one lug to 140 ft-lbs while its neighbor sits at 80 distorts the hat. The other pulsation source is uneven pad material transfer from skipping the bedding procedure — those 8–10 moderate stops at the end aren't ceremony, they lay down the even transfer film the pads need.

Torque the rear caliper guide pin bolts to 22 ft-lbs. The bracket bolts are where published numbers split: the forum thread cited above gives 77 ft-lbs, while Project-JK's torque list gives 55 ft-lbs for the rear caliper adapter. Check a factory service manual for your year before you torque them. Lug nuts go to 95 ft-lbs in a star pattern. While the caliper is off, pull the guide pins, wipe them, and re-grease with silicone brake grease — dry pins bind the caliper and chew the outboard pad on one side. Torn pin boots let water in; replace them, they're a few dollars. Pump the pedal a dozen times before driving to seat the pad against the rotor — the first stop with new pads should be at low speed in a parking lot, not on the freeway.

Steps

  1. Loosen rear lug nuts, then jack the rear axle and set jack stands under the axle tubes. Pull the wheel.
  2. Remove the two caliper guide pin bolts (13mm head, hex internal on some years) and lift the caliper off. Hang it from a coil with mechanic's wire — do not let it dangle on the hose.
  3. Slide the old pads out of the bracket. Note clip and shim orientation; new pads usually include fresh hardware.
  4. Remove the two caliper bracket bolts (21mm) and pull the bracket off so you can remove the rotor.
  5. Pull the rotor straight off. If it's stuck, back off the parking brake adjuster through the access hole, then strike the rotor hat with a deadblow.
  6. Wire-brush the hub face flat. Any rust ridge here will cause runout and pulsation.
  7. Install the new rotor. Reinstall the caliper bracket and torque it to the service manual figure (published values are 55 or 77 ft-lbs).
  8. Compress the caliper piston with a c-clamp or piston tool. Open the master cylinder cap first and watch the level so it doesn't overflow.
  9. Clean and re-grease the caliper guide pins with silicone brake grease; replace any torn pin boots.
  10. Install new pads with anti-squeal compound on the back of the pad ears, slide the caliper over, and torque guide pin bolts to 22 ft-lbs.
  11. Adjust the parking brake star wheel until the shoes drag, then back off 6–8 clicks to free rotation.
  12. Reinstall the wheel, torque lug nuts to 95 ft-lbs in a star pattern, lower the Jeep, pump the pedal until firm, then bed the pads with 8–10 moderate stops from 35 mph.

Why it works

  • One-hour-per-side job with hand tools
  • Big improvement in pedal feel after even basic pad-and-rotor refresh
  • Drum-in-hat parking brake can be inspected and adjusted at the same time
  • Guide-pin service during the pad swap prevents the most common uneven-wear comeback

Trade-offs

  • Parking brake adjusters seize and complicate rotor removal
  • Stock master cylinder is a known weak point on lifted JKs running 35s+ — pads alone won't fix soft pedal
  • Caliper guide pins need fresh grease every service or they bind and chew pads unevenly
  • Neglected fluid boils on long descents — pad service without a fluid flush is half a brake job

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • 18mm socket
  • 21mm socket for caliper bracket
  • c-clamp or piston compressor
  • torque wrench
  • brake cleaner
  • wire brush
