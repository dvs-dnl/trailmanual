Wheel Spacers — 1.25" and 1.75" Bolt-On

Difficulty 1/51 hrs$89–1852007-2011, 2012-2018

Wheel spacers move the tire outward to improve clearance or widen track width — hub-centric bolt-on spacers from Spidertrax or Teraflex are the safe choice; lug-centric spacers without proper hub registration are not.

Wheel spacers solve a specific problem: existing wheels with insufficient backspacing for a new lift height. If your wheels came spec'd for a 1.5" lift and you've added 3", the backspacing may now cause the tire to contact the upper control arm bracket at full compression. A 1.25" spacer moves the wheel outboard enough to provide clearance without buying a new set of wheels. They're also used intentionally to widen track width for stability — on a tall JK with a heavy overland setup, slightly wider track reduces body roll. Spidertrax's WHS007 at $135/pair is the most trusted spacer option for the JK — hub-centric design, 6061 aluminum billet, and they use the correct bore to sit fully on the hub face before the lug nuts load.

The hub-centric versus lug-centric distinction is the most important safety consideration in this category. Hub-centric spacers register on the wheel hub center bore (71.5mm on the JK) — the hub bore is what carries the vehicle's weight, and the lug nuts hold the assembly in place. Lug-centric spacers have a larger center bore and rely on the lug nuts to carry the load, which stresses the studs and causes vibration and premature wear. Every spacer listed here is hub-centric — do not purchase spacers that don't specify this. On budget spacers without hub registration, the vibration they induce at highway speed is often mistaken for a wheel balance or bearing issue.

Torque spec discipline matters on spacers. The spacer mounts to the hub on the factory studs; the wheel then mounts to the studs pressed into the spacer. Torque the spacer to the hub at 95–100 ft-lbs, then torque the wheel to the spacer at the same spec. After the first 50 miles, re-torque the spacer-to-hub interface — spacers can loosen slightly on initial seating. Thick spacers (1.75"+) may push the wheel far enough outboard to reduce effective thread engagement on the factory studs — check that you have at least one diameter of stud engagement past the wheel mounting surface, or purchase extended wheel studs.

Spacers make sense when they correct a specific fitment problem or when the alternative (new wheels) is disproportionately more expensive. If you're building from scratch or choosing wheels for a new tire size, buying wheels with the correct backspacing is cleaner and lighter than adding spacers. Spacers add approximately 2–4 lbs per corner in unsprung weight and introduce one more assembly interface that can loosen over time.

Run the numbers before defaulting to spacers. A quality hub-centric pair set for all four corners is $270–$370 (Spidertrax x2). A set of five aftermarket wheels with correct backspacing for 35s (4.5" backspacing on a 17x9 is the standard JK recipe) runs $800–$1,400 before tires are remounted, plus $100–$150 in mount/balance labor. If your current wheels are factory takeoffs you plan to replace at the next tire size anyway, spacers are a stopgap you'll pay for twice. If your wheels are already the ones you want — beadlock-capable, correct load rating, you like them — spacers are the permanent, cheaper answer. See the wheel selection guide for the backspacing targets by tire size and the 37" fitment guide for where spacers stop being enough.

Moving the wheel centerline outboard increases scrub radius — the lever arm between the steering axis and the tire's contact patch. Practical effects on a JK with 1.5–1.75" spacers: heavier steering effort at parking speeds, more feedback (kick) through the wheel when one front tire hits a bump or brakes on split traction, and measurably higher loads on the unit bearings, ball joints, and outer tie rod ends. None of this is dangerous on a maintained front end; all of it accelerates wear on marginal parts. If the front end has play — rock the tire at 12 and 6 with the Jeep on stands — fix the tie rods and ends before adding track width, because spacers amplify existing looseness into wander.

The spacer interface is a second lug joint, and it's hidden behind the wheel where you can't see it loosen. The maintenance rhythm that makes spacers boring (in the good way): re-torque spacer-to-hub at 50 miles after install, again at 500, then check at every tire rotation. Use no thread locker on lug hardware — correct torque on clean, dry threads is the spec; red thread locker makes future removal a torch job and can give a false torque reading on install. Mark each spacer nut with a paint pen after the 500-mile check so a loosening nut shows itself at a glance. When a tire shop touches the Jeep, tell them the spacers are there — impact guns hammering the wheel nuts to 140 ft-lb while the spacer-to-hub nuts sit at 95 is how mixed-torque vibrations start.

Steps

  1. Lift the vehicle and place on jack stands; remove the wheel.
  2. Clean the hub face and wheel mounting surface — debris under the spacer causes runout.
  3. Slide the spacer onto the hub, ensuring the center bore fully seats over the hub register.
  4. Thread the spacer-to-hub lug nuts by hand before torquing.
  5. Torque the spacer to the hub at 95–100 ft-lbs in a star pattern.
  6. Mount the wheel to the spacer studs and torque to 95–100 ft-lbs.
  7. Reinstall and repeat for all four corners.
  8. Re-torque all spacer lug nuts after 50 miles.

Why it works

  • Solves backspacing fitment problems without purchasing new wheels
  • Hub-centric design eliminates vibration issues associated with lug-centric budget spacers
  • 1.25" spacer typically costs less than replacing all four wheels with different backspacing
  • Wider track width slightly improves stability on tall builds with heavy gear

Trade-offs

  • Thick spacers reduce thread engagement on factory studs — verify engagement length before use
  • Adds two torque check points per corner versus running no spacers
  • Lug-centric budget spacers (not on this list) are a safety hazard — avoid them
  • Does not address the underlying backspacing issue as cleanly as choosing the correct wheel

Tools required

  • torque wrench
  • lug wrench
  • floor jack
  • jack stands
