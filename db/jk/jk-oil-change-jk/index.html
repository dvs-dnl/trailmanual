Jeep Wrangler JK / Maintenance

Oil Change — Pentastar 3.6L Full Procedure

Difficulty 1/50.5–1 hrs$45–902012-2018Jump to steps
Short answer

Use 5W-20 full synthetic meeting Chrysler MS-6395 (confirm the grade on your oil fill cap or in the owner's manual) and a cartridge filter; have 6 quarts on hand and fill to the dipstick. The 2014–2018 JK shares its plastic filter cap with the 2018–2019 JL, and Quadratec's Pentastar oil change how-to tightens that cap to 18 ft-lb with a 24mm socket and the 13mm drain plug to 20 ft-lb on a fresh crush washer. Total job runs 30–60 minutes and costs $45–$90 in parts.

The 3.6L Pentastar that arrived in JKs for 2012 uses a top-mount cartridge filter — a plastic cap on top of the engine, not a spin-on canister underneath. This is the detail most owners coming from the 3.8L V6 or another platform get wrong on their first change, and getting it wrong costs an $85 housing.

Oil spec, and why the grade matters here

Mopar calls for 5W-20 meeting MS-6395. Any quality full synthetic with an API SN or SN+ rating meets that — there is no measurable advantage to running OEM-branded oil over a Mobil 1, Pennzoil Platinum, or Valvoline Advanced of the same grade. Capacity is 6.0 US quarts with a filter change.

Resist the temptation to run a heavier grade. The Pentastar's variable valve timing system actuates the cam phasers with engine oil pressure through very small control passages, and the phaser solenoids are calibrated around 5W-20 viscosity. Owners in hot climates sometimes step up to 5W-30 reasoning that thicker is safer at 115°F. What actually happens is slower phaser response at cold start, and on the 2012–2013 engines that already have a known cam-phaser rattle history, a heavier oil makes the morning tick worse rather than better. Stay at 5W-20.

Overfilling is the other common error. Push past the upper hatch mark on the dipstick and crankcase pressure drives oil mist into the intake tract through the PCV system. Pentastars are port-injected on the JK, so the intake valves are washed by fuel and this is less catastrophic than on a direct-injected engine — but oil carryover still coats the throttle body and contributes to the rough-idle-after-oil-change complaint that shows up on forums every spring. Fill to 6 quarts, check, and stop.

Interval. The oil life monitor typically calls for a change between 6,000 and 8,000 miles on a street-driven JK. Trail use compresses that hard. Long periods at low road speed with the engine loaded — crawling, sand, towing in the heat — put heat into the oil without the airflow that normally sheds it, and dusty trail running loads the filter faster than pavement miles. If your JK sees regular dirt, run 5,000 miles regardless of what the monitor says. See the maintenance category for the JK for the rest of the fluid schedule.

The filter housing — the part that breaks

The cartridge filter sits in a plastic housing on top of the engine, behind the throttle body, angled toward the passenger side. Use a 24mm socket on the cap's hex. The housing cracks if you use channel locks or a strap wrench, because the plastic embrittles after a few hundred heat cycles.

Mopar 68191349AC is the OEM cartridge and ships with a new O-ring in the box. FRAM CH11665, Wix 57082, and Purolator equivalents fit and filter fine; confirm the O-ring is included, because the old one will not reseal reliably once it has been compressed and heat-cycled.

The torque number is the one to respect. Quadratec's how-to gives 18 ft-lb, the only published figure we found, so treat it as a ceiling and check your service manual. That is roughly hand-tight plus a nudge, not the arm-strength you would use on a spin-on filter. Strip the cap threads and the fix is a new housing assembly (68105583AF, about $85 plus an hour of labor) because the threads are molded into the housing, not a replaceable insert. A torque wrench that reads in the 15–25 ft-lb range is worth owning for this one fastener alone.

Drain plug. 13mm hex on the oil pan, passenger side, angled slightly rearward. Torque is 20 ft-lb on a fresh crush washer. Reusing the washer is the single most common cause of the slow drip that shows up on the skid plate a week later — they cost about a dollar, buy five.

A DIY change lands at $40–$55 in consumables. A quick-lube shop charges $75–$110 for the same synthetic-and-cartridge job, and is also where most stripped filter housings originate — the flat-rate tech reaching for an impact driver is exactly the failure mode the plastic threads cannot survive.

Steps

  1. Warm the engine for 2–3 minutes to thin the oil, then shut it off. Hot oil drains faster and carries more suspended contamination out with it; scalding-hot oil is a burn risk, so warm is the target, not fully up to temperature.
  2. Remove the engine cover (pull straight up — friction fit on four ball studs).
  3. Loosen the oil filter cap one full turn to break the seal. This lets the oil trapped in the housing drain down into the pan before you remove it, which keeps oil off the intake manifold and the front axle.
  4. Position the drain pan under the oil pan plug, offset a few inches rearward — the initial stream shoots back, not straight down.
  5. Remove the 13mm drain plug. Let it drain a full 5 minutes; the last quart comes out slowly.
  6. Remove the filter cap completely and lift out the old cartridge. Wipe the housing bore with a clean lint-free rag.
  7. Peel the old O-ring off the cap groove. Install the new O-ring and lubricate it with fresh oil — a dry O-ring rolls out of the groove on install and leaks immediately.
  8. Snap the new cartridge into the cap, then thread the cap in by hand until it seats. Tighten with a 24mm socket, no more than 18 ft-lb.
  9. Install the drain plug with a new crush washer. Torque to 20 ft-lb.
  10. Pour in 5 quarts of 5W-20 synthetic through the oil fill cap, check the dipstick, and top up toward the full mark. Start the engine and verify the oil pressure light goes out within 3 seconds.
  11. Shut off, wait 5 minutes for drain-back, then check the dipstick. Top off only if below the lower hatch mark.
  12. Reset the oil life monitor: ignition on without cranking, press the accelerator fully to the floor three times within 10 seconds, then cycle the ignition off.

Why it works

  • Top-mount filter means no crawling under the truck for the filter half of the job
  • Standard 13mm drain plug, no proprietary tooling
  • Parts stock at any auto parts store on any given day
  • 30–60 minutes once you have done it a second time

Trade-offs

  • Plastic filter housing cracks if you use anything but the correct cap socket
  • Stripped cap threads mean a new housing (~$85 part) — the threads are not serviceable
  • Oil runs down the back of the engine if you pull the drain plug before cracking the filter cap
  • The oil life monitor reset sequence is non-obvious and has to be done by hand

Tools required

  • 13mm socket
  • 24mm socket (oil filter cap)
  • drain pan (6+ quart)
  • torque wrench (in-lb capable)
  • floor jack and stands (optional)
  • shop rags
