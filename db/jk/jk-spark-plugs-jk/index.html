Spark Plug Replacement — 3.8L and 3.6L

Difficulty 2/51–2.5 hrs$35–902007-2018
Short answer

The 3.8L uses two plugs per cylinder (12 total) and the 3.6L uses one per cylinder (6 total). Both are coil-on-plug. Gap and torque matter: 3.8L at 0.043", 13 ft-lb; 3.6L at 0.043", 13 ft-lb. Change interval is 30K (3.8L copper) or 100K (Pentastar iridium).

The 3.8L V6 (2007–2011) and the 3.6L Pentastar (2012–2018) take very different approaches. Both are coil-on-plug systems, but the 3.8L runs a dual-plug-per-cylinder setup for emissions — two plugs, two coil packs on each side of each cylinder, twelve plugs total. The Pentastar runs one plug per cylinder with a single coil-on-plug at each, six total.

3.8L plug choice. Mopar spec is NGK ZFR5LP-13G platinum, gapped at 0.043". You can run copper plugs (NGK ZFR5N) for cheaper if you don't mind changing them every 30K miles. The platinum plugs go 60K. Iridium upgrades are pointless on this engine — the 3.8L doesn't reward fancier plugs and they cost 3x more.

3.6L plug choice. Mopar spec is the Champion RE10PMC5 (also sold as NGK PLZKAR6A-11), iridium, gapped at 0.043". Service interval is 100K miles. Do not regap iridium plugs — the precious metal tip is thin and chips. They come pre-gapped from the factory; check with a wire gauge but don't bend.

Access. 3.8L driver-side plugs require pulling the intake plenum on the rear three. Plan for 2+ hours if it's your first time. 3.6L is dramatically better — pull the engine cover, disconnect 6 coil connectors, remove 6 coil bolts, and the plugs are right there.

Torque is critical. Pentastar uses aluminum heads. Overtightening strips threads, undertightening lets the plug back out under combustion pressure. Use a torque wrench. 13 ft-lb on both engines.

The plugs you pull out are a free diagnostic report on the engine. A light tan or gray electrode is a healthy burn. Black, dry, sooty deposits mean a rich mixture or a Jeep that only sees short trips — the plug never gets hot enough to self-clean. Wet, oily threads or an oil-soaked boot point at leaking valve cover gaskets seeping into the plug wells, which is common on higher-mileage Pentastars; replace the valve cover gasket at the same time or the new coil boot will be swimming within a year. White, blistered porcelain is the one that should stop you: that's an overheated plug from a lean condition, a vacuum leak, or a cooling problem, and swapping in fresh plugs without finding the cause treats the symptom only.

One plug that looks different from its five (or eleven) siblings is a cylinder-specific problem. On 2012–2013 3.6L engines, a persistent misfire on the left bank — especially cylinder 2 — with a ticking noise is the known early-Pentastar cylinder head defect, which FCA covered under an extended 10-year/150,000-mile head warranty. Confirm which cylinder is misfiring with a scan tool before assuming plugs will fix it — see OBD2 diagnostics for the JK for pulling cylinder-specific misfire counters.

A P0300 (random misfire) or P030X (cylinder-specific) code doesn't automatically mean plugs. The 60-second test that saves a parts-cannon session: swap the suspect cylinder's coil with a neighbor and clear the code. If the misfire follows the coil, buy a coil (~$45–$70 for a quality Mopar or NGK unit; skip the $15 no-name packs, their internal insulation fails early). If it stays with the cylinder, then plugs, injector, or compression are on the table in that order of likelihood and cost. Weak or corroded grounds also mimic ignition faults on the 3.6L — if you're chasing an intermittent multi-cylinder misfire with clean plugs and known-good coils, work through battery cables and grounds before replacing more ignition parts.

The 100K Pentastar interval assumes highway duty. Deep water crossings, dusty trail air, and long idling sessions all shorten plug life: water wicking into the plug wells corrodes the boot spring and seat, and dust that gets past a worn air filter erodes electrodes faster. A practical schedule for a trail-driven JK is plug inspection (pull one, read it, reinstall) every 30K and replacement at 60–80K even on iridium. Budget the job realistically: 3.6L is $60–$80 in plugs and an hour of work; 3.8L is $35–$70 in plugs but budget the full afternoon for the plenum. If you're planning a Pentastar tune, do plugs first — a tune leaning on tired ignition hardware will surface misfires the stock calibration masked.

Steps

  1. Engine cold. Disconnect negative battery cable.
  2. 3.6L: Pull the engine cover (friction fit). Disconnect each coil connector. Remove the 10mm coil hold-down bolt. Pull each coil straight up.
  3. 3.8L: Same idea but you have a coil on each side of each cylinder. Driver-side rear three require intake plenum removal — pull the 4 intake bolts and lift it aside, leaving fuel lines connected if possible.
  4. Use compressed air to blow debris out of the plug well before pulling the plug — dirt falling into the cylinder is a real risk.
  5. Use a magnetic or rubber-insert plug socket to back out the old plug. Check the porcelain for cracking and the electrode for excessive wear (gap should still be within 0.005" of spec).
  6. Verify gap on new plugs with a wire gauge (3.6L: 0.043"; 3.8L: 0.043"). Do not regap iridium.
  7. Skip anti-seize on factory-coated plugs (most modern plugs come coated). If you must use it, a pinhead-sized dab on the threads only — never on the tip or seat.
  8. Thread plug by hand until snug, then torque to 13 ft-lb.
  9. Apply dielectric grease to the inside of the coil boot. Install coil. Torque the 10mm bolt to 60 in-lb (5 ft-lb).
  10. Reconnect coil connectors. Reinstall engine cover. Reconnect battery. Start, listen for misfire.

Why it works

  • 3.6L: 100K interval and 1-hour job
  • Coil-on-plug = no spark plug wires to fail
  • Common NGK / Champion / Denso parts
  • No misfire codes if torqued correctly

Trade-offs

  • 3.8L driver-side requires intake plenum removal
  • 12 plugs total on 3.8L = double the work
  • Aluminum heads punish overtorquing
  • Wrong-gap iridium plugs cause misfires under load

Tools required

  • 10mm socket
  • 5/8" spark plug socket (3.8L) or 14mm thin-wall plug socket (3.6L)
  • 3/8" ratchet and 6" extension
  • torque wrench
  • anti-seize (use sparingly)
  • dielectric grease
