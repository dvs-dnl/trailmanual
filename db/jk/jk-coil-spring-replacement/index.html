Jeep Wrangler JK / Suspension

Coil Spring Replacement

Difficulty 3/53–6 hrs$150–6002007-2018
Short answer

Sagging or broken coil springs cause uneven ride height, poor bump absorption, and bottoming on trail — replace them in pairs (both fronts or both rears) with either Mopar OE or aftermarket lift springs sized to your build.

Stock JK coil springs are progressive-rate units that settle over time, especially under bumpers, winches, and roof racks. After 100,000 miles, a stock spring can sag 0.5"–1" below its original free length. Mismatched ride height (one side lower than the other) usually points to one tired spring or a cracked coil — and it shows up at the wheel arch height before you feel it in the seat.

You have three replacement paths. First: Mopar OE springs (52126318AC heavy-duty front for Rubicon-spec ride height, 52126314AC for Sport/Sahara front, 77070091 rear) — these put you back to factory geometry. Cost is around $75–$95 per spring. Second: aftermarket lift springs paired with longer shocks for 2"–3" of lift. Old Man Emu, Rough Country, Rubicon Express, and Teraflex all offer matched coil sets. Third: heavy-rate replacement coils to compensate for added weight (bumper, winch, half doors removed for permanent door storage) without adding lift.

Springs always replace in pairs. Mixing a new spring with a tired one will give you a measurable lean. Replace fronts together, rears together — or all four at once if you're upgrading the build.

Front install: support the frame on jack stands, disconnect the track bar at the axle, the sway bar end links, and one front shock per side. Drop the axle slowly with a floor jack — the front spring will lift out by hand once tension releases. Rear install: same approach but the rear sway bar disconnect is built into the geometry, and you'll need to disconnect the rear track bar. Watch the brake hoses and ABS lines as the axle drops.

Reassemble with the spring isolators (rubber pads at top and bottom) in place — missing isolators cause coil clunk over bumps. Torque the shocks to MetalCloak's JK figures (front upper 20 ft-lb, front lower 56, rear upper 37, rear lower 56), the track bar bolts to 125 ft-lb, and the sway bar end links to 75 ft-lb. Get an alignment after — caster changes as ride height changes.

Matching spring rate to the weight you actually carry

Springs are rated for a load, and a JK that's grown a steel bumper, winch, and rear tire carrier is no longer the load the factory springs were picked for. The working rules: about 200–280 lbs of bumper-plus-winch on the nose wants heavy-duty front coils (the Rubicon-spec Mopar 52126318AC or an aftermarket HD front pair) to hold ride height; a constant rear load — tire carrier, drawer system, fridge, rooftop tent — wants HD rear coils rather than taller ones. Old Man Emu makes this explicit by selling light, medium, and heavy rates at the same lift height; buy for the built weight of the Jeep, not the showroom weight. The mistake that produces a harsh ride is over-springing an unloaded Jeep — HD coils with no winch or armor ride like a delivery van. If you're solving stance and load together with new lift height, start from the lift kit overview so springs, shocks, and geometry parts get picked as a set.

Diagnose before you buy — sag isn't always springs

Measure before ordering. Take fender-arch-to-hub-center height at all four corners on level ground: more than 1/2" side-to-side difference on one axle points at a tired or cracked spring. Then look at the springs themselves — JK coils crack at the bottom coil where road salt collects in the lower isolator, and a cracked coil sits visibly shorter with a fresh fracture face you can find with a flashlight. But a lean can also come from a collapsed track bar bushing letting the axle shift laterally, which moves the body relative to the tires without any spring being weak — check the track bar bushings while you're under there. Worn shocks don't cause sag but they show up in the same complaint list; if the problem is float and bottoming at ride height rather than a height loss, read shock replacement first and save the spring money.

Install details that separate quiet from clunky

Coil springs are clocked: the pigtail (flat, tapered end) at the bottom indexes into a pocket in the lower isolator, and the spring seats fully only when the tail sits in that pocket. A spring installed 90 degrees off-clock rides on part of the coil, sits a few millimeters high on one side, and clunks over driveway transitions. Seat the isolators, index the tail, and confirm the top of the spring is captured in the upper mount before letting the axle take weight. Expect 1/8"–1/4" of settle in the first 500 miles on new springs — measure ride height after that settle, not on install day, before judging whether the rate choice was right. And on any spring swap that changed height more than an inch, re-check headlight aim; a nose-high JK with a fresh HD front pair blinds oncoming traffic until the buckets are adjusted down.

Steps

  1. Park on level ground. Loosen lug nuts on all four corners if doing all four springs.
  2. Lift the frame and support on jack stands behind the front axle (front job) or in front of the rear axle (rear job).
  3. Remove the wheels.
  4. Disconnect the track bar at the axle side.
  5. Disconnect the sway bar end links from the axle.
  6. Disconnect one shock per side at the lower mount.
  7. Lower the axle slowly with a floor jack until the coil spring releases — typically 6"–8" of droop.
  8. Pull the spring out by hand. Inspect upper and lower isolators; replace if cracked.
  9. Install the new spring with isolators seated correctly top and bottom.
  10. Raise the axle back into position; reconnect shocks, sway bar, track bar.
  11. Torque to spec: shocks front upper 20 ft-lb, front lower 56, rear upper 37, rear lower 56; track bar 125 ft-lb; sway bar links 75 ft-lb.
  12. Lower the vehicle, settle the suspension by driving 5–10 miles, then re-check torque.
  13. Schedule an alignment.

Why it works

  • Restores factory ride height and bump absorption
  • Doubles as a lift if you upgrade spring length
  • Springs are the cheapest suspension component to replace
  • No fluid bleed or special tooling needed

Trade-offs

  • Track bar disconnection requires alignment after
  • Mismatched springs (new + old on same axle) cause visible lean
  • Aftermarket lift springs change caster — pair with adjustable control arms for 3"+ lift
  • Heavier springs ride stiffer when the vehicle is empty

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • spring compressor (rear only if compressed)
  • metric sockets
  • torque wrench
  • pry bar
  • ratchet straps
