Wiring Aux Lights to the Raptor's Upfitter Switches

Difficulty 3/52.0–5.0 hrs$60–4002017-2020, 2021-present

The Raptor comes with factory upfitter switches (the AUX switches in the overhead console or dash) that are pre-wired to relays and fused circuits in the engine bay — they exist specifically so you can add lights and accessories without splicing into critical wiring. Use them. They're the cleanest, safest way to power aux lighting, and they keep the install reversible and dealer-friendly. Respect the amperage limits and fuse everything correctly.

One of the best features the Raptor gives off-road builders is factory upfitter switches: a bank of switches (Ford calls them AUX switches) wired from the cab to relay-controlled, fused power feeds in the engine bay. Ford put them there so owners could add winches, lights, air compressors, and other accessories cleanly, without tapping into the truck's critical circuits. They make accessory wiring dramatically tidier than running your own relay and switch from scratch.

Each upfitter switch controls a relay that switches a power feed at a labeled point in the engine bay (typically a wiring stub or the auxiliary fuse/relay box, depending on year). Each switch has an amperage rating — they are not all the same. Higher-draw devices (a light bar, an air compressor) must go on a switch rated for the current; small pods can share lower-rated circuits. Pull up your model-year's upfitter pinout and amperage chart before you connect anything; the ratings differ across generations and you can overload a low-rated circuit.

For loads beyond what an upfitter switch can directly carry, use the upfitter switch as the *trigger* for a separate, properly sized relay drawing power straight from the battery through its own fuse. The switch turns the relay's coil on; the relay carries the real current. This is the right pattern for a big light bar.

The clean approach for most aux lighting:

1. Mount the lights and route the power lead back toward the engine bay, away from heat and moving parts, secured in loom every several inches.

2. If the light's draw is within the upfitter switch's rating, connect it to that circuit's output and ground to a solid chassis point.

3. If the draw exceeds the switch rating, wire a dedicated relay: battery → fuse → relay → lights, with the relay's trigger coming from the upfitter switch output.

4. Fuse every circuit at the source, sized to the wire and the load — the fuse protects the wire, not the light.

5. Weatherproof every connection with heat-shrink (not bare electrical tape) — this truck lives in dust, water, and vibration.

Splicing into existing circuits or running an always-hot wire with an inline switch is how you get parasitic battery drain, blown factory fuses, and a wiring nightmare the next owner curses. The upfitter switches exist precisely to avoid that. They're switched (no key-off drain when wired correctly), fused, and reversible. There is no good reason to bypass them on a truck that came with them.

The most common mistake is overloading a low-amperage upfitter switch with a high-draw light bar — use the switch as a relay trigger instead. The second is fusing for the light's rating rather than the wire's; the fuse must protect the smallest wire in the circuit. The third is relying on tape over crimps — desert vibration and moisture will find every un-shrunk connection. And remember many areas restrict forward-facing aux lights on public roads; wire them to be off on the street.

Aux LED pods or a bar: $100–$350 depending on brand and output. A pre-made weatherproof relay/fuse harness: $30–$60. Connectors, loom, and heat-shrink: $20–$40. A full DIY install is an afternoon to a long Saturday depending on routing. Paying a shop adds $150–$400 in labor.

Why it works

  • Upfitter switches make the install clean, fused, switched, and reversible
  • Relay-triggering keeps high-draw lights off the switch's low-current circuit
  • No tapping into factory wiring means no parasitic drain and clean resale

Trade-offs

  • Each switch has a real amperage limit that's quick to exceed
  • High-output lights still require a separate relay circuit
  • Sloppy weatherproofing fails fast in desert dust and water

Tools required

  • wire strippers/crimpers
  • multimeter
  • heat gun
  • socket set
  • fish tape
  • test light
