Ford Bronco / Suspension

4" Suspension Lift — Trail Build Baseline

Difficulty 3/58–20 hrs$800–22001966-1977
Short answer

4" is the standard trail-build lift for an early Bronco. It clears 35" tires with the right backspacing, gives a real articulation gain over a 2" kit, and stays in a height range where quality twin-tube or monotube shocks still work without remote reservoirs. Budget $800 for a DIY spring-and-shock install and closer to $2,200 once caster correction, extended brake lines, steering geometry, and an alignment are included. Those supporting items are what separate a safe 4" lift from a twitchy one, and skipping them is the reason so many lifted early Broncos feel nervous above 50 mph.

A 4" suspension lift is the baseline for a capable early Bronco trail build, sitting between the mild 2" lift and a full spring-over-axle conversion. It buys 35" tire clearance, better approach and departure angles, real articulation, and driveline angles that remain manageable with stock-length shafts. Because the early Bronco is leaf-sprung at both ends with no track bars or control arms, a lift here is a spring swap rather than a geometry rebuild — which is what keeps the job inside a home garage.

  • 35" tire clearance with 3.75"-4.5" backspacing wheels, usually with modest fender trimming at the front corners
  • Approach and departure angle gains that show up immediately on ledges
  • Genuine articulation improvement over a 2" kit, because 4" packs use longer, softer multi-leaf designs rather than added arch alone
  • Room for a 2-2.5" body-to-tire buffer at full compression once bumpstops are extended

Confirm wheel fitment against the backspacing guide before ordering. The wrong offset either rubs the radius of the fender at full stuff or pushes the tire out far enough to load the closed-knuckle Dana 44's wheel bearings harder than the design intends.

Not all 4" kits are the same spring. The difference between a pleasant truck and a buckboard is leaf count and pack design, not the advertised height.

  • Wild Horses 4x4 and James Duff build early-Bronco-specific packs with more leaves of thinner stock. They ride softer and flex further, and run roughly $1,000-1,300 for a complete four-corner kit.
  • Skyjacker and Superlift kits are the value tier at $900-1,100 complete. They lift correctly and are widely supported, but a stiffer pack rides firmer on pavement.
  • Tuff Country sits between the two on both price and ride.
  • Bargain no-name kits save $300 and cost it back in a harsh ride and springs that sag within two seasons.

Whatever you pick, buy the kit with greasable shackles and a full polyurethane or rubber bushing set included. Reusing 50-year-old shackle bushings on new springs wastes the new springs.

  • Shocks. 4" of lift demands extended-travel shocks. Bilstein 5100s or Fox 2.0 IFP units are the common choices at $80-140 per corner. Stock-length shocks will top out and let the axle pound the bumpstops, which destroys both the shock and the spring's main leaf. Match length to the measured extended and collapsed dimensions at your new ride height — see the shock selection guide.
  • Brake lines. Stock lines go taut at full droop with 4" of lift. Stainless braided extended lines run about $110 for the truck and are a safety requirement rather than an upgrade. A line that stretches at articulation tears at the crimp, and you find out at the worst moment.
  • Bumpstop extensions. Adding 4" of static height without extending bumpstops means the tire finds the fender before the suspension finds its stop. Extend with a stack of urethane pucks or a weld-on tower.
  • Extended sway bar links if your truck carries the factory front bar. Stock links are too short at 4" and will bind or tear their mounts.

This is the section owners skip and then spend a year regretting.

Caster. Arching a leaf spring rotates the axle, and 4" of lift pulls roughly 2 degrees of caster out of the front end. Low caster reads as vague on-center feel, slow return-to-center, and a truck that wanders in a crosswind. Correct it with tapered degree shims between the spring and the perch — $30 for a pair — or by cutting and rotating the perches on a rebuilt front end. Target 4-6 degrees positive at ride height and verify with an angle gauge on the knuckle face, not by feel.

Steering geometry. The early Bronco's box-and-bellcrank steering puts the drag link at an angle that changes with lift. At 4" the drag link and the front spring travel through different arcs, and the resulting bump steer shows as the truck darting when one front tire hits a bump. A drop pitman arm ($120-180) is the cheap correction; a crossover or high-steer conversion is the thorough one. Pair either with the steering box brace and stabilizer, because a lifted early Bronco puts real load into a frame-mounted box that was never braced from the factory.

Shackle geometry. If the truck is road-driven and still feels nervous after caster correction, a front shackle reversal is the next step. It changes the axle's fore-aft arc and generally improves both ride and steering stability.

At 4", front and rear driveshaft angles increase but stay inside U-joint operating range with stock suspension travel, so a slip yoke eliminator is not mandated by the lift alone. Reconsider once you add a locker, chase aggressive articulation, or go taller — at that point read the driveshaft upgrade guide and the SYE and CV driveshaft guide. Inspect front U-joints annually regardless; the steeper operating angle wears them faster than stock.

Gearing is the other half of the 35" decision. Stock 3.50 or 4.11 gears with 35s make the truck dull off idle and hard on the clutch or the C4. Plan a re-gear to 4.56 or 4.88 into the same budget rather than treating it as a later surprise, and recalibrate the speedometer to match.

  1. Break the U-bolt and shackle hardware loose with penetrating oil applied a day ahead. Fifty-year-old shackle bolts seize into their bushings, and a cutoff wheel is often faster than a fight.
  2. Support the frame on stands and the axle on a floor jack. Work one axle at a time so the other end holds the truck located.
  3. Fit the new center pins and set the spring on the perch before torquing U-bolts. New U-bolts come with the kit — reusing stretched ones is a false economy.
  4. Torque U-bolts in a cross pattern to the kit spec (typically 85-110 ft-lb), then re-torque after the first 100 miles and again at 500. New spring packs settle.
  5. Fit extended brake lines and bleed the system before the truck touches the ground.
  6. Set caster with shims, then get a front-end alignment with toe set to spec.
  • Spring wrap under power. Taller lifts increase axle wrap on leaf springs, which shows up as a shudder on hard acceleration. Traction bars or a stiffer front spring pack resolve it.
  • Sagging within a season. A pack that loses an inch in six months was underbuilt or overloaded. Winch bumpers and full-size spares change the front spring rate you need.
  • Death-wobble-adjacent symptoms. A lifted early Bronco that shakes at 45-55 mph nearly always has a worn bellcrank, loose steering box, or uncorrected caster rather than a bad tire balance. Chase the geometry first.
  • Fender contact at stuff. Expect to trim the front fender lips for 35s — the fender trimming guide covers how far to go without hurting the body line.

The $800 low end assumes springs and shocks only on a truck whose steering and brake lines are already sorted. Plan on $2,000 for a complete, safe 4" build, and treat the re-gear as a separate line item that follows within the same season.

Why it works

  • clears 35" tires with appropriate wheels and modest trimming
  • real articulation and departure-angle improvement over a 2" kit
  • complete bolt-on kits available from multiple established vendors
  • no fabrication required beyond brake lines and bumpstop extensions

Trade-offs

  • extended brake lines and caster correction are mandatory and safety-critical
  • bump steer needs a drop pitman arm or crossover steering to fully resolve
  • front driveshaft angles increase — inspect U-joints annually
  • costs three to four times a 2" kit once supporting parts are counted
  • 35s without a re-gear make the truck noticeably slower and harder on the drivetrain

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands (4)
  • torque wrench
  • breaker bar
  • 1/2" impact or long-handle wrenches
  • angle grinder
  • penetrating oil
  • caster/angle gauge
  • spring spreader or pry bar
  • line wrenches for brake fittings
