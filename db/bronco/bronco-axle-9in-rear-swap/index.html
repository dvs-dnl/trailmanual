Ford Bronco / Axles

Ford 9-Inch Rear Axle — The Default Upgrade

Difficulty 4/58–20 hrs$900–35001966-1977
Short answer

The Ford 9-inch is the rear axle most serious early Bronco builds end up running. It is stronger than the factory rear, has a removable third member that makes gear and locker changes far less painful, and the aftermarket support is endless. If you are regearing and adding a locker anyway, swapping to a 9-inch first is often the better spend.

The early Bronco shipped with a Ford 9-inch rear from the factory in many configurations, but axle width, gear ratio, and spline count vary, and decades of swaps mean you cannot assume what is under any given truck. A purpose-built 9-inch — whether a salvage Explorer housing narrowed and re-perched, or a new aftermarket housing from Currie or Dynatrac — gives you a known starting point.

Why the 9-inch wins

The defining feature is the dropout third member. The entire ring-and-pinion, carrier, and locker live in a removable center section that bolts to the front of the housing. You can set up a spare third member on the bench, then swap gear ratios or add a locker in an afternoon without disturbing the axle shafts or brakes. No other common axle makes drivetrain changes this approachable.

Strength scales with build. A 28-spline 9-inch is adequate for stock-ish tires; 31-spline handles 35s and moderate wheeling; 35-spline alloy shafts in a nodular case carry 37s and abuse. Match the spline count to your tire size and how hard you wheel.

What the swap involves

This is a fabrication-adjacent job, not a bolt-in. The work breaks down as:

  1. Width and perch location. The new housing must match your wheel mounting width and your leaf spring spacing. A salvage housing usually needs to be narrowed and always needs new spring perches welded at the correct location and pinion angle.
  1. Pinion angle. With the perches tacked, measure the driveline angle before final welding. Get this wrong and you will fight vibration or a worn U-joint for the life of the build.
  1. Brakes. Plan a disc conversion at the same time. Doing brakes while the axle is out is far less work than revisiting it later, and a heavier, more capable rear deserves better stopping.
  1. Gears and traction. Set the third member up with your target ratio and a locker before it goes in. See the gear ratio and locker guides for ratio selection by tire size.

Explorer 8.8 vs. true 9-inch — telling them apart

Salvage yards sell both, and they get confused constantly. The quickest tell is the differential cover: the 9-inch uses a round, 10-bolt cover with a removable third member visible as a separate casting bolted to the front face of the housing. The 8.8 uses an oval, 10-bolt cover and has no separate third member -- the ring and pinion mount directly in the housing, so there's nothing to unbolt and drop out for a bench gear change. If you can't tell from the cover shape, check the nose of the housing for a third-member bolt pattern; the 9-inch has one, the 8.8 doesn't. An 8.8 out of a salvage Explorer or Ranger is a legitimate cheaper option for a milder build, but you're trading away the swap-in-an-afternoon serviceability that's the whole reason most builders reach for a 9-inch in the first place.

Once the third member is set up, torque it to the housing at 32-35 ft-lb in a star pattern -- running the bolts down in a circle puts an uneven clamp load on the gasket surface, distorts it, and starts a leak. Torque axle shaft retainers (C-clip style or bearing-retainer style, depending on what your shafts use) to factory spec before the housing goes back in the truck. Both steps are tempting to shortcut when you're eager to get the axle installed, and both are miserable to redo once the housing is under the truck and full of gear oil.

What to watch for

Pinion angle and perch placement are where these builds go wrong. Measure twice, tack, verify on the ground at ride height, then weld. A driveline angle gauge is worth the small cost.

If you are buying a salvage Explorer 8.8 instead of a true 9-inch, understand the tradeoff: the 8.8 is cheaper and adequate for many builds, but it lacks the dropout serviceability that makes the 9-inch worth the premium. For a build that will see multiple gear or locker changes over its life, the 9-inch pays you back.

Costs and sources

  • Salvage 9-inch housing, re-perched: $400–$700
  • New aftermarket housing (Currie/Dynatrac): $1,200–$2,000
  • 35-spline alloy shafts: $450
  • Disc brake conversion: $350
  • Third member with locker and gears, set up: $1,000–$1,600
  • Gear set-up labor if you're not doing it yourself: $300–$500 per axle

Budget $900 for a salvage-based build you assemble yourself, $3,500 for a new-housing build with premium internals and shop setup. This is a long-horizon upgrade — done once, it carries the truck through every future tire and gearing change.

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • torque wrench
  • welder (for spring perches)
  • angle grinder
  • tape measure
  • driveline angle gauge
