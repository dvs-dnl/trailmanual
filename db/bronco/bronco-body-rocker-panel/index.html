Ford Bronco / Body & Rust

Rocker Panel Rust Repair and Replacement

Difficulty 4/512–40 hrs$400–25001966-1977
Short answer

Rocker rust is structural on the early Bronco, not cosmetic. The rocker sill ties the body together between the door hinge pillar and the kick panel — when it rots, the door sags and the gap goes uneven. Fix the rockers before you rehang doors or chase a body gap, because the rocker sets the geometry everything else references. A pair of outer skins runs $280; a full inner-and-outer section replacement with door realignment lands between $400 DIY and $2,500 at a shop.

Rockers on an early Bronco rust from the inside out. Trapped moisture in the closed section corrodes the inner sill while the outer skin still looks presentable from three feet away. Before you buy panels, probe the rocker with an awl along its entire length: soft, flaky, or hollow means the inner structure is gone and you are into a section replacement rather than a skim patch.

The early Bronco rocker is not a single stamping. It is an outer skin, an inner sill, and a closed cavity between them, with the assembly spot-welded to the floor pan flange at the bottom and running into the hinge pillar at the front and the rear quarter at the back. Three consequences follow:

  1. It carries load. The rocker resists the body's tendency to twist, and the front door's lower hinge geometry references it. A rotted rocker is why an otherwise straight truck has a door that drops when you open it.
  2. You cannot see the part that fails. The inner sill is the structural member and it rusts first, behind an outer skin that looks fine.
  3. Water gets in and stays. Drain holes at the bottom of the rocker plug with mud and undercoating. Once the cavity holds water, corrosion runs year-round.

How far the rust has gone decides whether this is an afternoon or two weekends:

FindingRepairTime
Surface rust, outer skin onlySand, treat, prime, paint — no welding3-5 hrs
Perforation in outer skin, solid innerOuter patch or full skin section6-10 hrs
Inner sill compromisedFull section replacement, structural12-20 hrs
Inner and outer destroyed, floor flange involvedSection replacement plus inner fabrication, floor tie-in25-40 hrs

Misjudging this is the classic early-Bronco trap — the outer skin photographs fine while the inner sill behind it is lace. Cut a 2" inspection window with a hole saw if you are unsure. It costs a patch and 20 minutes and saves you from ordering the wrong panels.

  1. Remove the door on that side. You need access to the rocker end at the hinge pillar, and you will want the door available to check gap during fitment. Mark the hinge positions before unbolting so you have a baseline to return to.
  2. Brace the opening. On a full inner-and-outer section replacement, tack a length of square tube diagonally across the door opening before cutting. Removing the rocker without bracing lets the opening relax and the door will never fit the same way again.
  3. Cut out the damaged section, going 1-2" into clean steel past the rot at each end. Drill the factory spot welds along the floor flange with a spot weld cutter rather than grinding through it — the flange is your fitment reference.
  4. Treat all exposed metal, especially inside the closed section. Wire wheel to bright steel where the new panel will land, encapsulate the surrounding surface rust, and let it cure fully.
  5. Fit and clamp the new section, then check door gap before any welding. Hang the door temporarily and verify the gap top to bottom. A panel tacked in 3mm high shows up as a sagging door and an uneven gap that no amount of hinge shimming will hide.
  6. Weld-through primer on every lap joint, then stitch-weld — short welds spaced out, skipping around the panel to control heat. Continuous seam welding warps rocker skins badly.
  7. Dress the welds with a flap disc, working light so you do not thin the panel, then seam-seal the joints.
  8. Drill a wax injection hole, spray cavity wax through the entire closed section with a wand, and plug the hole with a rubber grommet. Verify the factory drain holes at the bottom of the rocker are open before you call it done.

The repair only lasts if you stop the next round of inside-out rust. Inject 3M cavity wax (or the SEM or Eastwood equivalent) into every closed body section once the metalwork is finished — rockers, door bottoms, hinge pillars, and the rear quarter cavities. This is the single highest-value rust-prevention step on the truck, and it is the reason a properly repaired rocker outlives the factory one by decades.

Equally important: leave the drain holes open. Undercoating sprayed over the bottom of a rocker seals water in rather than out, which is how a well-intentioned 1980s rustproofing job accelerated the rot on half the surviving early Broncos.

The same trapped-moisture pattern drives floor pan rot and fender and body-mount corrosion, so treat all three areas in the same session while the interior is apart. If you are already in there for the floor, doing rockers at the same time saves a second teardown and a second round of paint blending.

If rock rails or sliders are in the truck's future, sort the rocker metal first and then mount the armor to the frame rather than to the body. A slider bolted or welded to a body rocker transfers rock impacts directly into sheet metal that was never designed to take them, and it traps mud against the freshly repaired panel. Frame-mounted sliders protect the rocker; body-mounted sliders eventually destroy it.

  • Fire. On most early Broncos the main fuel tank sits under the driver's seat, close enough to the rocker work that a drained-and-removed tank is the right call before striking an arc. Keep an extinguisher within reach.
  • Heat warp. Stitch, skip, and let the panel cool between passes. Patience is worth more than technique here.
  • Door realignment. Plan on re-shimming hinges and adjusting the striker after the rocker is in. Check the gap with the truck on level ground and the body mounts torqued, not on jack stands.
  • Hidden hinge pillar rot. The rocker and the hinge pillar rot together more often than not. Probe the pillar before you order, or you will be ordering twice.

DIY with a MIG welder puts both rockers in for $400-700 in parts. Shop work on both sides, including door realignment and blend paint, runs $1,800-2,500. As with floor pans, the spread between those numbers is most of what separates a $12,000 early Bronco from a $30,000 one.

Why it works

  • reproduction sections are widely available for both sides, inner and outer
  • fixes door sag and uneven gaps when done with the door fitted for reference
  • cavity wax treatment prevents recurrence for decades
  • open access while the interior is out makes it the cheapest time to do it

Trade-offs

  • inner rust is almost always worse than the outer skin suggests
  • door removal, bracing, and realignment are required to do it correctly
  • welding near the under-seat fuel tank demands real preparation
  • budget 30-50% more time than your first estimate

Tools required

  • MIG welder (0.023-0.030 wire)
  • angle grinder with cutoff and flap discs
  • body saw
  • spot weld cutter
  • hammer and dolly set
  • wire wheel
  • weld-through primer
  • cavity wax applicator wand
  • clamps and Cleco fasteners
  • awl or pick
