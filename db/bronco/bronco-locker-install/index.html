Ford Bronco / Axles

Installing a Locker — Ford 9-Inch Rear and Dana Front

Difficulty 4/53–9 hrs$300–13001966-1977
Short answer

Lock the rear first — it returns more climbing capability per dollar than anything else you can bolt to an early Bronco. Add a selectable front afterward for steep, off-camber ledges where an open front stalls the climb. Do the locker at the same time as a regear so you pay the precision setup labor once, and check your axle shaft spline count before you order anything, because a locker behind tired 28-spline shafts finds their limit fast. The companion locker options guide compares the types; this card is the build decision and the install.

There is a persistent piece of misinformation in the early Bronco world worth clearing up before you spend money, because it changes what parts you order:

Every early Bronco, 1966 through 1977, came from the factory with a Ford 9-inch rear axle. There is no factory Dana rear in these trucks. The Dana in an early Bronco is the front axle: a closed-knuckle Dana 30 from 1966 to 1971, and a closed-knuckle Dana 44 from mid-1971 through 1977. Both front axles use kingpins rather than ball joints, which is why front-end service on these trucks looks nothing like a modern solid-axle truck — see the closed-knuckle service guide.

Three practical consequences follow from that:

  1. You order a rear locker by 9-inch spline count, not by "Dana 44." Most early Broncos run 28-spline small-bearing shafts; upgraded trucks run 31 or 35-spline. ARB, Eaton, and Yukon all catalog the Ford 9-inch by spline count, and the wrong count is a returned part. Pull an axle shaft and count, or measure — do not assume.
  2. The rear housing is not your limiting factor. The 9-inch is strong enough that people race behind it. Your limit is shaft strength and, secondarily, the carrier.
  3. The front axle is usually what breaks on 35s. A Dana 30 with a locker under a 35-inch tire is the classic early Bronco failure. If that is your truck, the Dana 44 front is the upgrade to plan around.

This is the single most useful thing to understand about locking an early Bronco, and it is specific to the Ford 9-inch.

The 9-inch uses a removable carrier — the ring gear, pinion, carrier bearings, and differential all live in a cast third member that unbolts from the front of the housing as a unit. The Dana front axle does not work this way; it is an integral-carrier design where all the setup happens inside the housing, on your back, under the truck.

That difference gives you an option nobody talks about often enough: buy or build a second third member. Instead of tearing down the axle in your truck, you acquire a spare 9-inch center section, have it set up on a bench with the locker and the gears you want, and then swap it in. The physical swap is a few hours — drain, unbolt the driveshaft, pull the axle shafts, unbolt ten nuts, slide the old one out, new gasket, slide the new one in.

What that buys you:

  • The truck is off the road for an afternoon, not two weekends.
  • The setup work happens on a bench, where a shop can do it properly and where you can check backlash and pattern without lying in gravel.
  • You keep the original third member intact. If the build changes direction, or you sell the truck, or the new setup develops a whine, you have a known-good spare on the shelf. On a 50-year-old vehicle, that is real insurance.
  • A prebuilt 9-inch third member with a locker and gears runs roughly $1,600 to $2,400 depending on the locker and gear brand. Compare that against $700 for the locker, $320 for gears, $130 for the master kit, and $500 to $800 in setup labor — the prebuilt unit is often within a couple hundred dollars of doing it the hard way, and it is dramatically less downtime.

Do not extend that logic to the front. The Dana 30 or 44 front has to be set up in the housing, in the truck or on a stand.

Rear — automatic (Eaton Detroit Locker, Yukon Grizzly). Mechanical, no air line, no switch, and it locks whenever it is loaded. On a light, short-wheelbase truck it is very effective and quite noticeable: it ratchets and clicks in tight pavement turns and can push the rear around in the wet. On a Bronco that mostly wheels, this is a defensible and cheaper choice at roughly $450 to $700.

Rear — selectable air locker (ARB). Fully open until you flip the switch, so the truck drives and steers normally on the road and locks only when you want it. Costs more once you add the compressor: about $1,000 to $1,400 all-in for the locker, compressor, and plumbing. The right answer if the Bronco sees meaningful street miles.

Rear — limited slip (Auburn, Truetrac). The mild option for a street-biased truck. Adds traction without changing manners. Not enough for hard rock, but a real improvement over open at $400 to $600.

Front — selectable, and effectively only selectable. An automatic locker in the front of an early Bronco fights the steering, and on the many trucks still running a manual box or a modestly assisted conversion it makes low-speed maneuvering genuinely difficult. Run an ARB up front, and leave it open until you need it.

Wild Horses and the other early Bronco specialists stock ARB, Yukon, Eaton, and Auburn units for both the 9-inch rear and the Dana fronts, so availability is not the constraint — the constraint is getting the spline count and the carrier series right.

This is precision work. The steps below are the shape of the job, not a substitute for the gear manufacturer's setup specifications.

  1. Count splines and confirm the carrier series. Pull one rear axle shaft and count the splines at the differential end. Order to that number.
  2. Drain the axle and catch the old gear oil. Look at what comes out — glitter means the ring and pinion is already shedding, and that decides whether you are also regearing.
  3. Rear: pull the third member. Remove the driveshaft, pull both axle shafts, unbolt the ten nuts on the housing face, and slide the carrier out. Have a helper; it is heavier than it looks.
  4. Rear: set up on the bench. Press the carrier bearings onto the new locker, install with the correct shim stack, and set backlash to the gear manufacturer's spec — typically 0.008 to 0.012 inch — measured with a dial indicator on the ring gear face. Set carrier bearing preload per spec.
  5. Check the tooth contact pattern with marking compound before anything is torqued for the last time. Pattern is the real verdict; backlash is only one input to it. Adjust pinion depth with shims until the pattern sits correctly on both drive and coast sides.
  6. Reinstall with a new gasket, torque the housing nuts evenly, and refill with the correct gear oil. A limited slip needs friction modifier; a locker generally does not — read the instructions rather than defaulting.
  7. Front: work in the housing. Remove the carrier with a case spreader, install the locker, and repeat the backlash, preload, and pattern process. The closed-knuckle front means the axle shafts and knuckle work come apart differently than a modern axle — plan on new knuckle seals and inspect the kingpin bearings while you are in there.
  8. Air locker plumbing, if applicable. The air line exits a bulkhead fitting at the differential and runs to the compressor. Route it away from the exhaust, away from the steering linkage, and away from anything that moves at articulation, and secure it every 12 inches. On a truck this old, mount the compressor somewhere dry and give it a properly fused feed with a good ground — see the charging and wiring guides if the harness is original.
  9. Break it in. Drive gently for the first 30 to 50 miles, let the axle cool, and avoid towing. Change the gear oil after the first 500 miles to clear the break-in metal.

A locker does not make weak shafts stronger — it makes them break. An open differential lets a spinning tire relieve load. A locker refuses to, and sends that torque into whichever shaft has grip. If you are running 28-spline small-bearing shafts, a locker plus 35s plus a V8 is the combination that snaps them. Budget 31-spline chromoly shafts with a large-bearing conversion, roughly $550 to $800 a pair, at the same time. On a semi-float 9-inch, a shaft that lets go outboard of the bearing can allow the wheel to leave the truck, so treat this as a safety item rather than a durability nicety.

Regear at the same time or accept paying twice. The carrier is out either way. Setup labor is the expensive part of both jobs, and combining them costs an extra $320 in gears and $130 in a master kit rather than another $500 to $800 in labor. See the regear guide.

Match ratios front to rear exactly. The Dana 20 transfer case splits drive evenly. A ratio mismatch binds the driveline and destroys parts.

Know when to hand it off. Ring and pinion setup is the most tolerance-critical work on the truck, and a pattern that looks close but is wrong produces a howl at 45 mph and a failed gear set at 8,000 miles. If you have not set up a third member before, the bench-swap route on the rear lets you buy the expertise without buying the tools.

  • Rear automatic locker (Detroit, Grizzly), part only: $450–700
  • Rear ARB, plus compressor and plumbing: $1,000–1,400
  • Front ARB for Dana 30 or 44, part only: $750–900
  • Master carrier setup kit, per axle: $110–160
  • Professional setup labor, per axle: $400–800
  • Prebuilt 9-inch third member with locker and gears: $1,600–2,400
  • 31-spline chromoly rear shafts with large-bearing conversion: $550–800

A realistic rear-only build — automatic locker, gears, master kit, and shop setup — lands around $1,400 to $1,900. Adding a selectable front later brings the pair to roughly $2,800 to $3,600. Sequence it as rear locker and gears first, shafts with or immediately after, selectable front last. That order puts the capability on the truck in the sequence you will actually notice it.

Tools required

  • dial indicator with magnetic base
  • inch-pound torque wrench
  • foot-pound torque wrench
  • bearing puller/press
  • case spreader (front Dana only)
  • snap ring pliers
  • gear marking compound
  • brass drift
