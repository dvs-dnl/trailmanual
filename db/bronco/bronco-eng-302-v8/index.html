Factory 302 V8 — Specs, Carb, and Tune

Difficulty 2/50 hrs$01969-1977

The 302 is the sweet spot for a stock-appearing, reliable early Bronco. It is torquey enough for trail driving on 31–33" tires, parts are everywhere, and the single best dollar-for-dollar improvement is dropping a Pertronix module in place of the points. Leave the displacement alone and spend your money on ignition, cooling, and a clean carburetor.

Ford's 302 Windsor (5.0L) arrived in the Bronco in 1969 and was the dominant engine through 1977. Early trucks used the 2-barrel Autolite 2100; later ones got the 2150. Gross output was rated around 170 hp under the pre-1972 SAE gross standard, which works out to roughly 130–140 hp by modern net measurement. It is well matched to stock gearing with 31–33" tires and starts to feel strained above 35", especially with a tall rear gear.

The factory points-and-condenser ignition is the 302's biggest maintenance liability and its easiest win. A Pertronix Ignitor I (P/N 1281, about $90) bolts in under the distributor cap, eliminates the points entirely, and holds timing steady as the engine heats and cools. It is the highest-value $90 on the whole engine. While you are in there, set initial timing to 10–12° BTDC with the vacuum advance disconnected, then reconnect it; worn advance springs and a leaky vacuum can both soften throttle response. The full procedure — timing, dwell, vacuum advance, carburetor adjustment — lives in the ignition and carburetor tune-up guide.

The Autolite 2100 flows enough for a stock 302 and is worth keeping if it tunes cleanly; a $30 rebuild kit fixes most leaks and hesitation. If you want more, an Edelbrock Performer intake and a 600 cfm 4-barrel adds top-end and crisper response, though a stock-displacement trail 302 rarely needs it. Before you blame the carburetor for a stumble, confirm it is not actually an ignition or vacuum problem — a vacuum gauge tells you which, and the vacuum gauge diagnosis guide shows how to read it.

The 302 in an early Bronco runs hot in slow going — the engine bay is tight and the factory cooling system is marginal. Keep the 7-blade steel clutch fan and shroud intact; lightweight aluminum fans do not move enough air at crawl speed. If the truck creeps up the gauge on hot-day trails, treat it as a system problem and address the radiator and fan together per the cooling system upgrade guide rather than chasing it with a thermostat alone.

The 302 uses a rope rear main seal that weeps with age. When you have a reason to be in there — clutch, flexplate, oil pan — replace it with a Fel-Pro rubber conversion seal (BS40596). Front oil pan gasket leaks at the timing-cover corners are also common; a bead of RTV at the four corner junctions during a pan gasket job stops most of them. None of this is failure; it is the character of a 50-year-old small block.

If you need more low-end grunt for a heavy big-tire build, the factory 351 Windsor shares the 302's mounts and accessories and brings noticeably more torque. If your priority is modern cold-start, altitude tolerance, and fuel economy rather than more cubes, a fuel-injected 5.0L HO is the route — wiring complexity in exchange for turn-key reliability, covered in the 302 HO / EFI swap guide.

Why it works

  • excellent parts availability and a deep aftermarket
  • torquey and trail-capable on 31–33" tires
  • drops into any early Bronco with off-the-shelf mounts
  • responds well to a basic ignition and tune

Trade-offs

  • factory points ignition is maintenance-intensive until converted
  • gross-rated horsepower flatters the real output
  • rope rear main seal weeps with age
  • marginal cooling in the early Bronco bay needs attention for hot-weather wheeling

Tools required

  • timing light
  • vacuum gauge
  • carburetor tuning tools
  • feeler gauge
