Stock Restoration Build Recipe

Difficulty 4/5200–600 hrs$25000–800001966-1977

A stock restoration is a 12–36 month project that almost always costs more than the number you first wrote down. Budget $35K–$50K for a solid driver-quality result, and another $20K–$40K on top of that for show quality. You do a stock restore to preserve the truck's value and originality — the math rarely breaks even, and that is not the reason to do it.

The goal of this recipe is a period-correct early Bronco: factory appearance, factory-correct mechanicals, and the originality that the collector market rewards. It is the opposite of the trail build recipe, which spends the same money on capability instead of correctness. Decide which truck you are building before you cut anything apart, because the two paths diverge immediately and reversing course is expensive.

Tear down to a bare tub and bare frame. Catalog and bag every fastener and bracket, and photograph each assembly before it comes apart — you will not remember how the dash harness routed eight months from now. Note what is original versus previously replaced; numbers-matching components are worth preserving even when they need rebuilding. This phase is mostly labor and consumables, but it sets up everything after it.

This is the single most variable line in the budget, and it is driven entirely by where the truck lived. A dry Southwest truck might need floor pans and a rocker section, $3,000–$6,000 in metalwork. A salt-belt truck with structural rot — full floors, rockers, toe board, and inner structure — can run $15,000–$25,000 in professional bodywork. The floor pans are the usual starting point; the floor pan rust repair guide shows what that job actually involves before you decide whether to do it yourself or farm it out.

Media-blast the frame, inspect carefully for cracks at the body-mount holes and crossmembers, weld any you find, then encapsulate and paint. A clean frame is the foundation the rest of the restoration sits on, and finding a crack after the body is back on is the kind of mistake that undoes months of work.

Rebuild or replace the engine and transmission, service the transfer case, and rebuild both axles — bearings, seals, and correct preload front and rear. Replace every U-joint. The closed-knuckle Dana 44 front in particular wants a full kingpin-and-seal service while everything is apart; the closed-knuckle service guide covers it. If you are keeping the original ratios, verify them against tire size using the gear ratio guide so a "restored" truck does not drive poorly on modern rubber.

Epoxy primer, block sanding to flat, color, and clear. Factory colors include Poppy Red, Lime Gold, Candyapple Red, Midnight Blue, and Wimbledon White; pull the correct code from the door-jamb data tag for an accurate match. Paint quality is where a driver-grade restoration and a show truck visibly part ways, and where labor cost climbs fastest.

Seat upholstery, carpet, headliner, dash pad, and door panels. Early Bronco soft trim is well reproduced — Bronco Graveyard, Wild Horses, and Toms Bronco Parts stock most of it — so this phase is more about patience and fit than sourcing.

A high-quality stock restoration on a desirable 1969–1972 truck can bring $65,000–$120,000-plus at auction; a driver-quality restored example lands around $35,000–$60,000; a correct-appearing, unrestored truck with good bones sits at $25,000–$40,000. Restoration cost rarely equals market value. Go in understanding that, and the budget overruns sting less.

Why it works

  • preserves maximum market value and originality
  • factory-correct early Broncos are appreciating
  • the most satisfying long-term ownership for a purist

Trade-offs

  • almost always costs more than the original budget
  • a 2–3 year project minimum for quality results
  • requires professional-grade bodywork and paint to come out right
  • spends the same money on correctness that a trail build spends on capability

Tools required

  • full shop setup
  • MIG welder
  • air compressor
  • media blaster
  • body tools
  • engine stand
