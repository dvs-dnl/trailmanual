Ignition and Carburetor Tune-Up

Difficulty 2/52–5 hrs$80–2201966-1977

The single highest-value maintenance task on a carbureted early Bronco is replacing the points with a Pertronix Ignitor — $90, about 45 minutes, and you've eliminated the most failure-prone component in the entire ignition system. A complete tune-up adds plugs, cap, rotor, wires, timing, and a carb check.

A tune-up on a carbureted Bronco is two systems working together: the ignition has to deliver a hot, correctly timed spark, and the carburetor has to deliver the right mixture. Fix one and ignore the other and the truck still runs poorly. Work through them in order — ignition first, because a weak or mistimed spark masks every carb symptom.

Set initial timing with a timing light, engine at operating temperature, and the vacuum advance line disconnected and plugged:

  • 302 V8: 10–12° BTDC initial. Total advance (mechanical + vacuum) should reach 34–38° at 3,500 RPM.
  • 351W V8: 12° BTDC initial. Same total-advance target.
  • 200/170 I6: 6° BTDC initial.

If the engine pings under load, back initial timing down a degree or two; if it's sluggish off idle, the mechanical advance may be sticking. The engine-specific cards cover combustion symptoms in more depth — see the 302 V8 reference.

  1. Plugs: Gap to 0.035" for points ignition, 0.044" for Pertronix or MSD. Use Motorcraft SP432 (302/351W) or an equivalent copper-core plug. Install with anti-seize on the threads.
  2. Points (if still fitted): Set gap to 0.017" with a feeler gauge, or dwell to 26–30° with a dwell meter. If you're in here doing this, seriously consider the Pertronix swap instead — it deletes the part that drifts out of adjustment.
  3. Cap and rotor: Replace every 30,000 miles, or sooner if carbon tracking is visible on the cap interior.
  4. Plug wires: Inspect for cracks, especially at the boots. Resistance-test each wire — it should read under 10,000 ohms per foot. Gates or MSD wires are reliable aftermarket options.
  5. Timing: Set per the specs above with the vacuum advance disconnected and plugged.

The factory Autolite 2100 is a durable 2-barrel, and most running problems are float level or idle mixture, not a worn-out carb:

  • Float level: 7/16" from the gasket surface to the top of the float on the 2100. A high float floods; a low float leans out and stumbles.
  • Check the fuel bowl for leaks at the float valve seat — a weeping seat causes hard hot-starts and a fuel smell.
  • Idle mixture screws: start at 1.5 turns out from lightly seated, then adjust each for the highest steady idle vacuum on a gauge.

If you're tired of seasonal and altitude re-jetting, an EFI conversion is the longer-term answer — see Carb-to-EFI Conversion. For a stock truck that mostly stays near home elevation, a clean 2100 and a correct tune is hard to beat for the money.

A working vacuum advance retards timing at closed throttle and advances it under cruise load. Disconnect the vacuum line at idle — if timing jumps 6–12°, the advance canister is working. If the timing mark doesn't move, the canister diaphragm has failed; replace it ($12). Reading manifold vacuum with a gauge while you tune tells you a lot about engine health beyond the advance alone, which is why a vacuum gauge belongs in the tune-up kit.

The two recurring mistakes: setting timing with the vacuum advance still connected (you'll end up with initial timing far off), and chasing a carb problem that's actually a weak spark. Confirm a strong, correctly timed spark before you touch the carburetor. And after a points-to-Pertronix swap, re-check timing — the trigger point shifts slightly when the points come out.

  • Pertronix Ignitor I: $90
  • Plugs, cap, rotor, wires: roughly $95 together
  • Autolite 2100 rebuild kit: $30

A full ignition refresh with the Pertronix swap lands around $185–220 in parts. The labor is DIY-friendly with a timing light and a vacuum gauge — the value is in doing the steps in order rather than throwing parts at a symptom.
