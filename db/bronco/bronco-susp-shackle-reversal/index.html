Ford Bronco / Suspension

Front Shackle Reversal on an Early Bronco — Why It Doesn't Apply, and What Fixes Wander Instead

Difficulty 4/56–14 hrs$100–6001966-1977
Short answer

A front shackle reversal is a leaf-spring modification, and the early Bronco does not have front leaf springs: the 1966–77 Bronco's solid front axle rides on coil springs and radius arms, with leaf springs only at the rear. There is no front shackle to reverse. If you came here because the truck wanders, darts or rides harshly up front, the fixes are caster (C-bushings), the track bar, worn radius arm bushings and the steering — covered below.

Shackle reversals belong to leaf-sprung front axles, where the factory puts the shackle ahead of the axle and a reversal moves it behind. Advice written for those trucks gets repeated for the early Bronco, and it sends owners hunting for parts that don't exist on this chassis. The early Bronco's front axle is located fore-aft by two radius arms and side to side by a track bar, so its ride and steering behaviour comes from those parts and the coils.

What actually causes front-end wander on an early Bronco

  • Low caster. Caster is set where the radius arms meet the axle, through C-bushings. A lift or worn bushings drop caster, and low caster reads as vague on-center feel, slow return-to-center and crosswind wander. Wild Horses sells offset C-bushing kits for 1966–79 Broncos to correct it.
  • Worn radius arm bushings. Soft or split bushings at the frame end let the axle shift under braking and over bumps.
  • The track bar. A loose or worn track bar lets the axle move side to side. After a lift the axle also sits off-center unless an adjustable bar re-centers it.
  • Steering. A worn steering box, loose box mounting, or worn drag link and tie rod ends add play that feels like wander. Ford changed the factory boxes over the run, so identify yours before buying parts.

The fix, in order

  1. Inspect before buying. With the truck on the ground and a helper rocking the steering wheel, watch the drag link ends, tie rod ends, the track bar ends and the steering box mount for movement.
  2. Check the radius arm bushings at the frame end for cracks and movement; replace them as a set.
  3. Measure caster with an angle gauge or at an alignment shop. If it is low, fit offset C-bushings (the axle-end joint), then have the shop set caster and toe.
  4. Re-center the axle with an adjustable track bar on a lifted truck, and check the track bar mounts for elongated holes.
  5. Address the steering box and brace if play remains — see the steering stabilizer and box brace guide.

Ride quality

A harsh front ride on an early Bronco usually comes from tired or over-stiff coils, worn shocks, or bump stops that are too short after a lift, not from spring geometry. New coils matched to your weight and shocks matched to your ride height are the fix; see the 2" lift and 4" lift guides.

Why it works

  • The real fixes (C-bushings, bushings, track bar) are bolt-on
  • Inspection costs nothing and finds most problems
  • No frame welding needed

Trade-offs

  • Correcting caster still needs an alignment rack to verify
  • Several worn parts usually share the blame, so budget for more than one
  • Steering box work can turn into a box replacement on a high-mileage truck

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands (4)
  • torque wrench
  • pry bar
  • caster/angle gauge
  • measuring tape
