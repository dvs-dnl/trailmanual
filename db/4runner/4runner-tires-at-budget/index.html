Toyota 4Runner / Wheels & Tires

Budget All-Terrain — Big Capability Per Dollar

Difficulty 1/51–2 hrs$950–15002003-2009, 2010-2024
Short answer

The premium all-terrain gets the headlines, but a value-tier AT covers the vast majority of how a 4Runner is used — and the money saved buys sliders or a winch that matter more than the last few percent of tread compound. This is the sensible-money tire pick, with the two sizes that bolt on without a body shop.

Where a value AT wins

A mid-priced all-terrain from a reputable maker gives up little to a flagship on dry dirt, gravel, and light trail, while running quieter on the highway and costing meaningfully less per set. For a daily-driven 4Runner that sees weekend forest roads and the occasional moderate trail, that is the right balance of grip, manners, and price.

The gap between tiers has narrowed. Falken's Wildpeak A/T4W replaced the long-running A/T3W with a revised compound, staggered shoulder blocks, and a tread warranty up to 65,000 miles — flagship-adjacent durability at a value-tier price. General's Grabber ATX and Cooper's Discoverer AT3 4S sit in the same band. Where a value tire still gives ground is severe-service sidewall toughness and the last increment of wet-rock and deep-snow bite.

The two sizes that matter on a 4Runner

Every 4Runner from the 3rd gen forward runs a 6x139.7 bolt pattern on a 106.1 mm hub bore, and the common 5th gen wheel is a 17x7 at roughly +15 mm offset. That wheel takes both of the sizes worth considering:

  • 265/70R17 — about 31.6 in tall. This is the factory SR5 and Trail size. It bolts to a stock truck with no trimming, no rub at full lock, and no regear conversation. Figure roughly $170-215 per tire in the value tier.
  • 285/70R17 — about 32.7 in tall and 11.2 in wide. This is the size most builds land on. It fits a stock-height 4Runner with mild rubbing at full lock, and clears cleanly with a leveling or mild lift plus fender-liner and plastic trimming. A body mount chop is usually optional at this width. Figure roughly $210-265 per tire.

Step past 285/70R17 to a 285/75R17 (about 33.8 in) and the job changes character — that is a body mount chop, liner surgery, and rocker cap trimming. That path is covered in the 33-inch fitment guide, and it stops being a value-tier decision.

Reading the sidewall before you buy

Three markings decide whether a value AT suits your truck:

  1. Load range. A stock-ish 4Runner is fine on an SL or a P-metric. A 4Runner carrying a rear bumper, a drawer system, and a roof load wants a C or D load range so the sidewall does not squirm under weight. The heavier casing costs a little ride comfort and a little fuel economy.
  2. Three-Peak Mountain Snowflake. If you see genuine winter, the 3PMSF stamp separates an all-terrain that works in snow from a summer tire with blocks on it. Most current value ATs carry it — confirm on the specific size, because the rating is granted per size, not per model line.
  3. Load index and speed rating. Match or exceed the figures on the door jamb. Dropping load index to save money is the one corner not worth cutting on a 4,800-5,000 lb truck.

Where you feel the difference

Spend up only when the use demands it. Flagship all-terrains and mud-terrains pull ahead in deep snow, on wet rock, and in severe-service durability against sidewall cuts. A value AT is not the tire for a sharp-rock crawler — there the tougher three-ply casing of a premium AT or an MT earns its premium. If mud, wet granite, and snow are your normal, read the mud-terrain guide and make the trade deliberately.

The other real difference is tread life. A value AT that gets rotated every 5,000 miles and lives at correct pressure often outlasts a neglected premium tire, so the cheapest path to tread life is a rotation schedule rather than a bigger receipt.

What to watch for

  • Buy five, not four. The 4Runner's full-size spare is the whole point of the platform, and a mismatched spare is dead weight the moment you need it on a locked axle.
  • Torque the lug nuts to 83 lb-ft in a star pattern and re-torque after 50-100 miles. Shops overtighten with an impact and warp rotors.
  • Ask for a road-force balance on 285-width tires. Standard spin balancing hides the stiff-spot vibration that shows up at 65-70 mph.
  • A 32.7 in tire on the factory 3.727 ratio costs noticeable low-end response on the 4.0L. That is livable; a 33.8 in tire generally is not, and that is when regearing enters the budget.
  • Recalibrate the speedometer or accept roughly a 3-4 percent optimistic reading on 285/70R17.

Costs and where the savings go

As of 2026, five value-tier all-terrains in 285/70R17 run roughly $1,050-1,325 for the tires plus $150-200 for mount, balance, and disposal — call it $1,200-1,500 out the door. The same five in a flagship AT land $400-600 higher.

That difference is roughly a set of bolt-on rock sliders, or most of a mid-tier winch install, or a full recovery kit with a soft shackle, a kinetic rope, and two rated recovery points. On a 4Runner build, that gear changes outcomes more often than tread compound does.

Bottom line

A value all-terrain is the high-value default for most 4Runners: quiet enough to daily, capable enough for moderate trail, and kind to the budget. Run 265/70R17 for zero modification, 285/70R17 for the size the aftermarket is built around. Confirm 3PMSF and load range on the exact size, buy five, and put the saved money into armor and recovery gear.
