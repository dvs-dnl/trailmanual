Toyota 4Runner / Brakes

Front Brake Pads and Rotors

Difficulty 2/51.5–3 hrs$120–3502003-2009, 2010-2024Jump to steps
Short answer

Front pads on the 4Runner run 40,000–60,000 miles in mixed driving; the job takes under 2 hours, costs $120 for pads-only or $350 with new OEM rotors. The pads come out of the fixed caliper on two caliper pins held by a spring, so the caliper only comes off the knuckle when the rotors are being replaced.

Safety-critical system. Brake work decides whether the vehicle stops. Bleed the system, check for leaks, and test at walking speed before you drive it in traffic. Torque values and sequences come from the factory service manual for your year.

The 4Runner's front brake is a fixed, multi-piston caliper, not a sliding single-piston design. On the 5th gen, the pads hang on two caliper pins retained by a spring, and the pistons sit on both sides of the rotor; 4th gen front calipers are sold as 4-piston units. Parts are common, the pin-and-spring pad change is the same on both generations, and the job needs hand tools you likely already own.

Pads first, rotors when needed. Replace a rotor when it measures at or below the minimum thickness cast into it; the figure differs by generation. DBA lists its 2003–2009 rotor at 28 mm new and 26 mm minimum, and the 5th gen rotor is thicker, so measure with a micrometer and read the number on your rotor rather than using either figure from memory. In practice, you'll get one set of pads on a healthy rotor before warpage or scoring forces a replacement. If you off-road and dunk the rotors in water — creek crossings, mud — they may warp at 30,000 miles even when pad life is still good. That's not a defect, it's heat-cycling.

Pad choice matters for what you'll feel pedal-side. OEM Toyota pads (Advics or Akebono OE) are the quietest and produce moderate dust. Akebono ProACT ceramics produce less dust than OE and feel nearly identical. Avoid the bargain semi-metallic pads at chain auto parts stores — they bite harder cold but they grind your rotors faster and squeal in stop-and-go. For loaded overlanding rigs running 33s, step up to EBC Yellowstuff or Powerstop Z36 — they tolerate sustained heat from steep descents better than OE.

Rotors: stick with Toyota OEM or Centric Premium. Drilled-and-slotted from Powerstop or R1 Concepts look aggressive but offer zero performance benefit for a 4,800-lb truck on the street — the drill holes become stress risers and the slots wear pads faster. If you want better fade resistance, the upgrade path is bigger brakes (Powerbrake X-Line for 6th gen Tacoma calipers swap) not "performance" rotors on stock calipers.

There are no slider bolts on this caliper. The critical torque is on the two 19mm bolts that hold the caliper to the steering knuckle, and published figures conflict: Trail4Runner's install guide lists the factory 19mm bolts at 91 ft-lb, while a 4Runners.com parts-and-torque thread lists them at 137 lb-ft. Look up your year in the factory service manual before you torque them rather than trusting either number. A loose caliper bolt means vibration and eventual failure. Always use a torque wrench on brakes — they're life-safety hardware.

Steps

  1. Loosen front lug nuts while wheels are on the ground. Lift and support the front of the truck on jack stands at the frame.
  2. Remove front wheels. Inspect old rotor and pad thickness.
  3. Open the brake fluid reservoir cap (relieves pressure when compressing pistons). Cover the fender with a rag.
  4. Remove the spring clip that holds the two caliper pins, then drive the pins out with a pin punch. Lift the old pads and shims out of the caliper. Note their orientation.
  5. Spread the pistons back into their bores with a piston spreader, or a flat bar against an old pad, working both sides evenly. Move slowly to avoid forcing fluid backward into ABS.
  6. Pads only: skip to step 9.
  7. Rotors: remove the two 19mm caliper-to-knuckle bolts and hang the caliper from a coat hanger or bungee — never let it dangle by the hose. Pull the old rotor. Wire-brush the hub face clean to the bare metal — even small rust ridges cause runout.
  8. Install the new rotor (lightly oil the hub face to prevent rust seizure — keep oil off the friction surface). Refit the caliper and torque the two 19mm bolts to the service-manual figure for your year (see the conflict above).
  9. Install new pads with new anti-rattle shims. Apply a thin film of brake grease to the backs of the shims, never the friction face. Push the pins through the caliper and pads and refit the spring clip.
  10. Reinstall wheels and torque the lugs. Toyota's owner's manual lists 83 ft-lb for steel wheels and 76 ft-lb for aluminum wheels; aftermarket wheels take the wheel maker's figure.
  11. Pump the brake pedal 5–6 times until firm before driving. Top off fluid. Bed in pads with 8–10 moderate stops from 35 mph.

Why it works

  • Pads change on pins without removing the caliper — no special tools
  • Same procedure 2003–2024 with only pad/rotor part number changes
  • OEM Toyota parts under $300 for full job; $120 pads only
  • 50K+ pad life typical for daily-driven 4Runners

Trade-offs

  • Rotors warp from creek crossings before they wear out on heavy trail rigs
  • Cheap semi-metallic pads will grind rotors faster than they save you money
  • The 2020+ pad part number changed — verify by VIN before ordering
  • No factory brake upgrade path; bigger brakes mean swapping calipers from Tundra or aftermarket

Tools required

  • 19mm socket
  • pin punch
  • torque wrench
  • piston spreader or flat pry bar
  • wire brush
  • brake cleaner
  • jack stands
  • lug wrench
