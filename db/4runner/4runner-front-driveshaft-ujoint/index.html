Front Driveshaft and U-Joint Service (4th and 5th Gen 4Runner)

Difficulty 4/52–5 hrs$30–7002003-2009, 2010-2024

A clunk on 4WD engagement or a vibration that builds under throttle in 4HI usually traces to a dry or seized front driveshaft U-joint. Here is the part most write-ups get wrong: the factory joints on the 4Runner front shaft are staked in, not clip-retained. There are no snap rings to pull. Servicing one means grinding the factory stakes out of the yoke bores and converting to a clip-retained joint like the SKF UJ-10430 — a real press job, not a driveway hour. Budget $30 in parts and an afternoon if you have a press, $100-200 to hand the shaft to a driveline shop, or $300-550 for a complete replacement shaft.

The 4Runner's front driveshaft leads a rough life for a part nobody thinks about. On SR5, Trail, and TRD Off-Road trucks the transfer case is part-time, so in 2WD the front shaft turns with the wheels but carries no torque and sees no articulation cycling — the joints sit in one position, the grease migrates, and the needle bearings brinell into the cup races. On Limited models with the full-time Torsen center differential the front shaft is always driven, which keeps the joints exercised but wears them faster in raw miles. Different failure paths, same part.

Clunk on 4WD engagement or on throttle transition. The most common complaint. A worn joint has enough rotational backlash that the shaft snaps through it each time load reverses. Confirm by crawling under the truck with it in neutral and the wheels chocked: grab the shaft with both hands and try to rotate it against the flanges. Any perceptible rotational play at the joint, as opposed to normal driveline lash at the diff, is a bad joint.

Vibration that builds with speed in 4WD only. Points at the front shaft specifically, since it is only loaded in 4WD on part-time trucks. A vibration present in 2WD is a rear shaft, tire balance, or carrier bearing issue instead.

Rust-colored powder around the bearing caps. The tell-tale. Fretting corrosion from a dry joint pushes fine reddish-brown dust out around the cap seals. If you see it, the joint is dry and its remaining life is short.

Notchy rotation. With the shaft out, work each joint through its full sweep by hand. It should move smoothly in every direction with light, even resistance. Notchiness, a tight spot, or any grinding feel means replace.

Slip yoke binding. Separate failure, often mistaken for a joint. The splined slip section should telescope by hand with moderate effort. A seized slip yoke transmits driveline plunge straight into the transfer case output and diff pinion, and it produces a shudder on suspension compression rather than a speed-related vibration.

Toyota assembles these driveshafts by pressing a die into the yoke bore after the bearing cap is seated, rolling a burr of yoke material over the cap edge to lock it in. There is no groove and no clip. That construction is why Toyota sells the driveshaft as an assembly and why dealers will tell you the joints are not serviceable.

They are serviceable, with caveats. The conversion is well documented across the Toyota truck platforms: grind or drill away the staked material at all four cap bores until the caps can move, press the old joint out, clean the bores back to round, and install a clip-retained joint that carries its own retaining grooves. The SKF UJ-10430 is the joint most commonly cited for this conversion on Toyota truck shafts. Measure before you buy — cap diameter and cap-to-cap span vary by shaft and year, and a joint that is close but not correct will either not press in or will press in loose. Take the old joint or the shaft to a driveline shop and have them confirm the number.

If that paragraph made you uneasy, that is the correct reaction, and the alternative is legitimate: a local driveline shop will do the same conversion for $100-200 with the right press and tooling, and they will balance the shaft afterward. For a lot of owners that is the better trade.

Metric sockets and a torque wrench, a hydraulic press (a 12-ton shop press, or a heavy bench vise with sockets as a distant second choice), an angle grinder or die grinder with a carbide burr for the stakes, snap ring pliers for the new clips, a grease gun, a paint marker, and a pry bar. A bearing splitter helps. Penetrating oil for the flange bolts, which live in road salt and mud.

  1. Chock the rear wheels, set the parking brake, and support the truck on stands. Never work under a truck on a jack.
  2. Mark the shaft-to-flange orientation at both ends with a paint marker, and mark which end faces the transfer case. Driveshafts are balanced as assemblies and clocking one 180 degrees introduces a vibration that no amount of joint work will cure.
  3. Break the flange bolts loose at the front differential and transfer case flanges. They are frequently seized — penetrating oil and patience beat a rounded head.
  4. Remove the shaft. Support its weight as the last bolts come out.
  5. Clean the yoke bores with a wire wheel so you can see the staked material clearly.
  6. Grind the stakes. Work the burr away from each of the four cap bores with a carbide burr or a small grinding stone, removing the rolled-over material and no more. You are removing the lip, not opening the bore.
  7. Press the old joint out. Support the yoke ear, press one cap through, rotate, press the other. Expect it to be stubborn — a staked joint that has never moved often needs heat on the yoke ear and real press force.
  8. Clean and inspect the bores. They must be round, clean, and free of galling. A bore that got opened up by the grinder is scrap, and that shaft now needs replacement.
  9. Press the new joint in square. Start one cap, insert the cross, then work both caps in alternately with the cross held centered. Keep every needle bearing standing in its cup — one tipped needle produces a tight joint that fails within a few thousand miles.
  10. Seat the retaining clips in their grooves, both sides of both caps.
  11. Check the joint by hand through its full sweep. Smooth and free, no bind, no notch. If it is tight, seat the caps by striking the yoke ears sharply with a brass hammer, which relieves the preload.
  12. Grease the joint until clean grease purges from all four cap seals.
  13. Reinstall in the marked clock position and torque the flange bolts to the service-manual value for your year — do not guess, and do not reuse visibly stretched hardware.
  14. Test. Quiet surface, 4HI, gentle acceleration and deceleration. The clunk and vibration should be gone.

Grinding into the bore is the failure that ends the job. Take the stake and stop. If you open the bore even slightly, the new cap presses in loose, walks under load, and destroys the yoke.

Pressing a joint in cocked cracks a cap. Go slow, check squareness constantly, and back off and realign the moment anything binds.

Balance is real. Reinstall in the original clock position. If a vibration remains after a known-good joint, the shaft is bent or out of balance and the answer is a shop balance or a replacement shaft.

Do not neglect the slip yoke. While the shaft is out, grease the slip splines and confirm they telescope freely. This is the second-most-common front driveline complaint and you are already there.

Exercise 4WD. The root cause of most of these failures is disuse. Engage 4HI for a few miles on a loose surface once a month. It keeps the joints, the slip yoke, the front diff, and the actuator alive. While you are thinking about it, check the front diff fluid — 4runner-front-diff-fluid-4runner — and the transfer case, 4runner-transfer-case-fluid-4runner.

Consider the whole shaft on a high-mileage truck. If both joints are bad, the slip yoke is worn, and the shaft has 180,000 miles on it, the arithmetic favors a replacement assembly with non-staked joints over rebuilding a tired core.

The value play is the driveline shop. They have the press, the correct joints on the shelf, the tooling to keep the bores true, and a balancer — for $100-250 you get a shaft that is right, without owning a press or gambling on grinder control. DIY the removal and reinstallation, which is the part that saves real labor, and hand them the shaft on the bench.
