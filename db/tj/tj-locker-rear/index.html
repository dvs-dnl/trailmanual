Rear Locker for the TJ — Dana 35 and Dana 44

Difficulty 4/54–6 hrs$280–14851997-2006
Short answer

A TJ Rubicon doesn't need this guide: it left the factory with air-actuated Tru-Lok lockers front and rear. On every other TJ, check your rear axle first. If it's a Dana 35, address the axle's known weak points before adding a locker, because a locked Dana 35 on aggressive terrain asks the C-clips and side shafts to do something they weren't built for.

The rear axle is the build variable here. Every non-Rubicon TJ has a Dana 35 rear unless it was ordered with the optional Dana 44, and the 2003–2006 Rubicon always has a Dana 44. The Dana 35 uses C-clip axle retention, so when a side shaft breaks under load, the wheel can separate from the vehicle. Its small ring gear and thin side shafts are the limiting factor. Adding a locker to a Dana 35 and running 35" tires on technical terrain is a scenario where shaft failure becomes a real probability, not a theoretical one. The durable answers are a rear axle swap to a Dana 44 or Ford 8.8, or chromoly shafts with a C-clip eliminator before the locker goes in.

If you're keeping the Dana 35, match the locker to the build. A Powertrax Lock-Right lunchbox locker ($280) is the budget path for 31s and a light foot. It drops into the existing carrier and ratchets in tight pavement turns. The selectable options are the Eaton Performance ELocker for 27-spline axles (about $1,485) and the ARB Air Locker RD105, which is built for upgraded 30-spline shafts. For a rig that mixes street driving and trail use, selectable is the correct choice, since it stays open until you flip the switch. The ARB also requires an air compressor and solenoid kit, sold separately at approximately $250, so plan for the additional install time and wiring. An automatic Detroit Locker suits a dedicated trail rig, though it noticeably changes driving behavior on pavement.

If your TJ has the optional Dana 44, ARB lists 30-spline Dana 44 lockers by ratio: the RD117 for 3.73 and down, and the RD116 for 3.92 and up.

Rubicon owners should not buy an aftermarket rear locker for a working truck. Jeep's announcement called for air-actuated lockers on each axle, made by Tochigi Fuji Sanjyo, and they're Tru-Lok units, not ARB. They're driver-actuated from a dash switch in low range below about 10 mph, the rear locks first, and the rear acts as a limited-slip when unlocked. If yours has stopped engaging, diagnose the factory air system before you spend money replacing it.

Steps

  1. Drain the rear differential fluid completely.
  2. Raise the rear axle on jack stands with both rear tires off the ground.
  3. Remove both rear wheels and brake drums or calipers (set aside; do not disconnect brake lines).
  4. Remove the differential cover and the cross-shaft retaining pin, push the axle shafts in to release the C-clips, and slide the shafts out. Upgraded shafts with a C-clip eliminator are retained at the axle end instead.
  5. Remove the differential carrier assembly from the housing. Measure and record backlash first, and mark the bearing caps so they go back on the same side.
  6. For a lunchbox locker, leave the carrier in place, remove the spider gears, and install the locker per manufacturer instructions. For a full-case locker, move the ring gear to the new carrier.
  7. Reinstall the carrier assembly with bearing preload as specified. Dana axle backlash typically runs between 0.005" and 0.009", but follow the factory service manual figure for your axle.
  8. For ARB Air Locker: route air line through axle housing per ARB instructions; connect to solenoid and compressor.
  9. Install new diff cover gasket or apply RTV; torque cover bolts to 20–25 ft-lbs in a cross pattern.
  10. Reinstall axle shafts and retaining hardware; torque to spec.
  11. Refill with the gear oil specified for the axle; use friction modifier if your TJ has a limited-slip OEM unit.
  12. Reinstall brake hardware, wheels; torque lug nuts to 95 ft-lbs.
  13. Test on dry pavement at low speed to verify engagement feel before trail use.

Why it works

  • A locker on a Dana 44 or a reinforced Dana 35 is a capable rear end for moderate to aggressive trail use
  • A selectable design preserves on-road driving behavior — unlocked for streets, locked for trails
  • A lunchbox locker provides automatic engagement with no compressor, wiring, or solenoid
  • Dramatically improves traction on side-hills, loose terrain, and one-wheel-up obstacle lines

Trade-offs

  • Dana 35 C-clip retention is a failure risk under locker loads — upgrade axle shafts or replace the axle before locking it
  • ARB Air Locker requires compressor, solenoid wiring, and air line routing — significant additional install complexity and cost
  • Automatic and lunchbox lockers cause noticeable handling changes on pavement — ratcheting in tight turns, potential for rear-end stepping in low-grip corners
  • Installation requires differential expertise — incorrect bearing preload or backlash will damage the ring and pinion
  • The locker alone does not compensate for regear needs — 35" tires still require 4.56 or 4.88 gears regardless of locking differential

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • socket set
  • torque wrench
  • bearing puller
  • pinion depth gauge
  • dial indicator
  • RTV sealant
