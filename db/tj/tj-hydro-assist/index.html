Hydraulic Assist Steering: Is It Worth It on a TJ?

Difficulty 4/56–16 hrs$1100–24001997-2002, 2003-2006

Hydraulic assist is a heavy-tire fix, not a general upgrade. On a TJ running 37s — or 35s with sticky rubber and a front locker — hydro-assist takes load off a steering box that was never sized for it and gives you authority against a rock at crawl speed. On 33s and smaller it buys nothing, and bolting it onto a worn front end to cure wander hides the real fault until something breaks.

Hydro-assist adds a hydraulic ram between the axle and the steering linkage, fed by the power-steering system, so hydraulic pressure pushes the tires while the box mostly meters where that pressure goes. The benefit is specific: turning oversized tires against an obstacle at low speed, where the factory Saginaw box builds heat, the sector shaft deflects, and the driver ends up fighting the wheel. It does nothing measurable at highway speed, and it does not make a loose front end tight.

Three pieces have to cooperate. The ram is a double-acting hydraulic cylinder, typically 1.5-inch or 1.75-inch bore with 6–8 inches of stroke, with the body clamped to the axle tube and the rod attached to the tie rod or a high-steer arm. The control source is either a ported steering box — a box machined with two extra ports so it directs flow to the ram in proportion to steering input — or a full double-ended box. And the supply side is a pump with enough flow and pressure to feed both the box and the ram without starving either.

The critical detail people skip is the control source. Running a ram off T-fittings spliced into the existing box lines is the cheap version and it produces a system that can push the tires when the driver is not asking, because there is nothing metering return flow. Use a ported box or a purpose-built assist box. PSC and West Texas Offroad both sell TJ-specific ported boxes and full kits; Howe sells the ram and valve hardware separately for people building their own.

37-inch tires and up. A front locker on 35s in rock. A rig that already cooks its power-steering fluid or shows a box that visibly flexes under load. Competition and hard rock use where the driver is steering against a tire that is loaded and not rolling.

The TJ box is on the small side for any of that, which is why the ram is only half the install. A steering box brace — Currie, Synergy, and Rusty's all make one for the TJ at roughly $130–200 — ties the sector shaft snout to the frame rail so the added hydraulic force is not trying to crack the box housing or tear the thin unibody-style frame section at the box mount. Adding a ram without a brace is the single most common way owners turn a steering upgrade into a cracked box.

A cooler is the other non-optional piece. Hydro-assist does more work, more work makes more heat, and hot power-steering fluid foams and loses assist exactly when you need it. A small inline cooler runs $60–120 and mounts in the return line.

On 33s, a healthy box, a braced sector shaft, and tight linkage steer fine. If the complaint is wander, shimmy, or death wobble, hydro-assist is the wrong purchase — those are worn-part symptoms, and hydraulics layered over slop hides them until a joint separates.

Work the front end in this order before spending on a ram: the track bar at both ends, the drag link and tie rod ends, upper and lower ball joints, unit bearings, and the steering box mount bolts and frame section around them. A new steering stabilizer belongs at the end of that list, not the start. If the truck steers straight and tight after that work and still overwhelms the box turning 37s in the rocks, hydro-assist is the correct next step.

Ram sizing follows tire size and steering geometry. A 1.5-inch bore is the common choice for 35s and light 37s; 1.75-inch is for 37s and up, heavy front ends, and locked fronts. Oversizing the ram on a light rig makes the steering feel numb and can overpower the linkage.

Mounting location decides how much the ram helps and how exposed it is. Clamped to the axle tube with the rod to the tie rod is the standard TJ arrangement and works with the factory Y-link. If the rig is already running high-steer knuckles and a crossover setup, mount the ram to the high-steer arm instead — the geometry is cleaner and the ram sits higher out of harm's way. Either way, cycle the steering lock to lock by hand with the ram loosely mounted and confirm the rod never bottoms before the box does. A ram that reaches its stroke limit before the box reaches lock will tear its mount off the axle.

Plan for the pump. The factory TJ pump can feed a modest ram on a mild build, but a 1.75-inch ram on 37s wants more flow than it reliably makes when hot. PSC and others sell higher-flow replacement pumps in the $300–500 range; budget for one rather than discovering the shortfall on the trail.

This system is the steering. Treat every step as safety-critical.

Route and clamp hose away from exhaust, away from the pitman arm sweep, and away from anything that moves at full articulation. Cycle the suspension through full droop and full stuff with the hoses attached and watch for tension or rub. Use proper hydraulic hose rated well above system pressure with crimped ends — not push-on fittings, not pipe thread with tape.

Bleed the system completely before the first drive. With the front wheels off the ground and the reservoir topped, cycle the steering lock to lock slowly ten or more times, checking level between cycles, until the fluid stops aerating and the wheel stops feeling spongy. Trapped air in a hydro-assist system reads as intermittent assist, which on a steep off-camber line is how people roll rigs.

Check the ram mount bolts and hose clamps after the first outing and periodically after that. If you are not confident plumbing hydraulics, pay a shop — a burst line here is a steering failure, not an inconvenience.

A complete TJ hydro-assist kit with ram, ported box, hoses, and mounts runs roughly $1,200–1,800 from PSC or West Texas Offroad. Add $130–200 for a box brace, $60–120 for a cooler, and $300–500 if the pump needs upgrading. Shop installation runs $600–1,200 depending on whether high-steer and linkage work go in at the same time. Realistic all-in on a TJ: $1,800–2,800 DIY, $2,500–4,000 with a shop doing the plumbing.

The honest framing is that hydro-assist is the last item on a big-tire steering list, not the first. A braced box, a straight and tight front end, correct track bar geometry for your lift height, and properly sized linkage solve most TJ steering complaints for a quarter of the money. Buy the ram when the rig has outgrown all of that.
