Jeep Wrangler TJ / Maintenance

Transfer Case Fluid — NP231 and NV231

Difficulty 1/50.5–1 hrs$20–401997-2002, 2003-2006Jump to steps
Short answer

Drain and refill the TJ's NP231 or NV231 transfer case every 30,000 miles, and sooner after deep water, with ATF+4 pumped in until it begins to weep from the fill hole. Budget two to three quarts, about 45 minutes, and one rule you cannot skip: break the fill plug loose before you drain anything.

Every TJ except the Rubicon carries the NP231 (relabeled NV231 after New Venture Gear took over the design). It is a chain-driven, part-time transfer case with a 2.72:1 planetary low range and an aluminum housing. The Rubicon uses the NV241OR with a 4:1 low range, so if your Jeep has the Rubicon badge, the fill procedure is similar but the case is a different part. The 231's chain and planetary are durable, but they live on the fluid film in the case, and the case has no filter and no cooler. Fluid that has cooked, picked up water, or collected fine metal is the main thing that shortens its life.

Which fluid. Mopar specifies ATF+4 for the later NV231 (Mopar 68218057AC is a one-quart bottle), and earlier owner manuals call for a Dexron-type ATF. ATF+4 is the sensible default because it is the current Mopar fluid and you probably already stock it for the transmission. Do not substitute gear oil: it is the wrong viscosity for the pump-and-splash lubrication in this case and can change shift feel. Whatever you choose, use one fluid consistently so you can read its condition at each service.

How much it takes. Published capacity figures run lower than what actually goes in after a full drain, and the forum threads reflect that disagreement. The reliable method is to fill until fluid reaches the bottom edge of the fill hole, which is how the factory level is defined. Expect roughly 1.5 to 2 quarts, but buy three so you never stop short. The Jeep must be level when you check; a nose-up or nose-down parking spot changes the apparent level by an ounce or more.

Plug access and the stuck-plug trap. The fill plug sits on the rear half of the case and the drain plug on the underside. Both plugs have a recess for a 1/2-inch square drive, so a 1/2-inch ratchet or breaker bar goes straight into the plug with no socket. If a skid plate covers the case, you may need to unbolt it first. Always crack the fill plug before you open the drain, because a drained case with a seized fill plug is a Jeep you cannot refill and cannot safely drive. Penetrating oil on the plug an evening ahead helps. If it will not break loose with a long bar, stop and sort that out with the case still full.

Reading the drain. Warm the case with a 10-minute drive so the fluid flows, but be careful, because the case and fluid can scald. Dark red-brown is normal for a 30,000-mile interval. Black and burnt-smelling fluid means heat or overdue service; shorten the interval to 15,000 miles. Milky or foamy fluid means water has entered, most often through a clogged breather vent or after a deep crossing; change it immediately, then run it again a few hundred miles later. A light gray paste on a magnet or a pinch of fine fuzz is normal wear. Visible flakes or chips mean something is breaking inside, such as a chain pad or a bearing, and the case needs to come apart before it fails on the trail.

Vent and seals. The case breathes through a vent with a hose routed upward. A pinched, plugged, or low-routed vent builds pressure on a hot case and pushes fluid past the output seals. Check that the hose is intact and routed high, particularly after a lift or exhaust work moved things around. A weeping rear output seal usually points to a worn tail-housing bushing rather than the seal alone; the NP231 service guide covers that failure and the slip-yoke-eliminator decision.

Magnetic plug. Some magnetic replacement plugs exist for the 231 and cost $10 to $20. Confirm the thread size and drive style match before you buy, and do not over-tighten, since the housing is aluminum and the threads strip.

Costs and sources. ATF+4: $7 to $11 per quart; plan on three. Hand fluid pump: $10 to $15. Optional magnetic plug: $10 to $20. Shop labor if you skip DIY: $60 to $120 plus fluid. Do it alongside the differential fluid service so both gear-case services and the transfer case share the same interval, and see the front driveshaft guide if you find the front output seal weeping.

Steps

  1. Drive a short distance to warm the fluid, then park on the most level ground you can find. Set the parking brake and chock the wheels.
  2. Slide a wide drain pan under the transfer case. If a skid plate is bolted over it, remove the skid plate first.
  3. Break the fill plug loose with a 1/2-inch ratchet and then snug it back by hand. If it does not move, stop here.
  4. Remove the drain plug and let the case empty for 15 to 20 minutes. Catch a sample in a clean jar and look at color, smell, and any particles.
  5. If the plug has a washer or sealing ring, replace it. Clean the plug, then reinstall it and tighten to about 20 to 25 ft-lbs. The housing is aluminum, so use a torque wrench rather than a long breaker bar.
  6. Attach a hand pump or squeeze bottle with a hose to the new ATF+4 and feed it into the fill hole until fluid reaches the lower edge of the hole and begins to seep out.
  7. Reinstall the fill plug to the same torque and wipe the case clean so a leak will be visible later.
  8. Check that the vent hose is intact and routed upward and clear of the exhaust.
  9. Drive 10 miles, shifting into 4-Hi and 4-Lo once on dirt (never on dry pavement), then re-inspect for weeping at both plugs and the output seals.
  10. Write the date and mileage on a strip of masking tape or in your maintenance log. The interval is 30,000 miles, or 15,000 if you wheel in mud and water.

Why it works

  • Cheap and quick: about $20 to $40 in fluid and a morning of work, with a diagnostic payoff that no other 30-minute job offers.
  • A drain sample tells you about the chain, bearings, and seals before a failure leaves you stranded.
  • Fixing a clogged vent during the service prevents the seal leaks that otherwise get blamed on the case.

Trade-offs

  • A seized fill plug turns a routine service into a tow if you drain first.
  • Fill-to-the-hole means the quantity changes by rig and parking spot, which confuses first-timers who expect a fixed number.
  • The housing and plugs are aluminum and strip easily with a long bar or heavy hand.
  • Fresh fluid will not fix a worn chain or a failing output bearing; if the drain shows chips, plan a rebuild rather than another refill.

Tools required

  • 3/8 ratchet
  • fluid pump
  • drain pan
  • 1/2 drive ratchet for fill/drain plugs
