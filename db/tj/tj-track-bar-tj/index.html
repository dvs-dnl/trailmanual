Jeep Wrangler TJ / Steering

Track Bar Replacement — TJ Specifics

Difficulty 3/51–3 hrs$150–4001997-2006
Short answer

The TJ front track bar locates your axle side-to-side; if you've added a lift over 2" or you feel highway wander, replace it with an adjustable HD bar to re-center the axle and stop death wobble.

The TJ front track bar uses a tapered-stud mount on the frame side and a bolt-through bracket on the axle side. The factory bar is fine at stock height, but when you lift the front the axle shifts toward the driver side because the bar pulls it that way through the lift arc. After a 2"–4" lift, an adjustable bar is required if you want the steering to feel right.

The TJ has one quirk worth knowing: the factory frame-side mount uses a tapered hole. Some aftermarket bars (Synergy, RockJock) keep the tapered fitment; others (cheaper bars) convert it to a straight through-bolt and require drilling the axle bracket to 9/16". The drilled-out conversion is permanent. If you want to keep the option of reverting to a stock bar, choose a bar that preserves the taper.

Brand picks: Synergy 8520-01 ($360) — dual-durometer bushings, Teflon bearing, maintenance-free, quiet ride. RockJock CE-9120TJJ ($330) — 4130 chromoly tube with rebuildable Johnny Joints, the choice for hard wheeling. Rough Country adjustable ($160) — budget option, urethane bushings, fine for daily-driver lifts under 3". For stock-height TJs replacing a worn factory bar, OEM Mopar 52088433AB ($145) is a direct swap.

Symptoms that you need a new track bar: steering wheel off-center on a straight road, axle visibly off-center between the wheel wells, clunking over bumps from worn bushings, or active death wobble after hitting a bump at 45+ mph. Death wobble has multiple causes (worn ball joints, loose tie rod, bent steering link), but a worn track bar is the single most common trigger.

Diagnosing the track bar before you buy a new one saves a wasted purchase. With the front on stands and the axle supported (tension off the bar), grab the bar at the axle end and pry it up and down with a large flat bar or breaker bar. Factory rubber bushings should show only a small amount of flex — anything that feels like a hinge, or a bar that clunks when you release the pry bar, is worn. Also check for play at the frame-side tapered stud by having a helper turn the steering wheel lock to lock while you watch the stud for movement in the mount — a loose taper will show visible wobble there, and that's a different failure than bushing wear, since a loose taper means the taper itself has worn oversize and no amount of bushing replacement fixes it.

Lifts over about 4" change the geometry enough that a straight-length adjustable bar isn't the whole fix. As the axle drops on a longer spring or coil spacer, the factory frame-side mounting point stays at its stock height, so the bar now runs at a steeper downward angle than the axle-side mounting bracket was designed for. That angle mismatch binds the bushings at full droop and can put the bar into a bind before the suspension even reaches its travel limit. A track bar relocation bracket (JKS950 and similar) raises the frame-side mount to restore a closer-to-stock bar angle. Below 4" of lift this is optional; above it, treat the relocation bracket as part of the track bar job, not a separate upgrade — the bar you buy is the wrong length for the geometry without it.

Final torque happens at ride height. The factory spec is 74 ft-lbs at the frame side and 55 ft-lbs at the axle bracket — these are lower than JK numbers because the TJ uses smaller hardware. Aftermarket bars with larger bolts will specify their own torque, often 100–125 ft-lbs. Always follow the bar manufacturer's torque spec for the supplied hardware.

Length adjustment on an adjustable bar happens through a threaded sleeve at the center of the tube, locked with a jam nut on each side. Set the starting length to match the old bar (or, on a fresh lift, to your measured centering numbers below) before you torque anything, then fine-tune once the Jeep is back on the ground. Don't skip the jam nuts — a track bar that backs out of adjustment under load is a steering failure waiting to happen, not a minor annoyance.

A track bar alone will not cure death wobble if other steering components are also worn. Before condemning the track bar, rule out the tie rod ends, drag link ends, steering stabilizer, and ball joints — a worn part in any of those will reintroduce the wobble even with a brand-new track bar installed.

Steps

  1. Park on level ground, set parking brake, chock rear wheels.
  2. Loosen front lug nuts, lift front, set on jack stands under frame, remove wheels.
  3. Measure frame rail to inner tire edge on both sides — record.
  4. Support front axle on floor jack to take tension off the track bar.
  5. Remove the axle-side bolt first (typically 18mm head).
  6. Remove the frame-side bolt (21mm) — note tapered stud fitment if applicable.
  7. Compare new bar to old at equal length as starting point.
  8. Install frame side first, then axle side, finger-tight.
  9. Reinstall wheels, lower to ride height.
  10. Torque to manufacturer spec at ride height.
  11. Re-measure axle centering — adjust bar length if off by more than 1/8".
  12. Tighten the jam nuts against the adjustment sleeve once centering is correct.
  13. Drive 50 miles, re-check all torque.

Why it works

  • Cures TJ death wobble from worn factory bar
  • Adjustable bar re-centers axle after lift
  • HD bars resist bending on rocks
  • Bolt-in install if you choose a bar that keeps the tapered mount

Trade-offs

  • Some bars require drilling the axle bracket — irreversible
  • Tapered-stud mount can seize and be hard to remove
  • Torque-at-ride-height step is critical or bushings wear early
  • Bar replacement alone won't fix death wobble if ball joints or tie rod are also worn
  • Lifts over ~4" need a relocation bracket too, or the bar runs at a bind-inducing angle

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • 21mm socket
  • 18mm socket
  • breaker bar
  • torque wrench
  • tape measure
  • pry bar (for the play test)
