Jeep Wrangler TJ / Interior

TJ Sound Deadening — Reducing Road and Trail Noise

Difficulty 2/54–8 hrs$60–2501997-2006
Short answer

Sound deadening a TJ reduces drone and panel resonance, but it will not make the Jeep quiet. Expect a modest, noticeable drop in floor and firewall boom on the highway for $60 to $200 in material and a full weekend of work. If your Jeep has a soft top or loud off-road tires, those are bigger noise sources than the sheet metal and deadener will not hide them.

The TJ's interior noise comes from three sources: tire and road noise coming through the floor and wheel wells, engine and exhaust noise through the firewall and floor, and wind noise around soft-top seams and door openings. Butyl damping mat addresses only the first two, and only the part that is caused by metal panels ringing. The sheet metal is thin and flat, so it resonates; adding mass and a constrained layer lowers the ringing. To block airborne noise (rather than damp vibration) you need a decoupling foam layer and a heavy barrier on top, which adds cost and weight.

An honest priority list for a TJ owner who wants a quieter ride:

  1. Hardtop or a quality soft top. If you drive a soft top at 65 mph, it is the loudest thing in the cabin. A hardtop typically makes a bigger difference than any amount of floor mat, and it also matters more than the next items.
  2. Tire choice. An aggressive mud-terrain tire roars on pavement. An all-terrain with a quieter tread pattern reduces road noise more than any deadener will. See TJ tire fitment for 35s for the tradeoffs of tire size.
  3. Firewall and floor damping. This is the work in this guide.
  4. Exhaust. A louder aftermarket exhaust adds drone that no mat cures; consider the exhaust before the mat.
  • Butyl damping mat, 80 mil (2 mm), sold under brands such as Kilmat, Noico, Dynamat, and Second Skin Audio. Quality and thickness are comparable between the better budget brands; compare real thickness and the adhesive's temperature rating on the packaging. Plan on 36 to 50 square feet for floors and firewall, but measure your Jeep with a tape before ordering.
  • Optionally, closed-cell foam (1/8 to 1/4 inch) as a decoupling layer, and mass-loaded vinyl if you want to block airborne noise.
  • Isopropyl alcohol and clean rags
  • A roller for pressing the mat (a hard rubber roller is better than a card)
  • Trim tools, a socket set, a razor knife, and scissors
  • A hair dryer or low-temperature heat gun for working the mat around contours
  • Gloves, because the cut edges of the foil facing are sharp
  1. Prepare the Jeep. Disconnect the battery before you remove seats, before you unplug any seat wiring or work near the airbag circuits. Note that the TJ does not have a full carpet set as standard in all trims, so some of this work involves rubber mats and exposed floor pan.
  1. Remove seats, center console if present, and the carpet or mats. The front seats bolt to the floor with four fasteners each. The rear seat in a TJ tub folds forward and its mounting hardware also comes out. Bag and label the bolts.
  1. Inspect the floor for rust and moisture before covering anything. Press a screwdriver tip firmly into suspect spots near the seat mounts, along the seams, and at the body mounts. Repair any rust before sealing the metal under butyl. Covering moisture accelerates rust. See also TJ seat upgrade if you are replacing seats and will have the interior open anyway.
  1. Clean the metal. Wipe with isopropyl alcohol and let it dry. Dust, oil, and silicone prevent adhesion.
  1. Cut the mat into pieces that suit the panels. Complete coverage is not required; patches that cover 50 to 70 percent of a flat panel provide most of the damping effect. Use the knuckle test: tap the metal, listen for a ring, and place the mat where it rings.
  1. Apply to the floor, firewall, and tunnel. Peel the backing back gradually, press from the center outward, and roll firmly so there are no air pockets. Warm the mat gently over contours. Keep the heat gun moving and away from wiring, fuel lines, and the fuel tank area at the rear; a hair dryer is safer than a high-temperature gun.
  1. Stay out of hot zones and drains. Do not cover floor drain plugs, and keep mat away from exhaust heat shields and areas that become hot, since butyl mat has a temperature limit printed on the product. The transmission tunnel can run hot near the exhaust, so check the product rating.
  1. Optional foam layer. Cover the damped panels with closed-cell foam to decouple the carpet from the metal. Use the same cleaning and adhesive approach.
  1. Reinstall in reverse. Reinstall the seats and tighten the seat bolts to the service manual specification. Reconnect the battery and confirm the airbag warning light behaves normally.
  1. Test drive on pavement and a rough road, at highway speed with the top you normally run. Compare with a recording on your phone if you want an objective check.
  • Rust under the mat. The TJ's floor and the area around the body mounts are known rust spots. Check them before covering and again a year later at the edges.
  • Water. The tub drains must stay open. Do not block the drain plugs, and do not seal seams that water uses to leave.
  • Weight. Full coverage with 80 mil mat adds about 15 to 30 pounds for a Jeep this size. That is minor but worth knowing if you are weight-conscious.
  • Smell and heat. Butyl can give off odor in a hot Jeep. Ventilate during installation and avoid covering parts of the floor near the exhaust.
  • Wiring. The TJ harness runs near the seats and under the dash. Keep mat clear of connectors and avoid cutting into harness clips.

Costs and sources. Budget 80 mil butyl (Kilmat or Noico, 36 to 50 square feet): $60 to $90. Premium brands (Dynamat, Second Skin Audio) cost roughly twice as much for similar coverage. Foam and mass-loaded vinyl add $60 to $200. Roller and trim tools: $15 to $30. Total for a floor-and-firewall damping job is typically $80 to $150 for materials and a full day of work. For an add-on that makes a longer drive more comfortable, pair this with TJ seat upgrade.

Tools required

  • trim removal tool
  • roller or credit card (for pressing deadener)
  • scissors or utility knife
  • heat gun (optional)
  • isopropyl alcohol
