Jeep Wrangler TJ / Maintenance

Serpentine Belt Replacement

Difficulty 2/50.5–1 hrs$30–601997-2006Jump to steps
Short answer

If your TJ's serpentine belt is older than five years, or shows cracks across the ribs when you flex it backward, replace it before it strands you. The whole job is under an hour with one breaker bar. On the 4.0L the same belt drives the water pump, alternator, and power steering, so a failure is an overheat, a dead charging system, and heavy steering all at once.

The TJ uses a single six-rib serpentine belt around the crank, water pump, alternator, power-steering pump, A/C compressor if equipped, and a spring-loaded automatic tensioner. Belts fail on age more than mileage — the rubber hardens and the ribs crack whether the truck is driven or parked, so a garage-kept TJ with 30,000 miles and a ten-year-old belt is as likely to throw one as a daily driver at 120,000.

Knowing when it is done

Flex a section of belt backward so the ribs open up. Fine cracks running across a rib are normal wear; cracks deep enough to see the fabric, cracks in more than a few spots per inch, missing rib chunks, or a glazed shiny underside all mean replace. Fraying at the belt edges points at a misaligned pulley rather than a worn belt, and swapping the belt without finding the misalignment gets you a second shredded belt.

Noise is the other tell. A squeal on cold start that fades as the engine warms is usually a glazed belt or a weak tensioner. A steady chirp at idle that changes with accessory load is more often a pulley bearing. A rhythmic slapping sound is a belt with a chunk missing.

Getting the right length

Length depends on year, engine, and whether the truck has air conditioning, and buying by "1999 Wrangler" alone is how people end up back at the counter.

For the 4.0L with A/C, the Mopar belt is 5281668AA and the common Gates equivalent is K060910 — six ribs, roughly 91 inches effective length. For a 4.0L without A/C, the belt is materially shorter; the usual Gates number is in the K060749 range at about 75 inches. The 2.5L and later 2.4L four-cylinder use different lengths again. Confirm by VIN at the parts counter or at the Gates catalog, or measure the old belt around its ribbed face with a length of string before you order. If the truck has been modified — an A/C delete, a different alternator bracket, a high-output alternator with a different pulley diameter — the catalog number is a starting point, not an answer, and measuring is the only way to be sure.

Inspect the tensioner while you are in there

The automatic tensioner is a spring-loaded arm with a smooth idler pulley, on the front of the engine. It is the part most likely to be the actual problem when a fresh belt still squeals.

With the belt off, swing the tensioner arm through its full travel by hand. It should move smoothly with steady spring resistance and return firmly with no grinding, no free play at the pivot, and no clunk. Spin the tensioner pulley: any roughness, side-to-side wobble, or noise is a dead bearing. Do the same for every other pulley — alternator, water pump, power-steering pump, A/C compressor, and any idler. A rough water-pump bearing is your cue to do the coolant system work now rather than twice.

Replacement tensioner assemblies for the 4.0L run roughly $45–90 depending on brand; verify the number by VIN at the counter since bracket styles changed across the TJ run. Fitting a new belt to a tired tensioner is a repeat job within months, and a tensioner that is losing spring tension is also what lets the belt slip enough to cook a water pump.

Costs and sources

Belt: $24–40 (Gates K060910 around $24, Mopar 5281668AA around $38). Tensioner assembly if needed: $45–90. Idler pulley: $20–35. A shop charges roughly $120–180 for the belt alone, which is most of the reason to do it yourself.

Carry the old belt as a trail spare once it is off, provided it is not cracked through — a used belt in the back is what gets you off a forest road. Pair this with the rest of the under-hood maintenance set: the alternator and radiator guides cover the two accessories most likely to be the reason you are looking at the belt in the first place.

Steps

  1. Park the TJ, key off, hood up, engine cool. Cold matters — the belt path runs next to the exhaust manifold.
  2. Photograph the existing belt routing from two angles, or note the diagram on the radiator support.
  3. Position a 1/2-inch breaker bar or a dedicated serpentine tool on the tensioner pulley center bolt.
  4. Rotate the tensioner counterclockwise to relieve tension and hold it there.
  5. Slide the belt off the alternator pulley first, then release the tensioner slowly and remove the belt.
  6. Spin each pulley by hand and swing the tensioner through its travel — alternator, idler, tensioner, water pump, power steering, A/C.
  7. Compare the new belt to the old one on the bench for length and rib count.
  8. Route the new belt per your photo, seating it on every pulley except the alternator.
  9. Pull the tensioner counterclockwise again and slip the belt onto the alternator pulley.
  10. Release the tensioner slowly against the new belt and confirm every rib is seated in every groove — a belt riding one rib off will destroy itself in minutes.
  11. Start the engine and watch the belt track for 30 seconds. No squeal, no walking side to side on the pulleys, tensioner arm steady rather than oscillating.

Why it works

  • The cheapest preventive job in the engine bay
  • 30 to 60 minutes start to finish
  • No special tools beyond a breaker bar
  • Forces an inspection of every accessory pulley bearing while access is open

Trade-offs

  • Wrong belt length is a real risk — confirm by VIN or measure the old one
  • A never-serviced tensioner can be stubborn to break loose
  • A mis-routed or half-seated belt shreds on startup
  • A failing tensioner or pulley bearing will ruin the new belt if you do not check

Tools required

  • 1/2 breaker bar or serpentine belt tool
  • 15mm socket
