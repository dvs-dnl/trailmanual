Budget Trail TJ: Lift, 33s, and Lockers Under $2.5K

Difficulty 3/510–30 hrs$1500–25001997-2002, 2003-2006

A genuinely capable TJ does not cost a fortune. Spend the first $2,500 in the right order — protection, traction, then a modest lift and a rear locker — and you have a Jeep that wheels real trails. The classic mistake is a big lift and shiny bumpers while the rockers stay bare and the rear is open.

The TJ is so capable stock that a budget build is mostly traction and protection, not height. The 93.4-inch wheelbase, solid axles front and rear, and the 4.0L's torque mean a bone-stock TJ on 31s already out-crawls most SUVs; what it lacks is rocker protection, a locker, and enough tire to lift the belly. Here is where the money does the most good, in priority order, with the parts that hit the numbers.

First, the axle ratio. Pull the tag from the differential cover bolt or count driveshaft turns per wheel turn. A 4.0L with 3.73s pulls 33s acceptably; the 3.07s that came in many 4.0L automatics do not, and 33s on 3.07s turn the TJ into a fourth-gear-on-the-freeway slug that hunts between gears with the auto. If you have 3.07s, either stop at 32s or move the regear up the list — it is the one item that breaks the $2.5K ceiling. Second, the rear axle. The Dana 35 is fine for this build as written — 33s, a lunchbox locker, a driver who lifts when a tire hops — but it is the reason the locker in this recipe is a $300 drop-in and not a $900 selectable. Do not put a locker in a Dana 35 that already has a weeping seal or a grooved shaft; fix that first.

Rock sliders before anything else. They protect the rockers and pinch seams — the most expensive body damage a TJ takes on the trail — and they double as a step. Rough Country's TJ rocker guards run $250–300, Smittybilt SRC sliders $300–350, and a set of bolt-on frame-tied sliders from a smaller shop lands at $400–450; frame-tied is worth the extra money if you will ever put a Hi-Lift under them. Add rated recovery points if yours doesn't have them — the factory front tow hooks are rated and fine, but many TJs have lost them, and a pair of Mopar-pattern hooks is $40–60. The rock sliders guide compares the designs. Least glamorous money, best spent.

33x10.50R15 all-terrains plus a rear lunchbox locker is the single biggest capability jump for the dollar. A set of five 33x10.50R15s in a Falken Wildpeak A/T3W, Cooper Discoverer AT3 XLT, or General Grabber ATX runs $1,000–1,250; buy five so the spare matches and the rotation includes it. The factory 15x7 wheels will mount a 10.50 but rub the control arms at full lock — used 15x8 steel or alloy wheels with 3.75-inch backspacing are $150–300 for a set of five on the used market, and 15x8 Cragar-style steelies are $90–110 each new. For the locker, an Aussie Locker or Spartan lunchbox for the 27-spline Dana 35 is $280–330, installs through the cover in an afternoon with a spring-compressing trick and a pair of snap-ring pliers, and turns the open rear into a climbing machine. The rear locker guide covers the install and the on-road manners you accept with an automatic locker. Air down to 12–15 psi on the trail and the difference is night and day.

A 3-inch budget coil lift with matched shocks fits the 33s and keeps the geometry mild enough to skip expensive correction parts. Rough Country's 2.5-inch TJ kit with N3 shocks is $350–450; Rubicon Express's 2.5-inch Super-Ride spring kit is $450–550 with their shocks; a 2-inch spacer "budget boost" with new shocks is $200–300 and clears a 33x10.50 with the fenders trimmed and the bump stops extended. Whatever the height, add a transfer-case drop kit ($30–50) to tame the rear driveline angle at 2–3 inches, extended bump stops ($40–60) so the tire never meets the fender, and longer sway-bar links or disconnects ($60–120). Add an adjustable front track bar ($150–250) if you feel any wander after the lift, and see the budget lift guide for the full parts list. Torque control-arm bolts with the Jeep on its tires, not on stands.

Weekend one: sliders and recovery hooks, plus a full fluid service so you know the axles and transfer case are healthy going in. Weekend two: lift, bump stops, transfer-case drop, sway-bar links, and a driveway alignment check for toe and axle centering. Weekend three: wheels and tires mounted, fender trimming if the 33s touch, then the rear locker through the cover with fresh 80W-90. That order means the Jeep is drivable and protected after every weekend, and the locker goes in last, after you have confirmed the Dana 35 is dry and quiet. Total: $2,100–2,550 with used wheels, $2,400–2,900 with new steelies.

Hold off on regearing, a front locker, and big bumpers until the trail tells you that you need them — the 4.0L pulls 33s acceptably with the factory 3.73s. Skip a winch until you wheel alone; a $60 kinetic rope and a friend's Jeep recover most stuck TJs. Skip the SYE until the transfer-case drop stops working for you, which for most 2.5–3 inch TJs is never. Build protection and traction first; add capability as your wheeling grows into it. Spent in this order, every dollar buys real capability instead of looks: protection keeps a cheap trip from becoming an expensive repair, traction unlocks terrain, and the modest lift only makes room for the tires doing the work.
