Jeep Wrangler TJ / Suspension

TJ Rear Coil Spring Replacement (There Are No Rear Leaf Springs)

Difficulty 2/51.5–3 hrs$120–4001997-2002, 2003-2006Jump to steps
Short answer

No TJ left the factory with rear leaf springs. The 1999 factory service manual describes the TJ rear as a link/coil design: coil springs, upper and lower suspension arms, a track bar, a stabilizer bar and shocks. If you came here for a rear leaf pack, you have a YJ (1987–1995) or a modified truck. If you have a TJ with a sagging or bouncy rear, the job you want is a rear coil swap, and it is one of the friendlier suspension jobs on the truck.

The LJ Unlimited uses the same layout, with trailing arms, a track bar and coil springs at the rear. The TJ's move from the YJ's leaf springs to coils is the reason it articulates better and rides better than the Wrangler before it, and it is why rear lift on a TJ is a coil spring and shock decision rather than a leaf pack and shackle decision.

How the TJ rear is built

Each rear coil sits between the bottom of the frame rail and a pad on the axle, with a rubber isolator at the top and a plastic isolator at the bottom. Two lower arms and two upper arms locate the axle front to back, and the track bar locates it side to side between a frame rail bracket and an axle bracket. The manual notes the springs can go in with either end up.

Because nothing clamps the spring to the axle, the axle only has to drop far enough for the spring to clear its upper seat. That is why the job needs a jack under the axle and the shocks, sway bar links and track bar freed — those are what hold the axle up once the spring is unloaded.

If you are lifting the truck, the springs are one part of a system. Taller springs need longer shocks, may need a track bar bracket or adjustable bar to re-center the axle, and change pinion angle through the arms. See the lift options guide before buying springs for height.

Steps

  1. Raise and support the frame on jack stands. Put a floor jack under the rear axle to support it.
  2. Disconnect the stabilizer bar links and the shock absorbers at the axle brackets.
  3. Disconnect the track bar at the frame rail bracket.
  4. Lower the axle slowly until each spring is free of its upper seat, then lift the springs out.
  5. Check the upper rubber isolators and lower plastic isolators; replace any that are cracked or crushed.
  6. Set the new springs on the axle pad isolators and raise the axle until each spring seats in its upper isolator.
  7. Reconnect the stabilizer bar links and shocks at the axle and the track bar at the frame bracket. You may need to pry the axle sideways to line up the track bar bolt. Leave everything finger tight.
  8. Lower the truck onto its wheels so the suspension sits at ride height, then torque per the factory chart: shock lower nut 74 ft-lb (100 N·m), stabilizer bar link nuts and bolts 40 ft-lb (54 N·m), and track bar 74 ft-lb (100 N·m) at the frame bracket. If you removed the shock upper bolts, they take 23 ft-lb (31 N·m). ExtremeTerrain's TJ torque chart lists the same shock and track bar figures.
  9. If you also loosened the control arms, torque the lower arm nuts to 130 ft-lb (177 N·m) and the upper arm bolts and nuts to 55 ft-lb (75 N·m), again at ride height.
  10. Drive a few miles and recheck the track bar and shock nuts.

Why it works

  • No spring compressor needed; the axle does the work as it drops
  • Springs are interchangeable end for end, so there is no wrong way up
  • Fixes sag, a nose-high stance and a bottoming rear in one afternoon

Trade-offs

  • Taller springs drag in shocks, track bar geometry and pinion angle
  • Old track bar and arm bolts are often seized; budget penetrating oil and time
  • Torquing at droop instead of ride height preloads the bushings and shortens their life

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • second jack or axle support
  • jack stands
  • metric sockets and wrenches
  • breaker bar
  • torque wrench (to 130 ft-lb)
  • pry bar
  • penetrating oil
