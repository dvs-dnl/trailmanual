Jeep Wrangler TJ / Cooling

TJ Radiator Replacement — When to Replace and What to Buy

Difficulty 2/52–4 hrs$90–3201997-2006
Short answer

Replace a TJ radiator when the plastic end tank cracks, the core is clogged or leaking, or a pressure test fails. A replacement unit runs $90 to $320 in parts and takes a careful 2 to 4 hours. A new radiator will not fix overheating that comes from the fan clutch, thermostat, water pump, or a head gasket, so rule those out first.

The factory TJ radiator is an aluminum core with plastic end tanks crimped on. The failure is almost always at the crimp seam of a tank, usually the lower one, after a decade or more of heat cycles. You will see coolant weeping at the tank seam, crusty green or orange residue on the bottom of the radiator, a coolant level that drops with no puddle (it evaporates off a hot engine), or a cap that cannot hold pressure. A radiator that is fully clogged internally shows up differently: the upper hose is hot, the lower hose stays cool, and the temperature gauge climbs on the highway even though nothing leaks.

Confirm the diagnosis before spending money. A cooling-system pressure tester ($35 to $60 to buy, free to borrow from most parts-store loaner programs) pressurizes the system to the cap rating and shows a leak as a falling needle. If the needle holds and the Jeep still runs hot, read TJ coolant flush and check the fan clutch, thermostat, and water pump before buying a radiator.

Which radiator to buy. The radiator is specific to engine (4.0L versus 2.5L), and to whether the Jeep has an automatic transmission, which adds the cooler fittings. Confirm engine, transmission, and model year against the seller's fitment lookup before ordering; the part numbers in this guide are starting points, not guarantees. Three tiers cover most buyers:

  • Direct-fit replacement ($90 to $140). Brands such as OSC and Spectra Premium sell units shaped like the factory part. This is the right choice for a daily driver or a mild trail Jeep. Some replacements have aluminum tanks, some have plastic; ask which before you buy if tank material matters to you.
  • Heavier-core aluminum ($250 to $320). Mishimoto and similar makers sell thicker-core, all-aluminum radiators. Whether the extra core thickness measurably lowers temperatures on a 4.0 depends on airflow behind it, so treat the claim as unproven on your Jeep unless you log your own gauge or scan-tool readings before and after.
  • Used or salvage ($40 to $80). Cheap, and it carries the same age as the radiator you are removing. Only worth it as a stopgap.

What you need

  • Replacement radiator, confirmed for your engine and transmission
  • Coolant of the type your owner's manual specifies for your model year, plus distilled water if you buy concentrate. Roughly 10 to 11 quarts of mixed coolant is typical for a 4.0L TJ; confirm the capacity in your service manual. Do not mix coolant chemistries.
  • Drain pan of at least 3 gallons, clean funnel
  • Screwdrivers, a 10mm socket and extension, pliers or hose-clamp pliers
  • Flare-nut (line) wrench in the correct size for the transmission cooler lines, if the Jeep is an automatic
  • New hose clamps and, if the hoses are original, new upper and lower radiator hoses
  • Cooling-system pressure tester (borrowed or bought)
  • Shop towels, a few zip ties, and gloves

The procedure

  1. Start cold. Let the engine sit for several hours. Never open the radiator cap on a warm engine; scalding coolant sprays out under pressure.
  1. Drain the system. Place the pan under the radiator, open the drain petcock at the bottom of the radiator, and remove the cap to let it flow. Coolant is toxic and tastes sweet to pets, so catch all of it and take it to a recycling point.
  1. Mark and release the hoses. Loosen the clamps on the upper and lower radiator hoses and twist each hose free. A hose that is welded on by age can be sliced lengthwise with a utility knife if you are replacing it anyway; do not pry against the plastic tank neck, because that is how a good new radiator gets cracked.
  1. Automatic transmission only: release the cooler lines. Use the flare-nut wrench so the fittings do not round off. Expect a few ounces of transmission fluid to drain. Plug or cap the lines to keep dirt out and to stop the fluid dripping. Note which line goes to which fitting before you remove them.
  1. Free the overflow hose and the shroud. Unclip the overflow (coolant recovery) hose from the radiator neck. The fan shroud is held by a few fasteners at the top. Remove them and lift the shroud up and set it over the fan or lay it on the engine side, per what your Jeep's clearance allows. Do not pry the fan blades or the viscous fan clutch; both bend with little force.
  1. Remove the radiator. Take out the upper bracket fasteners, check for any remaining clips or the air-conditioning condenser attachment if equipped (the condenser stays connected; support it so it does not hang by its lines), and lift the radiator straight up. Watch the fan blades and the condenser fins on the way out.
  1. Compare the new radiator to the old one before installing. Fitting positions, the drain petcock, the transmission cooler fittings, and the mounting pins should line up. Transfer the lower rubber cushions to the new unit if they are not included, or replace them; a hardened cushion lets the radiator vibrate and cracks tanks.
  1. Install in reverse. Seat the radiator on its lower cushions, secure the upper bracket, reinstall the shroud, reconnect the cooler lines with a drop of clean transmission fluid on the threads, and tighten them firmly without over-torquing. Reconnect the hoses with new clamps and the overflow hose.
  1. Fill and bleed. Close the petcock. Fill with the 50/50 mix slowly at the radiator neck. Start the engine with the cap off, set the heater to full hot and the blower on low, and let the thermostat open. Top up as air escapes; the level will drop sharply when the thermostat opens. Replace the cap once the level holds and no more bubbles rise.
  1. Pressure test. With the cap on, pressurize the system to the rating stamped on the cap and watch for a falling needle for 10 to 15 minutes, then inspect every joint you touched.
  1. Automatic only: check the transmission fluid at operating temperature and top up to the dipstick mark. See TJ differential fluid for the habit of checking fluids together after a big service.
  1. Recheck after the first drive. Let the engine cool completely, then check the coolant level and top up the recovery bottle.

What to watch for

  • Overheating that returns. Weigh the fan clutch and thermostat next. With the engine off and cold, a viscous fan clutch should have noticeable drag when you spin the fan by hand; a fan that spins freely for several turns, or one that is locked solid, is worn out. Do not use a screwdriver or wrench near the fan with the engine running.
  • Old hoses. A 20-year-old hose that has swelled or gone soft is the usual cause of a second failure two weeks after the radiator job. Replace the radiator hoses and clamps while the system is open, and look at the heater hoses and the small bypass hoses at the thermostat and throttle body.
  • A cap that cannot hold pressure. A $10 radiator cap with a weak spring boils the Jeep over at lower temperatures and makes a new radiator look defective. Replace the cap with the correct rating whenever you replace the radiator.
  • Debris and bent fins. Clean bugs and mud from the condenser and radiator face with low-pressure water. Bent fins from winch cable or a stray rock reduce airflow; straighten them gently with a fin comb.
  • Stray fluids. Dispose of the old coolant and any transmission fluid properly. Never leave an open pan under a Jeep where a dog or child can reach it.
  • Aftermarket front bumpers and winches can block airflow to the radiator. If you add one, see TJ front bumper guide and leave an open path for air.

Costs and sources. Direct-fit radiator: $90 to $140. Heavier-core aluminum radiator: $250 to $320. Coolant: $22 to $30 per gallon premixed, two gallons needed to refill a drained 4.0 after flushing. Hoses: $25 to $60 for the pair. Cap: $8 to $15. Pressure tester: $35 to $60 to buy. A shop typically charges $350 to $600 for the same job including the radiator, so DIY saves roughly $250 to $400 if nothing complicates it. Order the radiator after confirming fitment for your engine, transmission, and model year; part numbers listed on this page are examples to search from. Pair the work with TJ serpentine belt replacement since the belt is exposed once the shroud is out.

Tools required

  • drain pan
  • Phillips screwdriver
  • 10mm socket
  • pliers
  • coolant funnel
  • flare-nut wrench (automatic)
  • cooling-system pressure tester
