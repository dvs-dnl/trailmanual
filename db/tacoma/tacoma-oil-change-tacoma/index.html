Oil Change — 1GR-FE 4.0L and 2GR-FKS 3.5L

Difficulty 1/50.5–1 hrs$45–902005-2015, 2016-2023

2nd gen Tacoma (1GR-FE 4.0L) holds 5.5 quarts. 3rd gen (2GR-FKS 3.5L) holds 6.2 quarts of 0W-20 full synthetic. Both use the same cartridge filter kit, 04152-YZZA1. Drain plug 30 ft-lb, filter housing cap 18 ft-lb — and the small drain-valve O-ring in that kit is the single most-forgotten part on the whole job.

Both Tacoma V6 engines share one oil change procedure across two generations, and both are 300,000-mile engines when they get clean oil on schedule. What changed in 2016 was the viscosity: the 2nd gen 4.0L 1GR-FE ran 5W-30 through the early years, and the 3rd gen 3.5L 2GR-FKS requires 0W-20 full synthetic.

The 3rd gen answer is unambiguous: 0W-20 full synthetic, 6.2 quarts. The 2GR-FKS runs dual injection with tight bearing clearances and depends on 0W-20 for fast oil pressure at cold start. Toyota's hot-climate guidance does allow 5W-30 in sustained high ambient temperatures, and Phoenix, Tucson, and Vegas owners who run 5W-30 synthetic in summer are inside that allowance — it also quiets timing chain noise on some trucks. The tradeoff is a small hit to measured fuel economy and, on a truck still under powertrain warranty, a paperwork argument you may not want.

The 2nd gen is where people get tripped up. Early 4.0L trucks (roughly 2005–2009) call for 5W-30. Toyota later moved the 1GR-FE recommendation toward 0W-20 in the second half of the generation. Both are approved fills for the engines that list them. Do not guess from a forum post: read the oil fill cap and the maintenance section of your own owner's manual, because the answer changed mid-generation and the parts counter will hand you whatever the computer says.

Capacity is 5.5 quarts on the 4.0L and 6.2 on the 3.5L, both with a filter change. Buy a 6-quart jug either way and keep the remainder for top-offs.

The filter housing lives on the bottom of the engine with a built-in drain valve, the same arrangement as the 4Runner. The Toyota kit (04152-YZZA1) contains three things: the cartridge, a large O-ring for the housing cover, and a small O-ring for the drain valve plug. Open the box and confirm all three are in there before you start.

That small O-ring is the failure point. It seals the plug you thread the drain tool into, and because it is tiny and gets set aside on the bench, it routinely goes back in old and hard. The result is a slow drip that shows up as a spot on the driveway a week later and gets blamed on the rear main seal. Replace it every time.

Use a steel 64mm 14-flute cap socket. The aluminum versions round their teeth on a housing that has been overtightened by a quick-lube shop, and once the flutes are rounded you are cutting the cap off. Torque the cover to 18 ft-lb and stop there — the housing is plastic, and cracking one costs $40–60 and a tow-free but annoying afternoon.

The plastic drain tool included in the Toyota kit threads into the housing bottom and routes oil down into your pan instead of spraying it across the crossmember. Use it. Without it the job is genuinely messy.

Access: both generations have a cutout in the factory front skid that lines up with the drain plug and filter housing, so you don't have to drop the skid. Plate-style aftermarket skids from RCI, CBI, and Tactica keep that access port. Some budget skids do not, which turns a 30-minute job into an hour with eight bolts. Check for the port before you buy a skid plate set.

Toyota's normal-service interval on 0W-20 synthetic is 10,000 miles or 12 months. The severe-service interval is 5,000 miles, and severe service is defined by conditions almost every Tacoma on this site actually sees: dusty roads, sustained low-speed off-road operation, towing, extended idling, and repeated short trips. If you wheel the truck or tow with it, run 5,000. If it commutes on pavement, 10,000 is defensible on a quality synthetic.

The honest way to settle the argument for your specific truck is a used-oil analysis. Blackstone Laboratories and similar run about $35–40 a sample and report wear metals, fuel dilution, and remaining additive package. Two samples tell you whether your interval is conservative or optimistic, which is worth more than any forum consensus. Fuel dilution is the number to watch on the direct-injected 2GR-FKS, particularly on trucks that idle a lot or do short winter trips.

One thing frequent oil changes will not fix: intake valve carbon on the 3rd gen. Dual injection (port plus direct) means the 2GR-FKS washes its valves better than a pure-DI engine, but it is not immune, and the fix for carbon is walnut blasting, not a shorter oil interval.

DIY: 6 quarts of full synthetic runs $30–45, the Toyota filter kit $8–12, a crush washer $1. Call it $45–58 per change. One-time tooling — a steel 64mm cap socket ($14–18), a drain pan ($15), and a torque wrench you should own regardless — pays for itself in about two changes.

A quick-lube shop charges $75–110 on synthetic and is where the overtightened housing caps and stripped drain plugs come from. A dealer runs $90–130 and does it correctly. If you never turn another bolt on this truck, this is still the job worth doing yourself: it costs half as much, and it puts you under the truck every few months looking at the frame, the skids, and the diff plugs, which is how problems get caught while they're cheap.

Steps

  1. Warm the engine 2–3 minutes so the oil flows, then shut it off. Do not do this on a fully hot engine — the oil will burn you.
  2. Lift the truck on ramps or jack stands on level ground. Level matters for the dipstick reading at the end.
  3. Confirm the stock skid plate access port lines up with the drain plug and filter housing.
  4. Position the drain pan under the 14mm drain plug. Remove the plug and drain a full 5+ minutes.
  5. Thread the small plastic drain tool from the filter kit into the bottom of the filter housing so the housing drains into the pan.
  6. Use the steel 64mm 14-flute cap socket to remove the housing cover. Lift out the old cartridge.
  7. Wipe the housing clean. Replace BOTH O-rings — large one on the cover, small one on the drain valve plug. Lightly oil both before seating.
  8. Snap the new cartridge into the cover, reinstall, and torque to 18 ft-lb. Stop at 18; the housing is plastic.
  9. Remove the drain tool. Install the drain valve plug with its new O-ring, snug only.
  10. Install the drain plug with a new crush washer. Torque to 30 ft-lb.
  11. Add 5.5 qt (2nd gen 4.0L) or 6.2 qt (3rd gen 3.5L) of the correct viscosity through the valve cover fill.
  12. Start the engine. Confirm the oil pressure light goes out within about 3 seconds. Idle 30 seconds and look underneath for drips at the drain plug, the cover, and the drain valve plug.
  13. Shut off, wait 5 full minutes for drainback, then check the dipstick and top off to the upper mark.
  14. Reset the maintenance light and log the mileage.

Why it works

  • Cartridge filter plus the included drain tool makes it a clean job
  • Factory skid plate has an access port on both generations, so nothing has to come off
  • Timing chain rather than a belt on both engines — no 90k service looming
  • 1GR-FE and 2GR-FKS both run past 300,000 miles on schedule maintenance
  • $45–58 DIY against $90–130 at a dealer, and you inspect the underside every time

Trade-offs

  • Viscosity changed mid-2nd-gen and again at the 3rd gen — read your own cap and manual rather than assuming
  • The small drain-valve O-ring is forgotten constantly and produces a slow leak blamed on the rear main
  • Plastic housing cap cracks if torqued past 18 ft-lb, and aluminum cap sockets round off an overtightened one
  • Direct-injection carbon on the 3rd gen is unaffected by oil interval — that's a walnut-blast job
  • Some budget aftermarket skid plates delete the factory access port

Tools required

  • 14mm socket
  • 64mm 14-flute oil filter cap socket
  • drain pan
  • torque wrench
  • ramps or jack stands
  • funnel
