Auxiliary Light Pods and Bar

Difficulty 2/51–4 hrs$120–9002005-2015, 2016-2023

The companion wiring guide covers how to power aux lights safely; this card is the hardware decision. A Tacoma's factory headlights leave the trail edges and the corners dark, and the right pods or bar fix that — but only if you match the beam pattern to how you wheel, size the circuit for the load, and mount it where it earns its place.

A pair of wide-flood or diffused pods on the bumper or A-pillars lights the immediate trail and the area beside the truck — best for slow crawling where you want to see your lines. A spot or driving bar throws far down a graded road or a fast desert two-track. Many Tacoma builds run both: ditch-light pods on flood for the trail and a bumper bar or driving pair for distance, each on its own circuit.

Ditch lights and small pods: Diode Dynamics SS3 pairs run $250-400 depending on Sport/Pro output, and the SS3 fog versions are SAE-compliant — the only entries here legal to run on the street; Baja Designs Squadron Sport pairs land around $220-270 with the S2 slightly below; Rigid D-Series pairs $250-330. Bars: a quality 20-inch single-row (the size the CBI Baja bumper carries internally) runs $180-400; skip the $60 no-name bar whose reflectors spray glare everywhere and whose housing fills with water the first winter. Brackets: Cali Raised or Rago hood-hinge ditch brackets are $60-100 and bolt to existing hardware, no drilling. Amber lens covers ($15-30) are cheap insurance for dust and snow, where white light bounces back into your eyes.

LEDs are efficient, not free. A pair of 40W pods draws about 6A at 13.8V; a 20-inch bar is typically 90-105W, call it 7-8A; a big 42-inch double-row can pull 240W and 17A. Size each circuit at 125% of continuous load: 14 AWG wire and a 10A fuse cover a pod pair, a big bar wants 12 AWG and a 15-20A fuse, and everything switches through a 30-40A relay so the dash switch carries milliamps, not the load. Never tap the headlight circuit — the current belongs on its own fused line from the battery, exactly as the aux wiring guide lays out. Total it up, too: lights plus a fridge plus an air compressor is when the dual-battery question stops being hypothetical.

One or two circuits: a $40-60 relay harness per circuit and factory-look dash switches in the Tacoma's blank plates do it cleanly. At three or more circuits the wiring behind the dash becomes the failure point, and a switch system — Switch-Pros SP9100 (~$560) or sPOD BantamX (~$500-600) — replaces the relay pile with one sealed module, programmable circuits, and a single power feed. Expensive, but it is the difference between an engine bay you can troubleshoot at night and a nest of butt connectors. Buy it once, at circuit three, rather than rewiring at circuit five.

Bumper-integrated mounts keep the light low and protected. Ditch-light brackets at the hood corners light the trail edges without adding a roofline that whistles and casts hood glare. A-pillar pods aim into corners. Avoid a high roof bar for trail use — it lights the dust and reflects off the hood; save the roofline for open desert running.

Choose the optic for the task, not the lumen headline: flood for near work, spot for distance, a combo for both — and treat lumen claims from budget brands as fiction, since a genuine 4,000-lumen pod outperforms a claimed 12,000-lumen no-name. Aim every light on a wall before the first night run so you are not blinding yourself off the hood or oncoming traffic. Keep any forward bar covered and off on the street: except for SAE-marked fog optics, aux lighting is off-road only, and uncovered bars on pavement draw tickets and complaints.

Pick pods for the trail, a bar or driving pair for distance, and wire each on its own fused relay circuit sized at 125% of load. Mount low and protected, aim before you rely on it, go to a switch system at circuit three, and keep it legal on the street. That is how aux lighting actually improves night wheeling instead of only looking the part.
