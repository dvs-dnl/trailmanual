Dual Battery Setup for JL Overland Builds

Difficulty 3/53–6 hrs$400–12002018-2024
Short answer

A dual battery setup on the JL requires an isolator or DC-DC charger — the JL's smart alternator does not charge a secondary battery reliably without one. Budget $400–$600 for a capable system; expect $800–$1,200 fully built out with lithium.

# Dual Battery Setup for JL Overland Builds

The JL Wrangler's factory alternator uses variable voltage regulation — it doesn't hold 14.4V constantly the way older alternators did. That matters because a straightforward battery isolator solenoid relies on voltage differential to charge the aux battery, and the JL's alternator may not produce enough differential to trigger it reliably. If you use a traditional isolator on a JL, you may find your aux battery never fully charges.

The correct solution is a DC-DC charger (also called a battery-to-battery charger or B2B charger). It actively pulls from the start battery and charges the aux battery at a proper charge profile, regardless of the alternator's voltage behavior. Victron, Redarc, and Sterling make well-regarded units.

  • DC-DC charger: Victron Orion-Tr Smart 12/12-30A (~$180) or Redarc BCDC1240D (~$280)
  • Auxiliary battery: AGM or LiFePO4 — see sizing notes below
  • Battery box or tray (for aux battery location)
  • 4 AWG or 2 AWG wiring (length depends on routing)
  • Inline fuse holders (ANL or MIDI fuses at each battery)
  • Ring terminals and heat-shrink butt connectors
  • Bus bars (positive and negative) for distribution
  • Battery disconnect switch (optional but recommended)

Sizing the auxiliary battery:

  • Overlanding (fridge, lighting, USB charging): 100Ah AGM sufficient; 50Ah LiFePO4 equivalent
  • Winching-capable: 200Ah AGM minimum; 100Ah LiFePO4 minimum
  • LiFePO4 is half the weight of AGM for equal usable capacity — worth the premium ($300–$600 vs $120–$200 for AGM at 100Ah)

Choose your aux battery location

The JL hood/engine bay has limited space. Most builds mount the aux battery:

  • Rear cargo area (most common for overlanders): requires longer wiring runs but unlimited battery size options
  • Under-hood in the passenger-side void (common for winch-assist setups): tighter clearance, limited to ~35 group size

Wire the DC-DC charger

  1. Mount the DC-DC charger in a dry, ventilated location — near the engine bay fuse box or on the firewall.
  2. Run a fused positive lead from the start battery to the DC-DC charger input. Fuse within 18" of the start battery — use a 40A ANL fuse for a 30A charger.
  3. Run a fused positive lead from the DC-DC charger output to the aux battery. Fuse within 18" of the aux battery — same 40A ANL.
  4. Run a chassis ground from the charger to a clean body ground point (not the battery terminal — ground the charger chassis to the frame directly).

Wire the aux battery for load distribution

  1. Mount a positive bus bar and negative bus bar near the aux battery.
  2. Run all aux loads (fridge, lights, inverter, USB ports) through the bus bars — not directly to the battery terminals.
  3. Add a fuse for each load circuit at the bus bar. Size each fuse to the wire gauge and load: a 10A circuit gets a 15A fuse, not a 60A.
  4. Grounding: run a dedicated negative cable from the aux battery negative terminal to a clean chassis ground point — do not share the start battery's ground path.

Verify the system

  1. Measure voltage at the aux battery with the vehicle off: should read 12.6–13.0V (full AGM) or 13.2–13.4V (full LiFePO4).
  2. Start the vehicle. Within 30 seconds, the DC-DC charger should activate. Measure aux battery voltage — it should begin climbing toward 14.4–14.6V.
  3. Monitor with a battery monitor (Victron BMV-712 is the standard) to track state of charge and confirm the system is working.
  • Wire gauge matters: Undersized wire is a fire hazard. Use a wire gauge calculator — 4 AWG handles 30A at 15-foot runs; longer runs need 2 AWG.
  • Fuse every positive lead within 18" of its source battery: This is not optional. An unfused wire that shorts is a vehicle fire.
  • LiFePO4 compatibility: Lithium batteries require a charger with a lithium charge profile. Most modern DC-DC chargers (Victron Orion-Tr Smart, Redarc BCDC) support LiFePO4. Verify before buying.
  • JL 2.0T eTorque note: 2.0T eTorque models have a 48V mild hybrid system with a belt-starter generator. The 12V system behavior is different. Consult Redarc or Victron's JL eTorque notes before selecting a charger.
  • Redarc BCDC1240D (40A, more capable): ~$280 from retailers
  • 100Ah AGM aux battery (Renogy or VMAXTANKS): $120–$180
  • 100Ah LiFePO4 (Ampere Time, Battle Born): $280–$450
  • 4 AWG wiring kit with lugs and fuses: $30–$60
  • Victron BMV-712 battery monitor: ~$120

A complete entry-level AGM system (DC-DC charger + 100Ah AGM + wiring kit) runs $400–$500 installed yourself. A full LiFePO4 overlanding setup with monitoring runs $800–$1,200.

  • socket set
  • wire stripper
  • crimper
  • heat gun
  • multimeter
  • drill
