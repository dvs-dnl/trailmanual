Jeep Wrangler JL / Armor & Skids

Rock Sliders and Rocker Guards

Difficulty 3/53–5 hrs$399–9502018-2024
Short answer

The rocker panels on the JL Wrangler are exposed — no factory protection — and a single high-centering on a rock shelf can fold sheet metal that costs $600+ to repair. A slider is the single cheapest per-dollar piece of trail insurance available for this platform.

The JL's unibody construction changes how sliders attach compared to the body-on-frame XJ. Sliders for the JL bolt through the body and into the integrated frame rails in the sill area — a robust mounting approach when done correctly, but one that requires precise fit and correct torque specs. Do not attempt to run a JK slider on a JL; the mounting points are different and a slider that's not properly attached to the structure will transfer impact forces to the wrong part of the body.

Poison Spyder's Rocker Knockers are the premium option — heavy-gauge DOM tube construction with a textured powdercoat finish and a step tube design that doubles as a rock step. The $699 price reflects the material quality, but these are genuinely trail-proven hardware. If your build sees regular technical terrain, the Poison Spyder units are worth the premium.

Rock Hard 4x4 is the value-focused choice that doesn't compromise on structural integrity. At $449, the Patriot Series sliders are 3/16" plate with 1.75" DOM tube construction — heavy enough to protect the rockers without the price of premium brands. These are a popular choice for trail builds on a budget. LOD's Destroyer series sits in the middle — $649 for notably thick plate construction and a relatively minimal profile that doesn't add much departure-angle penalty. The Smittybilt XRC sliders at $499 are the budget-ceiling option: adequate construction, lighter gauge than the Rock Hard or LOD, but a functional choice for mild-to-moderate trail use.

JL vs JLU fitment: The 2-door JL and 4-door JLU have different wheelbase and rocker lengths. Every manufacturer makes separate part numbers for JL and JLU — verify before ordering. Running a JL-spec slider on a JLU will leave rear coverage gaps; running a JLU slider on a JL won't fit at all.

Steps

  1. Chock wheels, lift vehicle on jack stands at the frame rails with enough clearance to work under the rocker area.
  2. Clean the mounting surfaces — factory undercoating and debris on the bolt hole areas will affect seat quality.
  3. Dry-fit both sliders before drilling any pilot holes or running any hardware. The fit should align with the body before torquing.
  4. For bolt-through installations: drill pilot holes per the manufacturer's template if provided. Use sharp bits — the factory sill material is hard.
  5. Install mounting hardware from the inside of the vehicle where required. Some slider designs require a helper inside to hold inner-body hardware while you torque from below.
  6. Start all hardware finger-tight before torquing anything.
  7. Use ratchet straps or a helper to hold the slider in alignment during initial torquing — they're heavy and will shift if unsupported.
  8. Torque all fasteners to manufacturer spec. For most JL sliders, this is 35–45 ft-lb on the main mounting points.
  9. Articulate the doors to confirm clearance — sliders close to the door bottoms need a clearance check before driving.

Why it works

  • Protects the most vulnerable body panel on a trail-going JL — rocker damage is expensive and common
  • Most slider designs double as a step tube, which is a genuine quality-of-life upgrade on a lifted JL
  • Rock Hard and Smittybilt options deliver real protection at accessible price points
  • Bolt-on installation (no welding required on any of these options) means a home builder can complete the job
  • Adds structural rigidity to the sill area — measurable improvement on flex-intensive trail use

Trade-offs

  • JL and JLU require separate part numbers — wrong fitment will not work
  • Heavy hardware (most sliders add 40–70 lbs) raises the CG slightly and increases unsprung weight
  • Slider profile affects departure angle — wider, lower-profile sliders sacrifice some departure clearance
  • Powder coat finish scratches quickly on rocky terrain — expect trail wear; that's what they're for
  • Smittybilt XRC gauge is lighter than Rock Hard or Poison Spyder; adequate for mild-moderate use but not the choice for serious rock work

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • socket set
  • torque wrench
  • drill
  • drill bits
  • ratchet straps (for alignment during install)
