Tie Rod and Drag Link Replacement — Power Wagon

Difficulty 3/51.5–4 hrs$150–6002014-2018, 2019-2024, 2025+

Worn tie rod and drag link ends cause loose, wandering steering and uneven tire wear, and on a heavy solid-axle truck they are a safety item — a failed end means a loss of steering. The factory linkage is adequate for stock use; trucks that wheel hard or run larger tires bend or wear the stock parts and benefit from forged heavy-duty replacements. Budget $150-$600 depending on parts grade, and always finish with a professional alignment. You can set toe well enough to drive, but the truck needs a proper alignment to wear tires evenly.

The Power Wagon steers its solid front axle through a tie rod (connecting the two wheels) and a drag link (connecting the steering box to the passenger-side knuckle, in the typical HD layout). Both use tapered ball-stud ends that wear with miles and trail abuse. Replacing worn ends restores tight, predictable steering and stops the inner-and-outer edge tire wear that loose linkage causes.

Loose, vague on-center steering, a clunk when you transition direction, and feathered or edge-worn front tires all point at the linkage. With the front lifted and the wheels off the ground, grab each front tire at 9-and-3 and push-pull: play there with the steering locked indicates a tie rod end. Grab at 12-and-6 to separate that from a wheel bearing or ball joint. Have a helper rock the steering wheel while you watch the drag link ends for any lash. Any torn boot on a ball-stud end means grit has gotten in and the joint is on borrowed time.

Soak the castle-nut threads and the tapered studs in penetrating oil. Remove the cotter pin and castle nut, then separate the taper — a tie-rod separator or ball-joint press is the clean way; a pickle fork works but tears the boot, so only fork a part you are replacing. Transfer the measurements: count the threads or measure the old assembly length so the new tie rod goes back at roughly the same length, preserving toe well enough to drive. Torque the studs to the service-manual values and install new cotter pins — a steering fastener without its cotter pin is a failure waiting to happen. Heavy-duty forged tie rods and drag links from the off-road makers resist bending on larger tires and use rebuildable, greasable ends.

After replacement, set toe close with a tape measure (front-of-tire vs rear-of-tire distance) so the truck tracks straight and is safe to drive, then get a professional alignment to set toe, and check caster, precisely — correct caster is what gives a solid-axle truck its on-center stability and resistance to shimmy. Re-torque and re-inspect after the first few hundred miles. Treat any steering looseness or a torn boot as a near-term safety repair, not a someday item.

On a heavy solid-axle truck, worn steering linkage is one of the ingredients in death wobble — the violent, self-feeding front-end shimmy a bump can trigger at speed. Worn tie-rod or drag-link ends add the free play that lets the front tires shake, and once it starts the only safe response is to slow down firmly and pull over; it usually calms below about 45 mph. Linkage is rarely the sole cause — a loose track bar, worn ball joints, insufficient caster, or an out-of-balance tire usually share the blame — so treat a shimmy as a whole-front-end inspection, not a single-part swap. Check the steering gear/box for lash and review how the front geometry moves through travel in the Articulink front-end explainer as part of the same look, and verify tire balance before you condemn the linkage.

For a stock-tire truck that simply wore out its ends, Mopar or Moog replacement parts restore it for $150-250 and are perfectly adequate. Once you run larger, heavier tires or wheel hard, the factory tie rod can bend in a hard hit — this is where forged HD linkage from Synergy, Carli, or Apex ($250-350 for the tie rod, similar for the drag link) earns its keep: thicker forged bar, larger tapered studs, and rebuildable, greasable ends you service instead of replace. Match this work to the rest of the front end — if the front brakes are due or you're chasing a wander that traces back to tire condition in the tire fitment guide, doing them together saves a second alignment. Whatever grade you buy, torque every stud to spec and fit new cotter pins — the single most important non-negotiable on any steering fastener.

Why it works

  • Restores tight, predictable steering and stops edge tire wear
  • HD forged linkage resists bending on larger tires and hard trail use
  • Greasable rebuildable ends last far longer than sealed factory parts

Trade-offs

  • A failed steering end is a genuine safety hazard — do not defer it
  • Separating tapers cleanly needs the right tool to avoid wrecking good boots
  • A home toe-set gets you driving, but a real alignment is non-negotiable

Tools required

  • floor jack + rated stands
  • tie-rod / pickle-fork separator or ball-joint press
  • metric + large wrenches
  • torque wrench
  • tape measure (for toe)
  • penetrating oil
