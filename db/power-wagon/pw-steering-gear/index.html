Steering Gear Box and Track Bar Service — Power Wagon

Difficulty 3/51–4 hrs$60–7002014-2018, 2019-2024, 2025+

Steering wander, a clunk over bumps, or the dreaded front-end shimmy on a solid-axle Power Wagon almost always starts at the track bar, not the steering box — diagnose in that order. The track bar locates the front axle side-to-side, and its bushings and ball-stud wear with off-road use, letting the axle move under the truck. The steering gear box itself can develop sector-shaft play at higher mileage. This is safety-critical: a worn track bar is a primary trigger for the shimmy that solid-axle trucks are prone to. Inspect before every trail trip.

The Power Wagon's solid front axle gives it the articulation and durability that make it the truck it is, but a solid axle steers through a track bar, drag link, and recirculating-ball gear box that all wear. Loose steering is not something to live with — on a heavy truck at speed it is a handling and safety problem. The good news is that the diagnosis follows a clear order and most fixes are bolt-on.

With the truck on the ground and a helper rocking the steering wheel back and forth, watch the front end: motion at the track bar ends (axle-side bracket or frame-side mount) before the wheels turn means a worn track bar bushing or ball-stud — the most common cause of wander and shimmy. Next watch the drag link and tie rod ends for play. Only after those check out do you look at the steering box: with the wheels straight, feel for slop in the pitman arm / sector shaft as the wheel is rocked — excess sector-shaft play points to a worn or loose gear box. Also confirm the steering-box mounting bolts are tight; a loose box mimics internal wear and can crack the frame mount over time.

A worn track bar is replaced as a unit (the OEM bushings are not separately serviceable on most). Heavy-duty aftermarket bars from the off-road suspension makers use a rebuildable ball-stud and greasable bushings that hold up far better under trail use. Replace with the suspension at ride height, torque the frame-side and axle-side fasteners to the service-manual values, and re-check after a few hundred miles — track bar fasteners can settle. A correctly fitted, tight track bar is the single biggest improvement to a wandering Power Wagon.

If the box has genuine sector-shaft play, fit a remanufactured or upgraded gear box. On lifted or hard-wheeled trucks a steering-box brace (sector-shaft support) ties the bottom of the sector shaft to the frame, cutting flex and protecting the box and frame mount — a worthwhile add for any built Power Wagon. Bleed and top the power-steering fluid after any box work, and verify there is no leak at the sector-shaft seal. Treat any return of shimmy as a stop-and-inspect event, not something to drive through.

Any front lift steepens the track bar's operating angle, and that has two consequences worth checking on a leveled or lifted Power Wagon. First, the axle shifts toward the driver side at the new ride height — a properly set adjustable track bar re-centers it (measure hub-to-frame on both sides). Second, a steeper bar converts more of every bump into lateral load at the bushings and ball-stud, so the same parts wear faster than they did at stock height. If the truck got a budget leveling kit or a mid-height lift and the steering has never felt as tight since, inspect the track bar first and consider the heavy-duty adjustable bar the corrective, not the upgrade. The Articulink front-end explainer covers how the link-coil front end locates the axle and why the track bar takes the brunt.

Diagnosis is free and takes fifteen minutes with a helper — do it before buying anything. A heavy-duty track bar runs $250–500 in parts and about an hour of labor (one frame-side bolt, one axle-side bolt, torque at ride height); a shop will charge 1–1.5 hours. A remanufactured or upgraded steering box is $350–700 in parts plus 2–3 hours of labor including the pitman-arm pull and fluid bleed; add an alignment and steering-wheel re-center after either job. The sector-shaft brace is $150 and under an hour with hand tools. Budget the full-front-end inspection into every one of these jobs — the tie rod and drag link guide covers the joints that commonly fail alongside the track bar, and replacing one worn joint while leaving its neighbors is how a truck ends up back on the lift in six months.

Why it works

  • A tight track bar cures most wander and shimmy on a solid-axle truck
  • The diagnosis follows a clear order — no parts-cannon needed
  • A steering brace protects the box and frame mount on built trucks

Trade-offs

  • A worn track bar is a real safety item, not a comfort complaint
  • Steering-box replacement needs a pitman-arm puller and high-torque tools
  • Shimmy can have stacked causes; verify every joint, not the first one found

Tools required

  • floor jack + rated stands
  • pry bar (for play check)
  • metric sockets incl. large (21mm/36mm)
  • torque wrench (high range)
  • pitman arm puller (if removing box)
  • power-steering fluid
