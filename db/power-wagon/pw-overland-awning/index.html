270° Awning for Power Wagon (ARB Touring / 23ZERO Peregrine)

Difficulty 2/52–5 hrs$400–11002014-2018, 2019-2024, 2025+
Short answer

An awning is one of the highest-ROI overland upgrades for the Power Wagon. Mount it to a bed rack or to the cab roof rack and you've got shade or rain cover at camp in 60 seconds. ARB Touring is the value reference. 23ZERO Peregrine 270° is the premium pick — wraps around the back of the truck so you've got shade three sides of the rig.

A Power Wagon-mounted awning needs a place to attach. Three common locations:

  1. Bed-rack side — most common. A bed rack (Front Runner, Decked CargoGlide, or a custom welded rack) with side awning channels. This is the standard pickup-truck overland setup.
  2. Cab roof rack — if you're running a cab-mount rack for lights/RTT, an awning mounts to its side rail. Less common because most PW owners want bed space free.
  3. Bed rail direct-mount — some kits mount to factory bed rails with brackets. Lower mounting height (less head room under the awning) but no rack required.

ARB Touring 2500mm ($520) — the value reference. 8'2" straight awning, deploys in 60 seconds, supports rain or sun. Includes ground poles. Mounts to any standard awning channel. Proven 10+ year design.

23ZERO Peregrine 270° ($950) — the premium pick. Wraps around the rear corner of the truck, covering driver side + rear + chunk of passenger side. Gives you shade across the back of the bed for cooking and seating. Double the price of straight awning but covers ~2x the area.

Roam Adventure Co. Rambler ($380) — budget straight awning. Same idea as ARB Touring, slightly less robust fabric, perfectly fine for weekend camping.

Mounting bracket cost is on top of awning cost. Most awnings mount to standard 80/20-style channel rails, which most overland racks include. If your rack doesn't have channels, plan $50-100 for bracket adapters.

For Power Wagon-specific consideration: bed-rack-mounted awnings put weight on the rack at the highest point. A 270° awning is 50-65 lb. Make sure your bed rack is rated for that plus whatever else (RTT, gear baskets) you're putting up there. The Power Wagon's high CG with a loaded bed rack is the trade-off you accept for the convenience.

Wind: all awnings tear in sustained 25+ mph wind. Take them down before sleeping if weather's coming in.

The awning itself deploys in a minute; the install is a one-time job that decides whether it stays solid for years.

  1. Confirm your mount surface. Most awnings clamp to 80/20-style channel on a bed rack or roof rack. If your rack has no channel, order the bracket adapters first — they are $50-100 and you do not want to discover the gap on assembly day.
  2. Set the height. On a bed rack, mount the awning so its arms clear the bed rail when folded and give standing headroom when deployed. Too low and you duck under it all weekend; too high and short arms will not reach the ground.
  3. Bolt the channel brackets to the rack with the awning held in place by a second person — these are awkward and heavy enough that a one-person install ends in scratched paint or a dropped awning.
  4. Torque the bracket bolts and add thread-locker. The awning lives on the highest, most vibration-exposed point on the truck; loose brackets are the number-one failure.
  5. Deploy it once at home, stake the legs, and confirm the fabric tensions flat with no sag. Adjust arm angle before you rely on it at camp.

Weigh the whole rack load before you commit: a 270° awning is 50-65 lb on top of any pw-rooftop-tent and gear baskets, all riding high on a truck that already has a tall center of gravity.

Wind is what destroys awnings — any of them will tear in sustained 25+ mph gusts, so take it down before you sleep if weather is moving in, no matter how well it is staked. The other watch item is cumulative rack weight and center of gravity: the Power Wagon already sits tall, and stacking an awning, a tent, and baskets at the top of the rack changes how the truck leans on off-camber trail, so respect your rack's rated load and keep the heaviest items low. Cheap awnings save money up front and then shred a fabric panel in the first real storm, so for anything beyond occasional fair-weather use the mid-tier fabric pays for itself.

Why it works

  • Shade and rain cover at camp in 60 seconds
  • 270° awnings cover three sides of the truck — game-changing for cooking
  • Bolts to any standard overland rack
  • High-ROI upgrade (cheap relative to a tent, big quality-of-life gain)

Trade-offs

  • Adds 30-65 lb to the highest point of the rack
  • 270° awnings double the cost of straight
  • Cheap awnings tear quickly in wind
  • Mounting depends on having a bed rack or roof rack already

Tools required

  • metric sockets
  • torque wrench
  • drill (if mounting brackets need new holes)
