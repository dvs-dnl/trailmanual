Ram Power Wagon / Suspension

Articulink Front Suspension: What It Does, What Wears Out, and When to Inspect It

Difficulty 2/50.5–2.0 hrs$0–18002014-2018, 2019-present
Short answer

Articulink is Ram's name for the high-movement pivot joints at the axle end of the Power Wagon's front control arms, introduced with the 2014 truck. They allow the front axle to move through far more travel than a bushing-only setup, and they are a wear item. Any perceptible lateral play at the axle end of a control arm means service now, not later — worn joints degrade steering precision, accelerate tire wear, and on a lifted truck they are a primary contributor to death wobble.

The Power Wagon's front end is a link/coil solid axle: an AAM 9.25" axle on coil springs, located by upper and lower control arms and a track bar. On a conventional truck, every one of those arms pivots on rubber or polyurethane bushings at both ends. Bushings flex to permit axle movement, but flex has a hard limit — past a certain angle the rubber stops deforming and the axle stops moving, which is what pins an inside front wheel in the air on a step-over.

Articulink replaces the axle-end pivot with a ball-and-socket-style joint that rotates on multiple axes rather than relying on material deformation. The frame-end bushings stay conventional. The result is that the axle can twist and articulate through a much wider range before anything binds.

Ram markets this as a substantial articulation gain over the pre-2014 Power Wagon; treat any specific percentage figure as a marketing number rather than a measured one. What is not in dispute is the trail behavior — the inside front tire stays loaded longer on side-hills and step-overs, which is exactly where a heavy truck loses traction.

The electronic sway bar disconnect is the other half of the system. Below 18 mph in 4WD the front bar releases, and with the bar out of the picture the Articulink joints carry the full articulation load. That interaction is why a truck with a stuck-connected sway bar never shows the articulation it paid for — see the sway bar disconnect service guide before blaming the joints.

The joints themselves are the primary wear item. New, they have essentially zero lateral play. They wear from the inside out after water intrusion or repeated high-load articulation without regreasing, and the resulting slop shows up first as imprecise steering.

The boots are the leading indicator. Cracked or torn boots let grit in and grease out, and a contaminated joint fails in a fraction of its normal life. Hot-climate trucks crack boots faster; a boot is inexpensive and a joint is not.

The frame-end bushings are conventional and wear on a conventional schedule — expect 80,000-120,000 miles before measurable deflection.

Do this on jack stands with the front wheels hanging free, so the joints are unloaded and any play is detectable.

  1. Lift the front of the truck and support the frame on jack stands. Confirm the truck is stable before getting under it.
  2. Grab each front tire at 9 and 3 o'clock and push/pull sharply while a helper watches the axle end of the control arms. Movement at the tire that shows up as movement in the joint is joint play; movement that appears at the wheel bearing or ball joint is a different problem. Watch the part, not the tire.
  3. Repeat at 6 and 12 o'clock to check vertical play and separate joint wear from ball joint wear.
  4. Visually inspect every boot with a flashlight — look for cracks, tears, and grease weeping out of the seal.
  5. Pry gently against the axle end of each control arm with a bar. Any perceptible movement between the arm eye and the axle bracket is a failing joint.
  6. On 2014-2018 trucks, look hardest at the lower joints — they carry more load than the uppers and generally wear first.
  7. While you are there, check the track bar ends, drag link, and tie rod (tie rod and drag link guide). Front-end play is rarely isolated to one component, and replacing one worn part while leaving three others is how people chase death wobble for a year.

There is no "monitor it" tier here. Play at these joints puts imprecise forces into the steering geometry, and the truck's mass amplifies every bit of it.

Ram does not publish a hard interval. A workable schedule from owner practice:

  • Inspect at 40,000 miles, then every 20,000 miles after
  • Inspect every 10,000 miles on a truck that sees regular trail use or water crossings
  • Regrease at every inspection if the boots are intact, using the grease specification in your service literature — see the fluids and service guide for the rest of the chassis schedule
  • Inspect immediately after any deep water crossing, and after any impact hard enough to knock the alignment out

If you are lifting the truck, inspect these joints during the build while the front end is already apart — the 4" mid-lift guide covers the geometry work, and a lift increases the static angle these joints operate at, so starting with worn ones is buying a problem.

The decision point: if you are replacing more than two worn OEM joints on a truck that sees real trail use, price the aftermarket arms with serviceable, higher-misalignment joints against the OEM rebuild. The aftermarket route costs more up front and is rebuildable rather than disposable, which changes the math over the second and third service interval. For a mostly-street truck, the OEM parts are the sensible spend.

Why it works

  • Real, measurable off-road articulation gain over conventional link/coil geometry
  • Bolt-in design is serviceable without specialty press tooling in most cases
  • OEM parts available through Mopar dealers, with an aftermarket upgrade path

Trade-offs

  • Adds wear points a conventional truck does not have
  • Joint replacement is not cheap, and a full refresh runs four figures
  • Cracked boots are a maintenance item most owners never check until the joint is already contaminated
  • Worn joints are a contributing cause of death wobble on lifted trucks, which makes neglect a safety issue rather than a comfort one

Tools required

  • pry bar
  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • flashlight
  • dial indicator or tape measure
  • grease gun
  • scan tool
