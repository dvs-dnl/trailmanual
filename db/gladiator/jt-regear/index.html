Re-Gearing the Gladiator JT — 4.56 and 4.88 Ratios

Difficulty 5/56–12 hrs$900–25002020-2024

Stock JT gearing (3.73) was calibrated for factory tires — run 37s without regearing and you're dragging a heavy truck through every gear while your fuel economy craters and your transmission runs hot.

The Gladiator JT's stock gear ratio is 3.73 on most trims. That ratio was engineered around the factory 32"–33" tire diameter. When you step up to 35s, the effective final drive ratio gets numerically lower — the engine has to pull a larger tire through each rotation, and it doesn't have the multiplication it needs to do that efficiently. At 37s, the problem is pronounced: slow acceleration, labored highway cruising, poor low-end pulling power, and a transmission that works harder in every gear.

The JT's longer 137" wheelbase also means more rotational mass in the driveshafts, and carrying a truck bed and its payload adds total system weight. The impact of running tall tires without a regear is more pronounced on the JT than on the JL because of that extra mass — you feel it earlier and more consistently. For a JT running 35s, 4.56 gears restore the drivetrain math. For a JT on 37s, especially in rock-crawling or regular trail use with gear in the bed, 4.88 is the correct answer.

The Dana 44 front (non-Rubicon trims have Dana 30 fronts — this guide covers the Dana 44 found on Rubicon and Mojave) and Dana 44 rear both need to be re-geared simultaneously. Mismatched front and rear ratios will cause binding in 4WD. Yukon Gear's 4.88 ring and pinion for the Dana 44 ($249 each axle) is well-proven and widely installed on JT/JL builds; the Yukon master install kit ($269) bundles all the bearings, seals, and shims needed so you're not sourcing pieces separately. Budget for professional installation unless you have prior differential rebuild experience — this is not a beginner job, and a poorly shimmed pinion will destroy a new gear set in short order.

The goal of a regear is to put your crawl ratio and highway RPM back where the factory intended for stock tires. Rough matches for the JT:

Tire sizeRecommended ratioResult
33" (stock-ish)3.73 (factory)No change needed
35"4.56Restores acceleration and highway cruise near stock
37"4.88Correct for trail/crawl use with gear in the bed
37"+ heavy overland5.13 (specialty)For very heavy, tall-tire builds — rare on the JT

Going one step milder (staying 4.56 on 37s) leaves the truck feeling doughy under load; going aggressive (4.88 on 35s) buzzes at highway speed and costs a little fuel economy. Match the ratio to the tire you're actually running, not the tire you might buy someday. Confirm your fitment first in 37-inch tire fitment on the JT or 35-inch fitment.

  • Pinion depth is the one that ruins gear sets. Backlash you can adjust after the fact; pinion depth you set once with the shim stack, and getting it wrong prints a bad contact pattern that whines and eventually fails. Read the gear pattern with marking compound and don't final-assemble until it's centered — this is why professional installation is worth it for a first-timer.
  • Gear the axles as a matched pair. Front and rear must run the same ratio or you'll get driveline binding in 4WD. This is also why a regear costs roughly double a single-axle job — you're paying for two complete teardowns.
  • Respect the break-in. New gears need a gentle first 500 miles with no heavy towing or hard trail load, then a fluid change to flush the metal from initial wear-in. Skipping the fluid change traps that grit in the diff.
  • Do it with the locker if one's coming. The teardown for a regear is identical to a locker install. If a locker is anywhere in your plan, combine them and pay the labor once — see Rear Locker for the Gladiator JT. Add the correct friction modifier if you're running a limited-slip, and top off with 75W-140 synthetic per transfer case and driveline fluid service.

Steps

  1. Drain both differentials completely.
  2. Front differential: remove front driveshaft, disconnect axle shafts, and remove differential cover.
  3. Remove and mark pinion flange nut (count and record the rotational torque before removal for reference on reassembly bearing preload).
  4. Remove the pinion shaft, bearing races, and carrier assembly. Clean the housing.
  5. Install the new ring gear on the carrier; torque ring gear bolts to spec.
  6. Set pinion depth using the new shim stack and a depth tool — this is the most critical measurement. Use gear marking compound to verify contact pattern before final assembly.
  7. Install new pinion bearings, set pinion preload to spec using an inch-pound torque wrench on the pinion flange nut.
  8. Install the carrier, set backlash (.006"–.010" for Dana 44), verify contact pattern again.
  9. Reinstall bearing caps and torque to spec. Install new axle seals.
  10. Repeat for the rear differential.
  11. Fill both axles with 75W-140 synthetic gear oil. Add ARB/locker additive if running a limited-slip unit.
  12. Drive gently for the first 500 miles during gear break-in; re-inspect fluid at 500 miles and replace.

Why it works

  • Restores factory powertrain feel after tire upsizing — acceleration, highway cruising, and fuel economy all improve
  • 4.88 gears dramatically improve crawl ratio on rock trails with 37s
  • JT's longer wheelbase and payload capacity make regearing more impactful vs. JL — the math favors it
  • Gear swap pairs naturally with a locker install (same labor, same differential teardown)

Trade-offs

  • Professional installation strongly recommended — incorrect pinion depth and preload ruin new gear sets
  • Both axles must be geared simultaneously to avoid 4WD binding — costs double vs. a single axle swap
  • Total cost including labor typically runs $1,500–$2,500 for parts and shop time on both axles
  • Non-Rubicon JT Sport/Willys/Overland have a Dana 30 front — regearing a Dana 30 is a different (and more limited) gear selection than the Dana 44
