Coolant Flush — Jeep Gladiator JT

Difficulty 2/51–2 hrs$25–602020-2024

The JT Gladiator's 3.6L Pentastar V6 calls for OAT (Organic Acid Technology) coolant, and using the wrong type — or letting it go past service life — is how you get corrosion deposits building up inside an aluminum cylinder head.

Jeep specs the Gladiator's 3.6L for HOAT or OAT coolant, specifically a silicate-free formula compatible with the aluminum coolant passages throughout the Pentastar engine. Mopar's factory fill is a 5-year/150,000-mile OAT formulation. The factory interval holds up under normal use — if the Gladiator sees regular off-road water crossings, dusty desert runs, or extended periods of high coolant temp, inspect it at 60,000 miles rather than waiting for the 5-year mark.

The telltale sign that coolant needs replacement: it should be bright orange-yellow (Mopar OAT) or green (some aftermarket fills). Dark brown or rust-tinted coolant is carrying iron oxide and aluminum corrosion — the system needs a flush, not a drain-and-fill.

Do not mix coolant types. Mixing OAT with IAT (green silicate coolant) produces a gel that clogs radiator passages. If you're unsure what's already in the system, flush the entire system before filling with fresh coolant.

The label confusion around Pentastar coolant causes more wrong fills than any other part of this job. Here's the plain version. The factory fill is Mopar's orange-yellow OAT (Organic Acid Technology) coolant, marketed as the 10-year/150,000-mile formula on newer bottles (5-year on the earliest JT bottles — both are the same OAT chemistry). What matters is that it's silicate-free and phosphate-free, because both silicates and phosphates attack the aluminum and the water-pump seal over time.

Acceptable substitutes are coolants specifically labeled compatible with Chrysler MS-90032 or the OAT/HOAT spec: Zerex G-05 and Prestone's yellow OAT formulation are the two most commonly confirmed. Where owners go wrong is grabbing a green "universal" IAT coolant or a generic "works with all makes" jug that's actually a hybrid — mixing chemistries drops the corrosion package out of suspension and forms the gel that clogs the radiator and heater core. If you can't confirm what a bottle is, don't use it. And always cut concentrate 50/50 with distilled water, never tap; the minerals in tap water are exactly what the aluminum passages don't tolerate.

If the truck spends real time in Phoenix-grade summer heat or tows near the 7,650-lb rating, the coolant's condition matters more than the calendar. Pair a coolant inspection with your regular service-interval checks, and if the engine has a history of running warm, rule out the Pentastar oil-cooler/coolant crossover leak before assuming the flush fixed everything.

A JT that overheats after a flush is almost always trapping air, not short on coolant. The Pentastar's coolant passages sit high relative to the fill point, and air trapped at the thermostat or in the heater core reads as a phantom overheat or a heater that blows cold.

Bleed it deliberately: with the reservoir cap off and the front of the truck slightly nose-up (park on a gentle incline or ramps), idle the engine with the heater set to full hot and the fan on low. As the thermostat opens you'll see the level drop and bubbles rise — top off as it settles. Squeeze the upper radiator hose firmly several times to push trapped air toward the reservoir. Bring it fully to temp, let it cool completely, then recheck the cold level at the MAX line and repeat the warm-up once. Two heat-cool cycles clears nearly every air pocket. If the heater still blows lukewarm after that, there's a stubborn pocket in the heater core — the incline-and-idle cycle usually clears it on the third try.

  • Air pockets: The JT cooling system, like most modern vehicles with a sealed reservoir, can trap air in the heater core. If the heater blows lukewarm air after a coolant flush, there's an air pocket. Squeeze the upper radiator hose several times with the cap off to help burp air out.
  • Petcock seal: The plastic petcock on the radiator can weep after being disturbed. Inspect it closely after the first warm-up cycle. If it seeps, tighten it gently or replace it ($8–12 at any parts store).
  • Overfilling: Coolant expands significantly when hot. Fill to MAX when cold — if you fill above MAX when cold, the system pushes excess through the overflow when it heats up.

Mopar OAT antifreeze (1 gallon): ~$20–24. Spec-correct and the cleanest choice — no compatibility guesswork.

Zerex G-05 antifreeze (1 gallon): ~$18–22. OAT-compatible, widely available at auto parts stores, confirmed safe for Pentastar aluminum. This is a common substitute.

Prestone Extended Life: ~$16–20. Verify it's the OAT or HOAT orange/yellow formulation — Prestone sells multiple types and the labels look similar.

Coolant flush is a 60–90 minute job. Shop rate for a cooling system flush typically runs $80–150. The DIY cost is under $30 if you already have a drain pan and funnel.

Steps

  1. Let the engine cool completely — at least two hours after the last run. The cooling system is pressurized when hot; opening the cap on a warm engine causes dangerous steam and coolant spray.
  2. Locate the radiator drain petcock on the lower passenger-side corner of the radiator. It's a plastic valve — turn counterclockwise to open. Position your drain pan under it. The JT cooling system holds approximately 11–12 quarts.
  3. Open the coolant reservoir cap to allow the system to drain freely. The cap is on the translucent overflow reservoir on the driver's side of the engine bay — not the radiator directly.
  4. If the outgoing fluid looks rusty or has debris, do a flush: close the petcock, fill the system with distilled water, run the engine to operating temp with the heater on full (to flush the heater core), let it cool, and drain again. Repeat until the outgoing water runs clear.
  5. Close the petcock and refill with a 50/50 mix of Mopar OAT coolant (or Zerex G-05 compatible) and distilled water. Use distilled water — not tap water. Tap water contains minerals that accelerate scale buildup inside aluminum passages.
  6. Fill to the MAX line on the overflow reservoir. The system will draw coolant from the reservoir as it comes to temperature.
  7. With the reservoir cap off, start the engine and let it idle until the thermostat opens (coolant temp gauge reaches normal operating range). You'll see the coolant level drop as hot coolant circulates and air purges from the system. Add coolant to maintain the level.
  8. Once the thermostat opens and the upper radiator hose feels hot to the touch, reinstall the reservoir cap.
  9. Check for leaks at the petcock, hose connections, and reservoir cap. Let the engine cool and verify the reservoir level is between MIN and MAX.
