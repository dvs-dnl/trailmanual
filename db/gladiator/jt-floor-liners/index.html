Jeep Gladiator JT / Interior

Floor Liner Selection for the Jeep Gladiator JT

Difficulty 1/50.25–0.5 hrs$89–2492020-2024
Short answer

The Gladiator JT's interior takes abuse that most truck cabs never see — trail mud, standing water from doorsoff driving, and gear shuffled in and out for camp. Factory mats don't survive this. Custom-fit liners do.

Buy by the JT fitment listing rather than assuming a Wrangler liner transfers. Some fronts are sold for both — Quadratec lists a WeatherTech DigitalFit front liner for the JL and JT together — but rear liners follow each truck's own cab, and a misfit gap defeats the purpose. Custom-molded liners for the JT have been available since 2020 from WeatherTech, Husky Liners, Maxliner, and Mopar. Each has trade-offs worth understanding before ordering.

WeatherTech DigitalFit (about $127 for the front pair at Quadratec) is the fit-precision pick, with deep molded coverage of the footwell. The lip height is taller than stock mats, which is the whole point — mud and water pool in the liner rather than soaking into the carpet. Downside: the material is slightly stiffer, which some owners find less comfortable under the heel in an all-day drive. For anything that involves water or mud — even occasional — it's the correct choice.

Husky WeatherBeater (~$99 for a front-and-rear set) gives most of the WeatherTech protection at a lower price. Fit is slightly less precise — there are small gaps at the corners that WeatherTech doesn't have — but for owners who don't submerge the floors regularly, the Husky set is honest value. Husky's X-Act Contour series, a step up in price, closes the gap considerably; Quadratec lists the WeatherBeater from $80.99 for the JT.

Maxliner A1/B1 set (~$89 complete) is the budget option. Fit is passable, material is adequate. Not the choice for serious trail or off-road use, but perfectly acceptable for the owner who wants something better than paper mats on a street-driven JT.

Mopar OEM liners ($149): FCA's official option. Identical fit to WeatherTech (same scanning process for the OEM supplier), with a Mopar logo. The premium over WeatherTech is purely branding — no functional difference. Skip unless you want the logo.

Doors-off consideration

The JT gets water on the floor — not trail water, but rain — every time you remove the doors and the sky opens up. Liners with high sidewall lips handle this. WeatherTech and Husky both have lip heights sufficient to contain rain events. After a downpour, pull the plugs in the JT's floor drain holes (factory-installed in the cab) and tilt the vehicle slightly to drain, or use a shop vac to pull standing water from inside the liner before it seeps under.

Installation

  1. Remove the factory floor mats (if present).
  2. WeatherTech and Husky liners drop into place with no hardware — they're held by friction and the factory mat retention posts. Line up the posts with the holes in the liner and press down.
  3. Check that the driver's liner does not interfere with pedal movement or return. This is the only safety check required — a liner that catches under the brake pedal is dangerous.
  4. Rear liners in 4-door Crew Cab JT models cover the second-row footwell. The Sport and Willys single-cab configuration uses a different rear section — confirm the correct part number before ordering.

Bed vs. Cab — Two Different Protection Jobs

Floor liners protect the cab. They do nothing for the bed, and JT owners routinely conflate the two. The cab footwell needs a molded liner with tall sidewalls to contain water and mud from doors-off driving. The bed needs its own protection strategy — a spray-in or drop-in bedliner — because the 5-foot steel bed rusts from the same trail mud and standing water, plus the abrasion of hauling gear, firewood, and recovery equipment. If you're outfitting a JT for trail and camp duty, budget for both; see bed liner options for the spray-in vs. drop-in decision, and cargo bed management for keeping loose gear from sliding into the tailgate on the trail.

Cleaning and Longevity

The reason to buy a liner is so you can hose it out — but that only works if you pull it to do it. Trapped water and grit under a liner will hold moisture against the factory carpet and, over seasons, promote mildew and corrosion at the seat and seatbelt anchor points. After a muddy weekend, remove the liners, rinse both the liner and the carpet beneath, and let both dry fully before reinstalling. Thermoplastic liners (WeatherTech, Husky) tolerate a pressure washer on a wide fan; rubber-blend mats can be scrubbed with mild soap. Avoid petroleum-based dressings on the driver's liner — they leave a slick film exactly where your foot needs grip on the pedals.

What to Watch For

The single safety item on this install is pedal clearance. A liner that shifts forward and catches under the brake or accelerator is a genuine hazard, not a nuisance. Confirm the driver's liner seats on the factory retention posts and doesn't creep. Re-check it after the first few drives and any time you've pulled it for cleaning. If your JT didn't come with floor retention posts (some base configurations), add aftermarket anchor hooks rather than trusting friction alone.

Costs and sources

Budget-tier Maxliner set: ~$89 complete (front + rear). Acceptable for street use.

Mid-tier Husky WeatherBeater set: ~$99–130. Best value for trail owners who want reliable containment without WeatherTech pricing.

Premium WeatherTech DigitalFit set (front + rear): ~$160–180. The ceiling for this category. Overkill for a garage-kept JT; exactly right for a rig that sees mud seasonally.

Floor liners are install-it-yourself items with no tools required. The only money saved by going to a shop is time, and there's no time to save — this is a 10-minute job.
