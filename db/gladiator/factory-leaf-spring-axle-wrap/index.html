Jeep Gladiator JT / Suspension

Rear axle hop and shudder on the Gladiator: the five-link coil rear and what to check

Difficulty 3/51.0–5.0 hrs$0–9002020-presentJump to steps
Short answer

The Gladiator has no rear leaf springs. Its rear is a five-link coil setup: two upper and two lower control arms plus a track bar, so leaf-spring axle wrap, traction bars, add-a-leafs and re-arching don't apply to this truck. The rear axle can still hop or shudder under hard throttle. Start with worn parts and shocks, measure pinion angle if the truck is lifted, and spend on arms only after that.

What Axle Wrap Is

This page used to describe the JT as leaf-sprung. That was wrong, and the link stays the same so old references still land here.

Stellantis's spec sheet lists the rear as a solid axle with link coil, trailing arms, a track bar, coil springs and a stabilizer bar. The Mojave gets a larger rear track bar and bushings. The launch press release adds that the control arms sit under the frame rails and the rear shocks face forward.

How to Identify It

Each part has its own job. The four control arms handle fore-aft location and resist the axle's rotation. The track bar handles side-to-side location. The coils carry the weight, and the shocks control how fast it all moves. On a leaf-sprung truck the spring does several of those jobs at once, which is why it can twist into an S-curve. There's no such part here, so there's nothing to re-arch and no job for a leaf-style traction bar.

Griggs Racing defines wheel hop as a violent, rapid bouncing of a drive wheel under hard acceleration, caused by the axle winding up under torque and then releasing that energy as oscillation. Griggs is a Mustang suspension company writing about solid rear axles, and it warns that hop damages driveline parts, axles and U-joints. Treat a repeating hop as something to fix rather than live with.

The Traction Bar Fix

On the JT, the parts resisting that windup are the control arms and their bushings. SuperPro says the factory rear lower arms can flex under heavy loads, which is a vendor selling replacement arms, so read it as a plausible mechanism rather than a measurement.

We found no Stellantis service bulletin or published diagnosis for rear axle hop on the JT, and no independent test comparing the fixes below. Ask a dealer to search bulletins by VIN if your truck is under warranty. Without that data this guide gives an order of checks, not a guaranteed cure, and we're not going to name one part as the fix.

  • Hop: the rear bounces under hard throttle, usually from a stop on a surface with grip. It stops when you lift off.
  • Highway drone or vibration: present at 65–75 mph under light throttle and changes when you lift off. That points to driveline angle, covered in the mid-lift guide, not hop.
  • Single clunk on throttle: look at U-joints and at loose arm, track bar or shock bolts before anything else.

The Re-Arch Option

1. Hardware and bushings. Get the truck safely on stands and inspect every rear arm bushing and both track bar ends, then check the bolts. Northridge's JL/JT chart lists factory rear values of 111 ft-lb plus an angle on the control arm bolts (65° and 60° on the lowers, 95° and 90° on the uppers), 85 ft-lb upper and 100 ft-lb lower on the track bar, and 89 ft-lb on the shocks. Those apply to factory parts. On aftermarket parts, use the maker's numbers.

2. Shocks. Hop is an oscillation, and damping is the shock's job. A rear shock that has lost its damping, or one that doesn't suit the current ride height, is a sensible next suspect and also a repair the truck may need anyway. The shock upgrade guide covers the options.

The Trailer Tow Tradeoff

3. Pinion angle on a lifted truck. Lift changes it, and kit makers treat this as a known JT issue. RockJock's bracket listing cites a pinion angle drop through the rear suspension's cycle, and Skyjacker's 3.5-inch kit description calls for longer rear upper arms to correct it. Measure with a digital gauge and compare to your kit maker's spec. Neither listing claims this cures hop, so treat it as correcting the setup, not as a promised fix.

4. Control arms. JKS's adjustable rear lowers are sold to set axle position and pinion angle across 0–4.5 inches of lift. SuperPro's fixed arms are sold on reduced flex and axle shift compared to rubber-bushed factory arms, and the listing doesn't mention hop.

Cost by path

5. A different rear link layout. Rock Krawler's RK07878 kit is a triangulated 4-link that removes the rear track bar and, per the listing, the bind of the OEM five-link. It's a rear suspension redesign you buy for travel and axle control. The listing makes no hop claim, so it's a poor first purchase for this symptom alone.

6. Your right foot. Griggs ties hop to hard acceleration. Rolling into the throttle costs nothing, and it matters most with a locked rear; see trail-using the rear locker.

The right spend depends on what the checks turn up. Don't buy arms for a truck with a torn bushing or a loose bolt.

An older version of this page said the JT runs leaf springs because it was engineered to tow. It doesn't. The rear coil setup is what carries the payload and tow ratings, and the rear shocks face forward for load management according to the launch release. If the rear sits low under tongue weight, that's a load question, not a hop question. Start with the payload and towing numbers and keep in mind that different rear coils don't raise a door-jamb rating.

Dialing in the install

Whatever you replace back here, the same details decide whether it works.

  • Torque at ride height, not on the jack. TeraFlex's JT rear instructions say not to tighten the control arms until the Jeep is on the ground. Tightening them with the axle hanging preloads the bushings.
  • Set pinion angle before final torque. If you're adding lift at the same time, finish the lift geometry first. The mid-lift guide covers the rear parts involved.
  • Check clearance at full flex. Cycle the suspension and confirm new arms and brackets clear the frame, exhaust and spare at full droop and full stuff.
  • Re-torque on schedule. TeraFlex calls for re-torquing suspension hardware after the first 300 miles and every 3,000 miles after that.

Steps

  1. Confirm the symptom: when it happens, on what surface, and whether it stops when you lift off
  2. Rule out U-joints and loose hardware
  3. Inspect rear arm bushings and track bar ends; check bolts against the factory values, or the maker's values on aftermarket parts
  4. Inspect the rear shocks and replace them if they're worn or don't suit the ride height
  5. On a lifted truck, measure rear pinion angle and correct it to the kit maker's spec
  6. Only then consider replacement or adjustable arms; torque them with the truck on the ground
  7. Retest on the same surface, and stop if the hop persists rather than stacking more parts

Why it works

  • The first checks cost little or nothing and catch real faults on their own
  • Correcting pinion angle and worn parts helps the truck whether or not it ends the hop
  • Rear arms and RockJock's brackets are replacement or bolt-on parts, with no fabrication called for in those listings

Trade-offs

  • No published Stellantis diagnosis or independent test backs a single cure
  • Most fix claims come from vendors selling the part, and most of those listings don't mention hop at all
  • On a stock truck with a healthy rear, an occasional hop on a hard launch may not justify any spend

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • breaker bar
  • torque wrench
  • digital angle gauge
  • pry bar
