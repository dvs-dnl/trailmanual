Trail-using the Rubicon's rear locker only

Difficulty 1/50.0 hrs$02020-present

Engaging only the rear locker is often the right call on trail — it provides the traction benefit with more steering ability than running front and rear locked together. Knowing when to use what is more valuable than knowing how to engage everything at once.

The Rubicon runs electronic lockers in both the front Dana 44 and the rear Dana 44. These are selectable differential locks — when engaged, both wheels on that axle are driven at the same speed regardless of traction conditions. The lockers can be engaged independently via the instrument cluster controls.

The system operates in 4L (low range) by default for full locker engagement. The rear locker can be engaged in 4H on some terrain conditions.

Moderate trail with steering demands: When you need traction but also need to steer precisely — navigating around obstacles, threading a line through loose rock — rear-only lock provides drive traction through the rear axle while leaving the front axle free to respond to steering inputs. Locking the front as well reduces steering feel and creates understeer on tight turns.

Mud and loose terrain: In deep mud or loose dirt, the rear locker keeps the rear wheels driving together without spinning down. The front axle in open-diff mode can still find traction and contribute, while the rear pushes. This is often enough to get through without front lock.

Highway-adjacent situations: If you find yourself in a difficult low-speed situation on or near pavement (a steep gravel driveway, a muddy turnaround), rear-only lock in 4H can provide the traction you need without forcing the front diff to fight the drivetrain binding that full lock creates on hard surfaces.

Add the front locker when rear-only isn't enough — typically:

  • Full-on rock crawling: Both wheels on both axles need to drive together when you're on slick rock or technical terrain where one wheel can easily lift
  • Deep water crossings: Maximum drive traction ensures you push through without getting stuck mid-crossing
  • Severely uneven terrain: When you're at the limit of articulation and one front wheel is off the ground

With both lockers engaged, the steering fights back. The truck wants to go straight; turning requires more input and creates axle binding on tight turns. On slick rock this binding is managed by terrain flex. On hard-pack or pavement, binding is uncomfortable and puts stress on the drivetrain.

Drive full-locked at low speed, on terrain that justifies it, and unlock before returning to any hard surface.

The Rubicon's electronic lockers are true lockers — not limited-slip. Engage them for terrain that demands it, not as a default trail setting.

Before you reach for a locker, understand what the Gladiator already does automatically. Every JT runs brake-lock differential (BLD) traction control — the ABS system pulses the brake on a spinning wheel to send torque across an open diff to the wheel with grip. On mild trail, BLD alone handles a lot: a single lifted tire on a rutted climb often gets sorted by the electronics before you'd ever think to lock.

Where BLD runs out is sustained, high-load slip. It's reactive and it works through the brakes, so it heats up and fades on a long, slow, one-wheel-spinning crawl. A mechanical or electronic locker doesn't fade — it physically ties the axle shafts together and holds. That's the dividing line: BLD for intermittent traction loss, a locker for terrain where you'll be putting steady torque through a lifted or low-grip wheel for more than a few seconds.

One more interaction worth knowing: engaging the rear locker on the Rubicon automatically relaxes some of the stability-control intervention, because the truck no longer needs to brake a "spinning" rear wheel that's now locked to its partner. That's by design — let the locker do the mechanical work and stop fighting the brakes. If you're adding a locker to a non-Rubicon trim, see the full install and hardware options in Rear Locker for the Gladiator JT — Dana 44 Options.

  • Unlock before pavement, every time. Driving a locked axle onto hard pavement forces both tires to turn at the same speed through a corner when they physically need to travel different distances. The result is tire scrub, driveline wind-up, and — repeated enough — accelerated wear on axle shafts and the ring-and-pinion. Make unlocking part of your "leaving the trail" checklist alongside airing up.
  • Throttle discipline on a locked rear. The JT's factory leaf-sprung rear is prone to axle wrap under hard throttle, and a locked rear multiplies the twisting load because both tires bite at once. Roll into the throttle rather than stabbing it. If you feel a hop or a shudder on a locked launch, that's wrap — see Factory leaf spring axle wrap on the JT.
  • 4L for full engagement. The front locker engages only in 4L on the Rubicon. If you try to lock the front in 4H and nothing happens, that's expected behavior, not a fault.
  • Confirm the indicator, not the switch. Locker engagement isn't instantaneous — the dogs sometimes need a few inches of wheel rotation to line up and drop in. Wait for the solid indicator light before assuming you're locked; a blinking light means it's still trying to engage.

Steps

  1. Select 4L for technical terrain (ensures proper engagement range)
  2. Engage rear locker via the instrument cluster control — wait for the indicator light to confirm lock
  3. Drive the section — assess whether rear-only is sufficient
  4. If more traction is needed, engage front locker as well
  5. After clearing the difficult section, disengage front locker first, then rear if appropriate
  6. Return to 4H or 2H before returning to any hard surface or higher-speed driving

Why it works

  • Rear-only lock is a useful middle-ground state — more traction than open, better steering than full-locked
  • Electronic engagement is fast and reliable
  • Independent front/rear control lets you match the locker setup to the terrain demand

Trade-offs

  • Driving locked on pavement or hard surfaces creates drivetrain binding and wear — unlock before returning to road
  • Rear locker on high-traction surfaces with hard acceleration can cause axle wrap — be smooth with the throttle
  • Some owners lock everything by default and miss the value of rear-only for moderate terrain

Tools required

  • 4LO transfer case position
  • rear locker switch (center dash)
  • traction/ESC off button
