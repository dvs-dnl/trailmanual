Jeep Gladiator JT / Steering

Gladiator steering recalls: what NHTSA lists

Difficulty 2/50.5–2.0 hrs$02020-2025Jump to steps
Short answer

NHTSA lists no intermediate steering shaft recall for the Gladiator. The steering recall that does cover the JT is 26V-363, filed in April 2026: on certain 2021–2025 Wrangler, Wrangler 4xe and Gladiator trucks, the electric hydraulic power steering pump wiring may overheat and cause a fire, even when the truck is parked with the ignition off. Check your VIN, and until the repair is done, park outside and away from buildings as NHTSA advises.

Safety-critical system. Steering and front-end work decides whether the vehicle tracks straight. Torque every joint to the factory spec and check toe before highway speed. Torque values and sequences come from the factory service manual for your year.

What the Recall Covers

Recall 26V-363 (FCA number 21D) covers about 1.08 million 2021–2025 Wrangler, Wrangler 4xe and Gladiator trucks. The JT's power steering is hydraulic, but its pump is electric, and the recall concerns that pump's wiring. Dealers inspect and replace the wiring and the pump as needed, free of charge. NHTSA's notice says VINs become searchable on June 11, 2026 and owner letters are expected from July 9, 2026, so a lookup before then can come back clean on an affected truck.

NHTSA's recall list for the 2020 Gladiator is a different set: a rear driveshaft assembled without grease (19V-636), manual-transmission clutch pressure plates, a seat belt, a backup camera, and the instrument cluster. None is a steering shaft. The intermediate shaft recall people remember is 18V-343, which covered 539 2018 Wrangler JLs with a weld that could split at the shaft spline. It does not cover the Gladiator.

Go to nhtsa.gov/recalls and enter your 17-character VIN. The site will return any open recalls on your truck. If 26V-363 appears and shows "remedy not yet available" or "remedy not yet received," follow the park-outside advice and contact a Jeep dealer. The repair is free.

How to Verify Your Truck

If a recall shows "remedy completed," the dealer has already done the work. Ask for the repair order so it's in your service history.

What to Listen and Feel For

There is no shaft recall on the JT, but the intermediate shaft and its U-joints still wear. Signs of a tired shaft:

  • Loose or clunky steering: A notchy or clunky sensation when turning, particularly at slow speeds or parking maneuvers
  • Play in the wheel: Noticeable dead zone at center before steering response begins
  • Click or clunk on full lock: A sharp sound at or near full steering lock — can indicate coupler binding or wear
  • Steering effort change: Sudden increase in required steering effort, particularly under load

None of these symptoms confirms the shaft is the problem — other steering components (track bar, ball joints, tie rod ends) create similar symptoms. The physical check below separates them.

After the Recall

The 26V-363 repair addresses a fire risk in the pump wiring. It doesn't change steering feel. If your JT wanders or clunks after the repair, the cause is elsewhere: track bar, ball joints, tie rod ends, the shaft, or alignment.

Recall status is one of the first things to verify on any Gladiator you're considering. A seller who says "it's had all its recalls done" is not proof — get the VIN and check it yourself at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands. An open recall isn't a dealbreaker (the fix is free and any Jeep dealer can perform it), but it tells you the previous owner wasn't tracking safety campaigns, which is worth factoring into how the rest of the truck was likely maintained.

Buying a Used Early JT

Pull the truck onto level ground and rock the steering wheel back and forth at a standstill with the engine running. Excessive dead play at center, or a distinct clunk, can come from the intermediate shaft coupler, the track bar mounts, or worn tie-rod ends — you won't isolate which from the driver's seat, but it flags that the front end needs a closer look. If you're weighing a specific truck, the payload and towing reality guide and the frame bend recall guide cover two more things worth checking on the same walkaround.

Related Steering Work

A recall check is a single item, not the whole front end. If your JT wanders, shimmies, or feels vague with no open recall, work through the rest of the steering system before spending on parts. Fresh, correct-spec fluid in the hydraulic assist matters more than most owners assume — see power steering fluid service. A worn or undersized damper is often blamed for symptoms it can't fix; the steering stabilizer upgrade guide explains when a stabilizer actually helps and when it only masks a worn component that needs replacing.

Checking the Shaft Yourself

The VIN lookup answers the recall question; a physical check answers the wear question. With the engine off and the key in a position that unlocks the column, have a helper rock the steering wheel back and forth through a few degrees of rotation while you watch the intermediate shaft — follow the column through the firewall down to the steering gear input. You are looking for lag: the column turning before the gear input responds, or visible relative movement at either U-joint or at the coupler. A healthy shaft transmits motion with zero perceptible delay.

One caution: don't confuse steering-box input play with shaft play — if the shaft moves as one piece but the pitman arm lags behind it, the slack is in the gear, which is a separate (and not recall-covered) diagnosis.

Other Early-JT Campaigns Worth Checking at the Same Time

The NHTSA VIN tool returns every open campaign on the truck, so read the whole list while you're there. On the 2020 list that includes the rear driveshaft campaign and, on six-speed manual trucks, the clutch pressure-plate campaigns that FCA issued across the JL/JT manual lineup. There is no frame recall; the frame bend guide covers what was actually reported. None of these cost the owner anything to remedy, and a truck carrying multiple open campaigns tells you something about how attentively it was owned.

Keep the paper trail. When a dealer completes a recall, ask for the repair order and file it with the service records — a VIN lookup only shows that a recall is no longer open, not what was done or when, so the repair order is your proof, and a documented history is worth real money when you sell. If you're the buyer instead, the same lookup costs nothing and takes two minutes; run it before you negotiate, not after.

Steps

  1. Locate your VIN (driver's door jamb sticker or dashboard through the windshield)
  2. Go to nhtsa.gov/recalls and enter the VIN
  3. Note whether 26V-363 or any other recall appears and whether the remedy has been completed
  4. If open: contact a Stellantis/Jeep dealer and schedule the recall repair
  5. While waiting for the 26V-363 repair, park outside and away from structures, as NHTSA advises
  6. After repair completion: verify the dealer recorded the completed recall in your service history

Why it works

  • The recall repair is free — no cost to the owner
  • The 26V-363 repair replaces the wiring and pump as needed
  • Stellantis's recall process is accessible and straightforward

Trade-offs

  • The pump recall is a fire risk even when the truck is parked and off
  • Dealer scheduling during recall campaigns can create wait times
  • Used-market JTs may have unknown recall status — always verify VIN before purchase

Tools required

  • flashlight
