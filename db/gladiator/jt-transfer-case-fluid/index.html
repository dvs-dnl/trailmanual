Transfer Case Fluid Service — Jeep Gladiator JT

Difficulty 2/50.5–1 hrs$25–602020-2024

The JT Gladiator runs an MP3022 (Sport/Willys) or MP3024 (Rubicon) transfer case depending on trim. Both use ATF (automatic transmission fluid), not gear oil — a mistake that ruins the unit quickly.

Transfer case service is one of the most skipped fluid changes on the JT — most owners do engine oil, some do differential fluid, fewer do the transfer case. It's a mistake. The fluid inside the transfer case shears over time, especially under the heat and load generated during four-wheel-drive engagement in technical terrain. Fresh fluid restores the clutch pack feel and keeps the electronic shift system operating correctly. FCA recommends service at 30,000 miles or every 3 years for off-road use — street-only use gets a longer interval, but most JT owners aren't street-only.

Critical fluid specification: The JT transfer case uses ATF+4 or equivalent DEXRON-VI. Do NOT use gear oil (75W-90, 75W-140) — the clutch packs inside the transfer case require ATF's friction modifiers to function correctly. Gear oil destroys them. Use Mopar ATF+4 (part number 68218057AC) or a compatible brand that meets the specification. Valvoline ATF+4 and Pennzoil Multi-Vehicle ATF are both acceptable alternatives.

The JT's transfer case sits between the transmission and the front and rear driveshafts, roughly centered under the cab. On a lifted JT with factory skidplates, the skidplate must be removed first — four mounting bolts. The drain plug is at the bottom of the transfer case; the fill plug is on the side, slightly higher. Both are typically 1/2" drive square plugs (no socket required, use a 1/2" ratchet directly). On some JT configurations, one or both may be hex bolt plugs — check before committing to a drain.

  • Overfilling: The weep method (fill until fluid seeps out the fill port) is accurate for the transfer case. Overfilling pressurizes the case and can push fluid past seals.
  • Metal shavings on the drain plug: A small amount of fine metallic paste on the drain plug is normal. Chunks or significant magnetic debris indicate internal wear — inspect more closely and consider having the case checked by a drivetrain shop.
  • Grinding or delayed engagement in 4WD: If the transfer case engages sluggishly, grinds when shifting between 2H/4H/4L, or refuses to transfer smoothly, a fluid change sometimes resolves it — degraded fluid loses the friction modifiers needed for smooth clutch pack engagement. If grinding continues after fresh fluid, the clutch packs may be worn.
  • Skidplate alignment: The factory skidplate bolts into specific locations. Reinstall before checking for leaks — the plate and transfer case share some mounting brackets on certain JT configurations.

The two cases take the same ATF+4 but differ in gearing and access:

  • MP3022 (Command-Trac, Sport/Willys/Overland): Part-time 4WD, 2.72:1 low range. Standard drain-and-fill.
  • MP3024 (Rock-Trac, Rubicon/Mojave): 4:1 low range for serious crawling, heavier-duty internals. Same fluid, same procedure, but the Rubicon's fuller skid package means more to remove for access.

Both hold roughly a pint. Both use ATF+4. Neither takes gear oil. If you regear the axles or add a rear locker, the transfer case fluid interval matters more — a locked, regeared truck puts more heat and shock load through the case.

Fold this into your broader service interval schedule so the transfer case does not become the fluid everyone forgets.

Mopar ATF+4 (OEM specification): ~$11–15 per quart at the dealer parts counter. One quart covers the JT transfer case plus a small margin.

Valvoline or Pennzoil ATF+4 alternative: ~$8–12 per quart. Same spec, lower cost, appropriate for this application.

Fluid pump kit (manual, one-time purchase): ~$10–15. Makes transfer case and differential fills manageable solo — don't try pouring from a quart bottle into a side port on an overhead angle.

Shop service for transfer case fluid: ~$80–120 including parts. The DIY savings here are worth capturing — this is a 30-minute job once you've done it once.

Steps

  1. Warm up the vehicle — drive 10–15 minutes so the fluid is warm and flows freely. Warm fluid drains more completely than cold.
  2. Lift the vehicle on jack stands to working height. Access to the transfer case requires working under the vehicle.
  3. If equipped with factory skidplates: remove the four mounting bolts and lower the plate. Secure it out of the way.
  4. Position the drain pan under the transfer case drain plug.
  5. Remove the FILL plug first — confirm you can break it loose before draining. If the fill plug is seized, don't drain — a drained transfer case with a stripped fill plug is a serious problem.
  6. Remove the drain plug. Allow to drain completely (5–7 minutes for warm fluid).
  7. Reinstall the drain plug. Torque to 20 ft-lbs. Do not overtighten the aluminum housing threads.
  8. Using a fluid pump (hand pump with a hose works well), push ATF+4 into the fill port until fluid begins to weep out the fill hole. This indicates the case is full. The JT transfer case holds approximately 1 pint (0.5 L) — have at least 1 quart on hand.
  9. Reinstall the fill plug. Torque to 20 ft-lbs.
  10. Reinstall the skidplate. Lower the vehicle and check for leaks at both plugs during the first drive.

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • 1/2" drive ratchet or breaker bar
  • fluid pump (for fill)
  • drain pan
  • nitrile gloves
  • shop rags
