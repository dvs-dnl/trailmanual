Jeep Gladiator JT / Suspension

Budget Lift Kit for the Gladiator (≤$600)

Difficulty 2/53–5 hrs$100–5992020-2024
Short answer

A budget lift gets your Gladiator off stock ride height for a better stance and a little extra tire clearance — but know what you're paying for and what you're not.

Under $600 buys you coil spacers up front, sometimes a matching rear spacer, and that's roughly it. The Gladiator JT is coil-sprung at both ends: the rear is a five-link coil setup with two upper and two lower control arms and a track bar, so there are no leaf packs, lift blocks or U-bolts on this truck. Many budget kits only address the front, and a 2" front spacer on a stock JT will leave the rear sitting lower than the front unless you add a rear coil spacer or longer rear coils. Some builders intentionally run a slight nose-up rake for clearance; most prefer level. Factor that into your budget before you order.

Rough Country's 2.5" spacer kit (about $100 with shock relocation brackets) is the cheapest listing here. Quadratec's parts list shows front coil spacers, front bump stops and front sway bar links, with no rear coil spacer, and it isn't compatible with the Mojave. It won't transform the suspension geometry, but it gives you the stance upgrade most Gladiator owners want in year one. The Teraflex 1.5" leveling kit ($116) is front spacers only, works with original or stock-length shocks, and lists 33" tires on Sport and Overland fenders and 35" on Rubicon fenders. Daystar's 3/4" rear spacers ($44) are the barebones add-on if you want the rear up too: two rear spacers and nothing else.

None of these kits include geometry correction. That means no adjustable control arms, no alignment cam bolts, no caster correction. At 1.5"–2" of lift, the factory geometry is generally acceptable, but you will need a front-end alignment after install regardless. Budget $80–$120 for the alignment shop visit — it's not optional. If you're planning to push to 35" tires, a budget spacer kit is a reasonable starting point, but you'll want to add alignment cam bolts and potentially a track bar drop bracket to keep the handling honest.

A spacer lift raises the truck; it does not improve how the suspension works. You get the stance and enough clearance for 33s, but the shocks are still the factory units now running at the top of their travel, the control arms are still fixed-length, and caster still drifts out of spec above 2". None of that matters much at 1.5"-2" on a street-and-gravel JT. It matters a lot if you're headed for 35s and real trails.

The honest decision point: if 33" tires and a level stance is the goal, a budget spacer kit is money well spent. If you already know you want 35s, a bumper, and armor, buying a spacer now means buying a mid-tier kit later and eating the cost of the spacers — go straight to the mid-tier. Don't buy a budget lift as a "foundation" for a build that's going bigger; it isn't one.

Torque factory lug nuts to 130 ft-lbs and re-check them after the first 50 miles — new wheel seating loosens them. On aftermarket wheels, use the wheel maker's number. If a hop or shudder under hard throttle shows up after the lift, work through the rear axle hop guide before blaming the drivetrain.

Don't run a spacer kit past 2.5". Beyond that the factory shocks top out, the ride gets harsh at extension, and you risk overextending the front CV and axle components. If you want more than 2.5", it's a mid-tier kit with replacement coils and shocks, not more spacer.

The budget kits handle the front with spacers; how they handle the rear varies, so read the parts list before you buy. A rear coil spacer is the cheapest path. Daystar's pair adds 3/4", and Skyjacker describes its rear spacers as raising the rear about an inch for a more level stance. A spacer adds height without adding spring rate, so it won't help the truck carry bed weight. Longer rear coils are the step up, and they cost more than this tier usually allows: TeraFlex's triple-rate 3.5" rear coils run about $348 and Synergy's 2"–4" rear coils about $260 a pair, and Synergy requires at least 2" of bump stop spacing with its springs. They're the right call for a JT that regularly hauls, once you're past a budget lift. Leaving the rear stock is a legitimate option too, and it's what a front-only kit like the Teraflex 1.5" does.

The install isn't finished when the wheels go back on. Re-aim the headlights — 2" of lift moves the cutoff line enough to blind oncoming traffic and to shorten your own usable beam. Get the alignment within the first 30 miles, re-torque lug nuts at 50 miles, and if you opened up the rear, re-check its hardware on the kit's schedule; TeraFlex's rear instructions call for a re-torque after the first 300 miles and every 3,000 miles after that. Cycle the front suspension to full droop (jack under the frame, wheels hanging) and confirm the brake lines and ABS wiring still have slack; spacer kits rarely exceed the factory line length at 2", but confirming costs five minutes and a failure costs a brake circuit. If the truck picks up a vibration at highway speed that wasn't there before, look at the rear driveline angle before blaming the kit. Pinion angle on this truck is set through the rear control arms, and adjustable rear lowers like JKS's are the correction, not a return label.

Steps

  1. Break the lug nuts loose on both front wheels before jacking up the vehicle.
  2. Support the front axle under the differential with a floor jack, then raise and secure the vehicle on jack stands at the frame.
  3. Remove the front wheels.
  4. Unbolt the front sway bar end links — you'll need slack to lower the axle enough to install spacers.
  5. Lower the jack slightly to decompress the coil springs. Install the coil spacers per the kit instructions, seating the spacer on top of the coil spring or on the upper mount depending on kit design.
  6. Reinstall end links (use new hardware if provided), reinstall wheels, and torque factory lug nuts to 130 ft-lbs. On aftermarket wheels, use the wheel maker's number.
  7. For the rear (only if your kit includes a rear coil spacer): follow the kit's instructions. TeraFlex's JT rear spring instructions show the general sequence: with the frame on stands and the axle supported, disconnect the sway bar links at the frame, unbolt the brake line brackets from the frame, pull the track bar from the axle, remove the upper control arms from the axle, loosen the remaining arm bolts, and remove the shocks at the axle. Lower the axle, clearing any retainers on wiring or hoses, seat the spacer, then reverse the order. Don't tighten the control arms until the truck is on the ground.
  8. Lower the vehicle and get a front-end alignment before driving more than 20–30 miles.

Why it works

  • Lowest cost entry point for improved stance and minor tire clearance
  • Front install is beginner-friendly — no spring compressors required for spacer kits
  • Reversible — returns to stock by removing spacers
  • Clears 33" tires on all JT trims with 2" of lift

Trade-offs

  • No geometry correction — alignment required, and handling degrades more noticeably above 2"
  • Many kits lift only the front and leave the rear coils at stock height — rake is common
  • Ride quality unchanged or slightly worse with spacers vs. replacement coils
  • Not a foundation for 35s or heavier wheeling; upgrading later means buying again

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • socket set
  • torque wrench
  • pry bar
