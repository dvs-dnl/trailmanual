Budget Lift Kit for the Gladiator (≤$600)

Difficulty 2/53–5 hrs$149–5992020-2024

A budget lift gets your Gladiator off stock ride height for a better stance and a little extra tire clearance — but know what you're paying for and what you're not.

Under $600 buys you coil spacers or a basic coil replacement up front, and that's roughly it. The Gladiator JT runs a coil-sprung front — same architecture as the JL Wrangler — but uses leaf springs in the rear. That rear leaf spring setup is the key detail that separates the JT from the JL, and it's why most budget kits only address the front. A 2" front spacer on a stock JT will leave the rear sitting lower than the front unless you add AAL (add-a-leaf) packs or swap to longer rear leaf springs. Some builders intentionally run a slight nose-up rake for clearance; most prefer level. Factor that into your budget before you order.

The Rough Country 2" kit ($199) is the most affordable complete solution, including a basic rear block to address the rake issue. It won't transform the suspension geometry, but it will clear 33s without rubbing and give you the stance upgrade most Gladiator owners want in year one. The Teraflex 1.5" spacer kit ($299) is a better-quality option that uses CNC-machined billet spacers and includes all hardware — it's the right call if you want something you won't replace in 18 months. Daystar's 2" coil spacers ($149) are the barebones option: front spacers only, no rear component, intended for owners who want a level look on pavement or light gravel.

None of these kits include geometry correction. That means no adjustable control arms, no alignment cam bolts, no caster correction. At 1.5"–2" of lift, the factory geometry is generally acceptable, but you will need a front-end alignment after install regardless. Budget $80–$120 for the alignment shop visit — it's not optional. If you're planning to push to 35" tires, a budget spacer kit is a reasonable starting point, but you'll want to add alignment cam bolts and potentially a track bar drop bracket to keep the handling honest.

A spacer lift raises the truck; it does not improve how the suspension works. You get the stance and enough clearance for 33s, but the shocks are still the factory units now running at the top of their travel, the control arms are still fixed-length, and caster still drifts out of spec above 2". None of that matters much at 1.5"-2" on a street-and-gravel JT. It matters a lot if you're headed for 35s and real trails.

The honest decision point: if 33" tires and a level stance is the goal, a budget spacer kit is money well spent. If you already know you want 35s, a bumper, and armor, buying a spacer now means buying a mid-tier kit later and eating the cost of the spacers — go straight to the mid-tier. Don't buy a budget lift as a "foundation" for a build that's going bigger; it isn't one.

Torque the lug nuts to 100-110 ft-lbs and re-check them after the first 50 miles — new wheel seating loosens them. If you added a rear block, watch for axle wrap under hard throttle: blocks increase the leverage that lets the rear axle rotate, and a budget rear block is the setup most prone to it. A shudder under acceleration after the lift is that, not a drivetrain fault.

Don't run a spacer kit past 2.5". Beyond that the factory shocks top out, the ride gets harsh at extension, and you risk overextending the front CV and axle components. If you want more than 2.5", it's a mid-tier kit with replacement coils and shocks, not more spacer.

The budget kits handle the front with spacers; how they handle the rear varies, and the rear choice matters more than the front on a leaf-sprung truck. A block between the axle and the leaf pack is the cheapest path and the one most budget kits take. It adds no spring rate — ride stays factory — but it lengthens the lever arm the axle uses to twist the pack under throttle, which is why blocks and axle wrap travel together; keep blocks to 1.5" or less. An add-a-leaf gains similar height by adding a leaf to the factory pack. It raises spring rate, so the truck carries bed weight better and wraps less, at the cost of a stiffer unloaded ride — the right call for a JT that regularly hauls. Longer shackles are the option to skip: they change the spring's operating angle, eat droop travel, and put the extra length into a pivoting link — the height gain isn't worth the geometry cost on this truck.

The install isn't finished when the wheels go back on. Re-aim the headlights — 2" of lift moves the cutoff line enough to blind oncoming traffic and to shorten your own usable beam. Get the alignment within the first 30 miles, re-torque lug nuts at 50 miles, and re-check rear U-bolt torque between 100 and 500 miles, because fresh U-bolts seat into the spring pack and lose preload. Cycle the front suspension to full droop (jack under the frame, wheels hanging) and confirm the brake lines and ABS wiring still have slack; spacer kits rarely exceed the factory line length at 2", but confirming costs five minutes and a failure costs a brake circuit. If the truck picks up a vibration at highway speed that wasn't there before, look at the rear driveline angle before blaming the kit — a rear block changes pinion angle slightly, and the fix is a degree shim, not a return label.

Steps

  1. Break the lug nuts loose on both front wheels before jacking up the vehicle.
  2. Support the front axle under the differential with a floor jack, then raise and secure the vehicle on jack stands at the frame.
  3. Remove the front wheels.
  4. Unbolt the front sway bar end links — you'll need slack to lower the axle enough to install spacers.
  5. Lower the jack slightly to decompress the coil springs. Install the coil spacers per the kit instructions, seating the spacer on top of the coil spring or on the upper mount depending on kit design.
  6. Reinstall end links (use new hardware if provided), reinstall wheels, torque lug nuts to spec (100–110 ft-lbs for JT).
  7. For the rear (if your kit includes a rear component): place the axle on jack stands, remove the rear leaf spring U-bolts, insert the supplied block between the axle and leaf pack, reinstall and torque U-bolts to spec.
  8. Lower the vehicle and get a front-end alignment before driving more than 20–30 miles.

Why it works

  • Lowest cost entry point for improved stance and minor tire clearance
  • Front install is beginner-friendly — no spring compressors required for spacer kits
  • Reversible — returns to stock by removing spacers
  • Clears 33" tires on all JT trims with 2" of lift

Trade-offs

  • No geometry correction — alignment required, and handling degrades more noticeably above 2"
  • Most kits do not fully address the rear leaf spring height — rake is common
  • Ride quality unchanged or slightly worse with spacers vs. replacement coils
  • Not a foundation for 35s or heavier wheeling; upgrading later means buying again

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • socket set
  • torque wrench
  • pry bar
