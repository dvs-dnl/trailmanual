Jeep Gladiator JT / Electrical

Alternator and Battery Upgrade — Jeep Gladiator JT

Difficulty 2/51–2.5 hrs$150–4502020-2024
Short answer

The stock JT electrical system handles factory loads fine, but the moment you add a winch, fridge, light bar, and a dual-zone air compressor, you're asking more than the factory 180- or 240-amp alternator was sized for.

Safety-critical system. Disconnect the battery before you start. Fuse every new circuit at the source and size the wire for the load and the run. Torque values and sequences come from the factory service manual for your year.

Jeep's 2024 Gladiator spec sheet lists an H7 battery rated 700 CCA, a 180-amp alternator on the Sport, and a 240-amp alternator on the Willys, Mojave and Rubicon (optional on the Sport). Earlier model years may differ; read the label on your battery before you buy a replacement. For a stock truck doing highway miles, that's adequate. For an overlander running a 12V fridge around the clock, a compressor air locker, LED light bar, and recharging accessories through USB, the math stops working — especially during slow trail speeds when alternator output drops significantly.

The first upgrade most JT owners make is swapping to an AGM (absorbed glass mat) battery. AGM batteries offer higher cold cranking amps, better deep-cycle recovery, and longer service life under repeated partial discharging — which is exactly how an overlanding electrical system uses a battery. The factory lead-acid battery isn't designed to be discharged below 50% repeatedly; an AGM handles it without permanent capacity loss.

Group size: The 2024 spec sheet lists an H7 (Group 94R). Match the group size on your existing battery's label, since the tray and hold-down are sized for it, and confirm any replacement's group before ordering.

Alternator: The stock 180- or 240-amp unit is adequate for most single-battery setups. If you're running a winch that pulls 400+ amps and a lithium secondary battery, consider a high-output alternator upgrade (Nations, Mechman, or Balmar offer JT-specific units in the 200–250 amp range at $350–500). For the majority of overlanders, the battery upgrade alone is the right move — the alternator holds up.

Battery Replacement Steps

  1. Make sure the engine is off and the key is out of the ignition. If your JT has a push-button start, remove the key fob from the vehicle to prevent accidental accessory activation.
  2. If you're concerned about losing radio presets or throttle body adaptations, connect a memory saver to the OBD-II port before disconnecting the battery.
  3. Locate the battery in the engine bay on the driver's side. Remove the negative cable first (black, 10mm bolt). Secure it away from the terminal — don't let it touch the battery post.
  4. Remove the positive cable (red, 10mm bolt). Then remove the battery hold-down bracket (typically one 13mm bolt).
  5. Lift the old battery out. It weighs about 45–50 lbs — get a grip before lifting.
  6. Clean the battery tray with a wire brush if there's corrosion. Apply a thin coat of battery terminal protector spray to the tray and terminal clamps.
  7. Set the new AGM battery in the tray, install the hold-down bracket, and torque it snug — not so tight that you crack the battery case.
  8. Connect the positive cable first, then the negative. Apply terminal protector spray to both posts.
  9. Start the engine and let it run for 15–20 minutes to allow the alternator to put an initial charge on the new battery.
  10. Check voltage with a multimeter at idle: 13.5–14.5V is normal charging range. Below 13V with the engine running points to an alternator issue.

Dual Battery Considerations

If your overlanding load consistently draws down the battery overnight, a dual battery setup is the answer — not a bigger single battery. A properly wired dual system uses a battery isolator or DC-DC charger to keep the secondary battery available for accessories while protecting the starting battery.

The Painless Wiring dual battery kit ($89) provides the isolator and wiring for a basic split-charge setup. A proper DC-DC charger (Redarc, Renogy) runs $120–250 but charges the secondary battery more efficiently, especially at low alternator RPM — relevant for overland camping where you run accessories at idle.

What to watch for

  • AGM charging voltage: AGM batteries require a charger or alternator that doesn't exceed 14.7V. Most factory alternators are within spec; if you've modified the electrical system, verify the charging voltage.
  • Terminal orientation: Confirm positive and negative terminal positions match the factory orientation before installing. A reversed battery in the factory tray will require different cable routing.
  • Memory reset: After disconnecting and reconnecting the battery, the JT may run slightly rough for the first few miles while the throttle body adaptation resets. This is normal.

Sizing your electrical budget

Before spending money, add up what you actually run. Size a JT electrical system by totaling the sustained draw of your accessories and comparing it against what the alternator can spare at trail RPM — not at highway cruise, where output is highest and least representative of how you camp.

Typical sustained draws: a 12V compressor fridge pulls 3–5A while the compressor cycles, an air-locker compressor 30–40A in short bursts, a 40W LED pod pair around 6–8A, a cab USB and charging load 5–10A, and a mounted winch 250–450A during a pull (but only for seconds at a time). The steady-state overnight load that flattens a battery is the fridge plus parasitic draw — usually 2–4A averaged. Over a 12-hour night that's 24–48Ah, which is roughly half the usable capacity of a single Group H6 AGM before you're into harmful deep-discharge territory.

That math is the reason the fix for an overnight-drain problem is a second battery, not a bigger starting battery — see the wiring approach in /db/gladiator/jt-dual-battery-setup/. The starting battery should never double as the house battery. Reserve the high-output alternator for the specific case where you run a winch hard and recharge a lithium bank at low RPM, where the factory 160–180A unit can't keep the starter battery topped while also feeding a DC-DC charger. Frequent winching stresses the charging system harder than lights ever will — plan the electrical budget around a recovery day, not a fair-weather cruise. Winch selection and current draw is covered in /db/gladiator/jt-winch/.

AGM vs lithium for the secondary battery

For the auxiliary battery, the choice is AGM or lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4), and the tradeoffs are real in both directions. AGM is cheaper up front ($185–290), tolerates the JT's factory charging voltage without a special profile, and handles cold well. Its downsides are weight (an H6 AGM is ~45 lbs) and usable depth — plan to pull no more than 50% before recharging, so a 65Ah AGM gives you about 32Ah of real overnight capacity.

LiFePO4 flips those numbers. A 100Ah lithium battery weighs 25–30 lbs, delivers 80–90Ah of usable capacity, and lasts several times as many cycles. The catches: it costs $500–900, it needs a DC-DC charger (Redarc BCDC1225D or Renogy 20–40A, $150–300) because the factory alternator voltage curve won't fully or safely charge lithium, and most LiFePO4 chemistries won't accept a charge below freezing without an internal heater. For a JT that camps in shoulder-season cold or above the snow line, confirm the battery has cold-charge protection or a heating element before buying.

The honest recommendation for most JT overlanders: start with a quality AGM secondary and a basic isolator, then move to lithium and a DC-DC charger only once you've measured your actual overnight draw and found the AGM's usable capacity is the real limiting factor. Auxiliary lighting is a common companion upgrade — wire it correctly per /db/gladiator/jt-led-lighting-upgrade/ so the new loads don't outrun the battery you chose.

Costs and sources

An H7 (Group 94R) AGM from Odyssey, Interstate or a similar name brand: roughly $250–300. Jeep's 2024 spec sheet lists H7, but match the group on the label of the battery you're replacing.

Optima YellowTop D35: ~$185–220. A solid step up from stock at a more accessible price point.

Battery replacement is a 30-minute job at home with no special tools. Shop rate for a battery swap runs $30–60 in labor beyond the battery cost — unnecessary for most owners.

  • 13mm socket
  • ratchet
  • extension
  • memory saver (optional)
  • multimeter
