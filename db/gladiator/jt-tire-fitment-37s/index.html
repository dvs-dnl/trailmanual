37s on a Gladiator: what it really takes

Difficulty 4/58.0–20.0 hrs$3500–80002020-presentJump to steps
Short answer

A Rubicon leaves the factory on 33-inch tires with 4.10 gears. You can bolt 35s on with a 2.5-inch lift. 37s require gearing, fender modification, possibly brake upgrades, and a hard look at the payload math — the Rubicon's already tight at 1,200 lb. Do this upgrade with your eyes open.

The Starting Point: What the Factory Gives You

Rubicon: 33-inch LT285/70R17 mud-terrains, 4.10 axle gears, Rock-Trac 4:1 transfer case.

Sport and Willys: 245/75R17 (about 31.5 inches) or LT255/75R17 (about 32 inches) depending on trim and year — both are on the JT's factory size list, so read your door placard. 3.73 axle gears.

Mojave: 33-inch Falken Wildpeaks, 4.10 axle gears (the desert-runner trim gets the short gears from the factory).

The Rubicon's 4.10 gears are designed for 33-inch tires. The relationship between tire size and gear ratio determines how hard the engine works at highway speed and how much torque multiplication you have in the lower ranges. Going to 35s on 4.10s is noticeable but livable. Going to 37s on 4.10s will strain the engine at highway speed, kill fuel economy, and put stress on the transmission.

35s: The Bolt-On Upgrade

With a 2.5-inch lift, 35-inch tires (315/70R17 metric — about 34.4 inches — or a true 35x12.50R17) fit a Gladiator without fender trimming. Most JT-specific 2.5-inch lift kits include the right hardware to clear this size. Stick with a proper JT-specific kit — the rear is a five-link coil setup exclusive to the Gladiator, so buy rear components listed for the JT, not JL parts.

The 4.10 gears on a Rubicon are borderline acceptable with 35s. If you highway-drive at freeway speeds regularly, budget for a re-gear to 4.56 or 4.88 when you put on 35s. It's not catastrophic without it, but the truck performs better with appropriate gearing.

37s: The Full Commitment

37-inch tires change the build equation in several ways simultaneously:

Gearing (mandatory): 37s on 4.10 gears will make the Gladiator sluggish on highway, strain the drivetrain, and reduce fuel economy significantly. 4.56 is the minimum; 4.88 is better. The re-gear means pulling both front and rear differentials, installing new ring and pinion sets, and setting up lash and backlash correctly. This is a shop job unless you have a ring-and-pinion setup background.

Lift: 37s need 3–3.5 inches of lift for clearance. This moves the driveshaft angles beyond the factory range and requires a full-length JT-specific kit with control arm corrections. Long-arm kits improve the geometry further at this lift height.

Fender trimming: The JT Rubicon has plastic fender flares that extend 4 inches from the body. At full articulation with 37s, even with a 3-inch lift, the tire can contact the fender flare. The fix is trimming the inner fender well liner and possibly trimming the flare itself. This is a cut-and-not-uncut decision — verify fitment at full stuff before you cut.

Brake consideration: The factory brake system is sized for 33-inch tires. Going to 37s means more rotating mass and a longer lever arm to stop. The factory brakes can stop a stock truck on 37s, but stopping performance degrades. High-performance brake pads are a worthwhile addition before you start running 37s on trail.

Payload impact: The Rubicon already runs 1,200 lb payload. 37-inch tires weigh approximately 15 lb more each than 33s — that's 60 lb added to the unsprung rotating weight. Every pound of tire and wheel added reduces your available payload. On a Rubicon loaded for trail with passengers and gear, this math gets tight quickly.

Recalibration: the step everyone skips

The JT's computer believes it still has 33s and 4.10s. On 37s with 4.88s, the speedometer, odometer, transmission shift points, and the ABS/ESP yaw math are all working from wrong numbers. The speedometer reads roughly 10% slow on 37s, the 8-speed hunts between gears because its shift maps assume a different effective final drive, and the stability control intervenes on bad data.

The fix is a programmer that writes the correct tire size and axle ratio into the ECU. The Tazer JT Mini (~$220-250) is the tool most JT owners use — it plugs into the OBD2 port and star connector, corrects tire size in 0.5-inch increments and the gear ratio to whatever you installed, and unlocks a few conveniences (TPMS threshold adjustment matters once you air down on trail). Budget it into the build; a 37-inch build without recalibration is an unfinished build. The re-gear itself is covered in the JT re-gear guide — the recalibration happens after the gears are in.

What breaks first on 37s

The Dana 44s (technically the AdvanTEK M210 front / M220 rear) carry 37s fine at the housing and shaft level. The wear moves to the perimeter parts:

  • Unit bearings (front hubs): The extra rotating mass and scrub radius load the front unit bearings hardest. Expect shortened life — a humming front end at 40,000-60,000 miles instead of 100,000+. Replacement runs ~$120-180 per side for quality Timken/SKF units, and it is a bolt-on job.
  • Ball joints: Same leverage problem. If you are re-gearing anyway, adding HD ball joints (Dynatrac, ~$400/set) while the axle is apart saves a second labor bill later.
  • Steering: The factory aluminum tie rod flexes visibly on 37s in rocks. An HD tie rod and drag link (Steer Smarts, Synergy — $400-700) is the difference between crisp steering and wander. Watch for the early symptoms in the death wobble guide before assuming it is alignment.
  • Front driveshaft: At 3.5+ inches of lift the front shaft operating angle approaches its limit. A cheap lift with no geometry correction accelerates CV/boot wear at the transfer case end.

None of this is a reason to skip 37s — it is the maintenance reality that separates a planned build from a string of surprise repair bills.

Steps

  1. Confirm current axle ratio (door sticker or manual)
  2. Price a re-gear: 4.56 for 35s, 4.88 for 37s — get quotes from two shops
  3. Select a JT-specific lift kit for the height required (not a JL kit)
  4. Mount one tire test-fitment before buying the full set — verify clearance at full lock and full articulation
  5. Trim fender liner/flare as needed — minimum cuts first, test fitment after each cut
  6. After re-gear: run break-in procedure per the gear oil manufacturer's instructions (typically avoid extended load for first 500 miles)
  7. Get an alignment after lift installation

Why it works

  • 37s dramatically increase trail capability on the JT's already-solid platform
  • The Dana 44s handle 37s without heroic axle upgrades (unlike XJ 8.25 builds)
  • The aftermarket has well-developed JT-specific 37-inch fitment solutions

Trade-offs

  • Full cost including re-gear, lift, tires, and alignment approaches $5,000–$8,000
  • Re-gearing the front and rear is shop-level work for most owners
  • Rubicon payload tightens further with the additional tire/wheel weight
  • Fender trimming is irreversible — measure twice

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • breaker bar
  • torque wrench
  • angle grinder (for fender trimming)
