Jeep Gladiator JT / Engine

Cold Air Intake Upgrade — Jeep Gladiator JT 3.6L

Difficulty 2/50.5–1.5 hrs$199–4502020-2024
Short answer

A cold air intake on the JT Gladiator 3.6L is worth single-digit horsepower at high rpm and a throttle response improvement you'll notice in daily driving. It will not transform the truck, and the honest gains are at the top of the RPM range — not where most Gladiator owners spend their time.

The JT's 3.6L Pentastar makes 285 hp factory. The intake system is restrictive by design — FCA optimized it for noise, not airflow. A cold air intake routes the filter box away from underhood heat and uses a larger-diameter intake tube to reduce restriction. The gains are real but small: K&N estimates 8.8 hp at 5,400 rpm for its 77-series kit on the JL and JT 3.6L, and that is the maker's own figure, not an independent dyno. Check emissions legality too: the same listing says the kit is legal in California, and states that follow California's rules, only on race vehicles that never see public roads. The throttle response improvement — faster pedal response at light throttle — is arguably more noticeable than the peak power number in street driving.

What a cold air intake won't do: add meaningful low-end torque (the gains are concentrated above 3,500 RPM), improve fuel economy significantly (any gain is offset by the tendency to use the extra power), or change anything about the JT's fundamental character as a truck. The 3.6L Pentastar is an efficient naturally aspirated engine — the intake is the right first modification, but the returns are modest.

Emission compliance note: Confirm the intake kit is CARB-compliant if you're in California or another CARB-state. Most major brands (K&N, aFe, Mishimoto) offer CARB-certified versions. An aftermarket intake on a JT that isn't CARB-certified in an emissions-tested state can trigger a check engine light or emissions failure.

Installation

  1. Disconnect the negative battery terminal. This prevents the ECU from throwing intake MAF codes during the install and resets learned fuel trims so the ECU can adapt to the new airflow characteristics.
  2. Remove the factory airbox lid. On the JT 3.6L, the lid is held by two 8mm bolts at the rear and two friction clips at the front. Lift it straight up after loosening.
  3. Disconnect the mass airflow sensor (MAF) connector from the factory intake tube. Squeeze the release tab and pull straight back — these connectors are plastic and break if levered sideways.
  4. Loosen the hose clamp securing the factory intake tube to the throttle body. Slide the tube off the throttle body elbow.
  5. Remove the factory airbox lower section — typically two 10mm bolts into the fender well.
  6. Follow the kit-specific instructions for your chosen intake. Most kits route the filter toward the fender or the front of the engine bay with a conical element in place of the factory box.
  7. Transfer the MAF sensor from the factory tube to the new tube. Typically two 8mm Torx screws. Handle the MAF element carefully — the sensing wire is fragile.
  8. Connect all couplers and tighten hose clamps hand-tight, then snug with a screwdriver (avoid over-tightening rubber couplers — they split).
  9. Reconnect the negative battery terminal. Start the engine and check for intake air leaks (hissing sound at idle, rough idle) before driving.

Does the 3.6L need a tune?

No. Unlike a turbocharged engine, the naturally aspirated 3.6L Pentastar does not need a supporting ECU tune to run an aftermarket intake safely. The stock ECU reads actual airflow through the relocated MAF sensor and adjusts fuel delivery through its normal closed-loop trims. As long as the MAF sensor is transferred correctly and mounted at the factory-specified distance from any bends in the new tube, the truck compensates on its own within a few drive cycles.

Two caveats that get people into trouble:

  • MAF placement matters. Every quality kit specifies where the MAF bung sits relative to the tube's bends because turbulent air across the sensor element reads inaccurately. Installing the sensor in the wrong bung, or flipping its orientation, produces a lean or rich condition the ECU can only partly trim out. If you get a persistent fuel-trim code (P0171/P0174) after an intake install, suspect MAF placement before anything else.
  • A tune is for other goals. People who tune a JT are usually chasing gains from larger tires (re-gearing the speedometer and shift points) or want throttle-response and transmission-behavior changes — not because the intake demands it. If you eventually re-gear for 37s, handle that as its own project; see the rear-end work in /db/gladiator/jt-regear/.

What to watch for

  • Hydrolocking risk with open filter intakes: A filter mounted low in the engine bay can ingest water during a water crossing. If your JT goes through water deeper than door-sill level, a sealed-box intake (Volant with sealed Powercore filter, or a factory airbox if you're going deep) is safer than an open conical filter. Many JT trail owners run an open intake on the street and swap back to the factory box for known water events.
  • MAF codes after install: A P0100 or P0102 code shortly after install usually means the MAF connector wasn't fully seated. Recheck the connector first. If the code persists, the ECU needs more drive time to adapt to the new airflow curve.
  • K&N oiling: If running a K&N oiled filter, don't over-oil during maintenance cleans. Excess oil migrates onto the MAF sensor and causes lean codes. Use the K&N recharger kit as directed — thin coats only.

Filter maintenance and cleaning

The maintenance burden depends entirely on which filter type you chose, and this is where trail use changes the math.

Oiled cotton-gauze filters (K&N, aFe oiled): clean and re-oil roughly every 30,000 street miles, but far more often in dusty trail conditions — a JT that runs washboard dirt roads weekly can load a filter in a fraction of that. Cleaning requires the brand's recharger kit: spray cleaner, rinse from the clean side out, dry fully (never with compressed air — it tears the media), then apply a thin, even coat of oil and let it wick for 20 minutes before reinstalling. The single most common mistake is over-oiling, which migrates onto the MAF sensor and throws lean codes. If you already have MAF-related codes on an oiled filter, clean the MAF element with dedicated MAF cleaner before assuming the sensor failed.

Dry synthetic filters (aFe Pro Dry S, Volant Powercore): no oil, no MAF contamination risk. Clean with compressed air from the clean side or a mild wash per the manufacturer, then reinstall dry. For anyone spending real time in dust, a dry filter is the lower-hassle, lower-risk choice, which is part of why the sealed Volant is the pick for water and dust exposure.

Whatever filter you run, inspect it at every oil change — pair it with the interval in /db/gladiator/jt-oil-change-pentastar/ so it becomes a habit rather than an afterthought. A clogged filter costs you the airflow gain you paid for and, worse, an unfiltered gap from a poorly seated element feeds grit straight into the engine.

Costs and sources

Mishimoto intake: ~$279. Solid OEM+ quality, available in polished aluminum. The value option in this category.

aFe Momentum GT: ~$349–410. Available with dry or oiled filter elements; check the CARB status for your state.

K&N 77-Series (77-1576KP): $472.99 at Quadratec. The brand recognition premium is real, but so is the support and filter replacement availability. Not for street use in California-rules states.

Volant with Powercore (sealed): ~$449. The correct choice if the JT sees water crossings. The sealed box provides an air source above the water line and the Powercore filter is dry-element (no oiling required).

Install is consistently under 90 minutes for anyone who's opened an engine bay before. Shop labor for this job is a waste — the complexity doesn't justify it.

Tools required

  • socket set (8mm, 10mm)
  • flathead screwdriver or trim tool
  • shop rags
