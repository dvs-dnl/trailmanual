Throttle Body Service and TPS Calibration

Difficulty 2/51–2 hrs$15–1201987-1990, 1991-1995, 1996, 1997-2001

A full throttle body service on the 4.0L means cleaning the bore and butterfly, servicing the IAC, and confirming the throttle position sensor reads where it should. On Renix Jeeps (1987-1990) the TPS is adjustable and has to be set; on HO Jeeps (1991-2001) it isn't, so you verify it instead. This guide covers both.

Carbon builds on the back of the throttle plate and around the bore from PCV vapors, and over a few years that buildup narrows the idle bypass enough to make the engine hunt, stall at stops, or idle high. Cleaning the throttle body fixes that. But cleaning is only half the job — if the throttle position sensor is out of spec or has a dead spot, you get the same symptoms plus hesitation off idle, hard shifts on automatics, and sometimes a check engine light. The TPS tells the ECU exactly how far the throttle is open, and on the automatic-equipped Renix trucks it also feeds the transmission controller, so a sensor that's reading wrong can confuse both at once.

The single most important fact to get straight before you start: the Renix TPS (1987-1990) is adjustable and the HO TPS (1991-2001) is not. Renix sensors mount with slotted holes and have to be dialed in with a multimeter to a target voltage. HO sensors bolt on one way only and are either in spec or replaced — there's nothing to turn. Confirm which one you have before you order a part or reach for a Torx driver, because the procedures diverge completely at the sensor.

Steps

  1. Park on level ground, set the parking brake, and let the engine cool. Disconnect the negative battery cable (10mm) — you'll be unplugging sensors, and you don't want the ECU storing false codes or the IAC moving while you work.
  2. Remove the air intake tube from the throttle body. Loosen the hose clamp at the throttle body (flat-blade or 7mm), disconnect the crankcase breather and any vacuum lines attached to the tube, and set it aside. You now have clear access to the throttle bore.
  3. Inspect the bore and butterfly. Open the throttle by hand using the cable linkage and look at the back side of the plate and the bore wall. Black, oily carbon is normal and is what you're removing. Heavy hard deposits mean it's been a long time.
  4. Clean the throttle body. Hold the throttle open by hand, spray CRC throttle body cleaner onto the plate and bore, and wipe with a lint-free rag. Use throttle body cleaner specifically — not carburetor cleaner, which is harsher and can attack the bore coating and the TPS shaft seal. Work the rag around the bore until the metal is clean. Do not jam rags or spray hard into the small cylindrical passage where the IAC pintle sits.
  5. Keep cleaner off the TPS. The throttle position sensor's shaft is poorly sealed, and solvent or water wicking past it ruins the sensor. Never spray directly at it. If you're removing the throttle body from the manifold for a deeper clean (often easier on 1991-2001 trucks), unbolt it, use a fresh throttle body-to-manifold gasket on reinstall, and torque the throttle body bolts to roughly 14 ft-lb.
  6. Service the IAC valve. The idle air control valve mounts on the throttle body with two Torx screws (T20/T25). Remove it gently — do not pull, twist, or pry on the pintle that sticks out, it's fragile. Wipe carbon off the pintle and seat with a rag dampened in cleaner. If the idle has been hunting badly and the valve is heavily fouled or worn, replace it rather than fighting it. Reinstall with the screws snug, not gorilla-tight.
  7. Identify your TPS before going further. Renix (1987-1990): the sensor sits on the throttle body with slotted mounting holes and a flat 3-wire connector to the ECU; automatics add a second square 4-wire connector for the transmission controller. HO (1991-2001): a single connector, two fixed mounting screws, no slots.
  8. HO trucks (1991-2001) — verify, don't adjust. Reconnect the battery. With the key on and engine off, back-probe the TPS signal wire to ground and read voltage on a multimeter. At closed throttle you should see roughly 0.6-1.0 volts. Slowly open the throttle by hand to wide open and watch the voltage climb smoothly to about 3.5-4.0 volts with no jumps, drops, or dead spots. A smooth sweep means the sensor is good and you're done — there's nothing to adjust. A flat spot, a jump to zero, or an erratic reading means replace the sensor. The HO TPS only fits one way; bolt the new one on and re-test.
  9. Renix trucks (1987-1990) — set the voltage. With the key on and engine off, back-probe the flat 3-wire connector. Measure the reference voltage across the outer two terminals (A positive to B ground) and write it down — it's usually around 4.8-5.0 volts. Then measure the output voltage on the signal terminal relative to ground at closed throttle. Loosen the two T20 Torx screws in the slotted holes and rotate the sensor until the output reads 83 percent of your reference voltage (example: 4.8 V reference × 0.83 = 3.98 V output). Tighten the screws while watching the meter so the reading doesn't drift. This is the cruiser54 method and it's the accepted spec for Renix XJs and MJs.
  10. Renix automatics — check the second connector. If you have an automatic, the square 4-wire connector feeds the transmission controller and follows the same 83-percent-of-reference target on its own circuit. Setting only the ECU side and ignoring the TCU side leaves you with correct fueling but wonky shifts. Set both.
  11. Reconnect everything: TPS connector(s), IAC, vacuum lines, breather hose, and the air intake tube. Double-check no hose was left off — an open vacuum port will cause a high idle or stall that mimics the problem you fixed.
  12. Reconnect the battery, start the engine, and let it idle. On HO trucks the ECU relearns idle over a few minutes; a short drive cycle with some stop-and-go locks in the adaptive idle. On Renix trucks, confirm the idle settles and there's no hesitation when you blip the throttle. If the idle still hunts after a clean IAC and an in-spec TPS, look next at vacuum leaks and the IAC's electrical connector.

Why it works

  • Restores smooth idle and crisp off-idle throttle response for the cost of a can of cleaner, if the TPS turns out to be fine.
  • The TPS check rules in or out a sensor that causes hesitation, stalling, and (on autos) bad shifts — symptoms people often chase in the wrong places.
  • Setting a Renix TPS correctly fixes fueling and transmission shift quality at the same time, since both controllers read that sensor.
  • No special tools beyond a multimeter; it's a driveway job on a cold engine.

Trade-offs

  • The Renix adjustment requires back-probing live connectors and reading a multimeter accurately — fiddly the first time, and a wrong setting drives idle and shift quality the wrong direction.
  • A heavily worn IAC or a TPS with a dead spot won't be saved by cleaning; you'll spend on parts after all.
  • Removing the throttle body for a deep clean means a new gasket and the risk of a vacuum leak if it isn't sealed and torqued properly.
  • On 1991-2001 trucks the TPS isn't adjustable, so if it's marginal the only fix is replacement — there's no dialing it back into spec.

Tools required

  • CRC throttle body cleaner
  • lint-free rags
  • T20 and T25 Torx drivers
  • 1/4 in. ratchet and socket set
  • flat-blade screwdriver
  • digital multimeter
  • back-probe pins or paper clips
  • needle-nose pliers
  • safety glasses
  • nitrile gloves
