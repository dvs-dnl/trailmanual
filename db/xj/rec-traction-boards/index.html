Jeep Cherokee XJ / Recovery

Traction Boards — Use, Placement, and Limits

Difficulty 2/50–1 hrs$80–3501984-1990, 1991-1995, 1996, 1997-2001
Short answer

Traction boards are the only recovery tool that works alone, with no second vehicle and no anchor tree — but only when the problem is a tire that can't find grip, and only if you air down and dig first.

Safety-critical system. Recovery gear carries loads far past the vehicle's weight. Use rated hardware, read the working load limit, and keep everyone out of the line of pull. Torque values and sequences come from the factory service manual for your year.

A traction board is a molded composite ramp with aggressive lugs on one face. You wedge it under a tire that's spinning in something soft, the lugs bite the tread on one side and the ground on the other, and the tire climbs out on the board instead of digging deeper. That's the whole mechanism, and understanding it tells you exactly when boards will work and when they'll waste twenty minutes of daylight.

Here's the honest verdict most reviews skip: boards earn their money in sand, mud, and snow. On rock, they're dead weight. If your XJ lives on Arizona desert trails and rocky shelf climbs, a pair of boards will ride on the roof for years without being touched, and your recovery budget is better spent on a strap, rated recovery points, and a way to air back up. If you run beach, wash, deep dust, or winter roads, boards are the highest-value item in the kit because they're the one thing that gets you out solo.

The single biggest mistake: reaching for boards too early

A stuck XJ has three possible causes, and boards fix exactly one of them.

The tires have no grip. Spinning in sand, mud, or snow with the belly still clear. This is the board's job, and boards are excellent at it.

The belly is carrying the weight. The Cherokee is high-centered on a berm, a rut crown, or a snow ridge, and the tires are barely loaded. Boards do nothing here — the tires aren't what's stuck. You need a shovel, a jack, or a winch to get the weight back on the tires first. Only then do boards matter.

Something is broken. A snapped axle shaft, a shredded sidewall, a locked-up hub. Boards can't move a wheel that won't turn.

Look at the belly and the tire contact patches before you unstrap anything. Ninety seconds of diagnosis saves the recovery.

Air down before anything else

Dropping an XJ on 31s from street pressure to 15-18 psi lengthens the contact patch dramatically and ends a meaningful share of soft-surface stucks on its own — no boards, no digging. In deep sand, 12 psi is common on a rig this light with the understanding that you're now at real risk of unseating a bead on a hard steering input.

If you're going to use boards, air down anyway. Lower pressure means the tire conforms around the lugs instead of skating across them, which is the difference between a board that bites and a board that gets flung out the back.

This is also why an air source is part of the traction board kit, not a separate purchase. Boards that get you unstuck five miles from pavement are only half a plan if you can't reinflate.

Steps — placing and driving a board recovery

  1. Pick your direction. Almost always reverse, back down your own tracks — the ground behind you is already compacted and you know it held the vehicle's weight once. Forward into unknown soft ground is how a one-board recovery becomes a four-board recovery.
  2. Dig. Clear the sand, mud, or snow piled in front of the tires in your chosen direction, and clear anything packed under the diffs, control arms, and skids. Boards cannot pull a rig through a berm it built for itself. MAXTRAX-style boards have a shaped leading edge intended to be used as a scoop for exactly this.
  3. Air down to 15-18 psi, lower in deep sand.
  4. Wedge the board hard against the tire tread — lugs facing up, tapered leading edge shoved under the contact patch, ramp angled up the direction you're driving. It should be jammed in tight, not resting nearby. A loose board shoots backward the moment the tire loads it.
  5. Board every tire that's spinning. With an open diff, that's the tire doing the spinning, not the one with grip. With two boards and four buried corners, do the drive axle's two first, then leapfrog.
  6. Clear the area. Nobody stands in line with the boards, front or rear. A board ejected by a spinning tire leaves at genuine speed, and the corners are sharp. This is the injury that actually happens with traction boards.
  7. Idle onto them. Low range, second gear, light steady throttle, wheels pointed straight. The tire should walk up the ramp. If it spins, you're off the throttle immediately — not easing off, off.
  8. Keep going to firm ground. Stopping on top of the boards means starting the whole recovery over. Plan where you're going before you move, and drive the full distance in one smooth pull.
  9. Walk back and collect the boards. They'll be buried, often a foot down. This is why leashes matter.

Why wheelspin destroys boards

A spinning tire on a stationary board is a friction welder. The lugs go from gripping to glazing to melting in a couple of seconds, and once they're rounded off, the board has lost the exact feature it exists for.

Genuine MAXTRAX are UV-stabilized reinforced nylon — the company cites University of Queensland testing where a board wrapped completely around a 33-inch tire without breaking. ARB's TRED Pro answers the same problem differently, with replaceable teeth in a polyolefin board rated to a 4.5-tonne (9,900 lb) vehicle, so a torn tooth is a part order instead of a new board. Budget boards use softer molded plastics with non-replaceable lugs, and owner reports of cracked boards and sheared lugs cluster heavily there.

That difference matters less than your right foot. A $100 board used with throttle discipline outlasts a $300 board used by someone who stands on it. Buy budget boards if the budget says so — know going in that they have less margin for the mistake, and inspect them before every trip.

XJ-specific notes

The Cherokee's low curb weight — roughly 3,200 to 3,600 lb depending on year and trim — is a real advantage here. Less mass to float over soft ground, less load per board, and a much better chance that airing down alone does the job. An XJ is one of the easier rigs to self-recover on sand.

Mounting is the harder problem. Boards are 45 inches long, so the options are a roof rack, a rear swing-out tire carrier, or flat against the cargo-area wall. Roof mounting is the common answer and the boards' weight is trivial against the factory rack's rating, but it puts them in full sun year-round — even UV-stabilized nylon degrades over seasons, and boards are among the most-stolen items off a parked overland rig, so locking pins are worth the money. Never mount boards where they can contact exhaust, and never strap them across the rear window where they block visibility.

Inside the cargo area, they're clean and dry but they come in caked with whatever you drove out of. A cheap contractor bag solves that.

What boards do besides recovery

They're a legitimate multi-tool, and this is part of why they earn the space:

  • Jack base. A Hi-Lift or bottle jack on sand sinks. A board flat under the foot spreads the load. This is a common and genuinely useful secondary job.
  • Bridging a rut or a washout. Two boards laid flat span a gap that would otherwise drop a wheel.
  • Wheel chocks. Lugs down, wedged at a tire.
  • A shovel. The leading edge on the better boards is shaped for scooping.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Rock does nothing for them. Hard rock already has all the friction a tire needs. If you're stuck on rock, you're high-centered or you've lost articulation — different problem, different tool.
  • Bottomless mud beats them. If the board sinks as fast as the tire, you're adding boards to the hole, not climbing out of it.
  • Two boards is a minimum, not a kit. Four is what you want for deep sand or a rig buried to the frame. Leapfrogging two works but costs time and daylight.
  • They don't fix a high-center. Get the weight back on the tires first.
  • They are consumable. Rounded-off lugs mean the board is finished, regardless of how intact the rest looks.

Why it works

  • The only recovery method that works genuinely solo — no second vehicle, no anchor point, no winch
  • Light enough that an XJ owner has no real reason to leave them behind on a soft-surface trip
  • Decisive in sand, mud, and snow, which is where most people get stuck in the first place
  • Doubles as a jack base, bridging ramp, chock, and shovel, so the storage space works more than one job
  • No stored energy — nothing under tension, so failure is undramatic compared to a strap or a winch line

Trade-offs

  • Useless on rock, which makes them poor value for a rig that only runs rocky trails
  • Bulky at 45 inches, and the good mounting locations on an XJ are all compromises
  • Wheelspin destroys them fast, and the failure is often permanent on budget boards
  • Buried boards have to be dug back out, which is real work at the end of a recovery
  • Frequently stolen off roof racks; locking mounts are close to mandatory if the rig parks unattended

Tools required

  • Shovel (the boards do not replace digging)
  • Tire gauge and deflator
  • Work gloves
  • Board leashes or light rope, 6-10 ft
  • Onboard air or a 12V compressor to air back up
