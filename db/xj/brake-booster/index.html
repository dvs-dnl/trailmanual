Jeep Cherokee XJ / Brakes

Brake Booster Replacement — XJ Cherokee

Difficulty 3/51.0–3.0 hrs$90–2201984-2001
Short answer

A dead booster means a rock-hard pedal and a truck that stops on leg strength alone — the swap is an hour or two of wrench time, and because the hydraulic system never opens, there's no bleeding afterward.

Confirm the booster is actually the problem before buying one. A hard pedal is more often a failed vacuum check valve ($15) or a cracked supply hose than a torn diaphragm — run the tests in the booster failure diagnosis guide first. If the diaphragm test fails (pedal doesn't drop when you start the engine with pressure held on it), or you hear a steady hiss from under the dash when you press the pedal, the booster itself is done.

Two fitment notes before you order. First, XJs ran a single-diaphragm booster for most of the run; 1995–1996 trucks got a dual-diaphragm unit, and many owners upgrade to the dual or a WJ Grand Cherokee booster while they're in there — if that interests you, read the master cylinder upgrade guide first, because bore size and booster capacity should be matched as a system. Second, some 1989–1991 XJs with factory ABS used a Bendix hydraulic booster system — a pump, accumulator, and integrated master on the firewall. That system is a different job entirely; most owners convert to the 1996+ vacuum setup, which means re-flaring three brake lines. This guide covers the standard vacuum booster.

The pleasant surprise of this job: the master cylinder pulls forward off the booster studs with the brake lines still attached. The hard lines have enough flex to move the master an inch or two — which means no fluid loss, no bleeding, and no cracked fittings. The unpleasant part is the four nuts inside the cabin. They live above the pedal assembly, you'll work by feel, and your neck will file a complaint.

Steps

  1. Confirm vacuum supply first. With the engine idling, the 4.0L should pull roughly 18–21 in-Hg at the booster hose. Low vacuum from a leak or tired engine makes a good booster feel dead — replacing the booster won't fix that.
  2. Disconnect the vacuum hose from the booster check valve. Inspect the valve and grommet while you're there. If the new booster doesn't ship with a check valve, transfer yours or install a new one — don't reuse a hardened grommet.
  3. Remove the two 13mm nuts holding the master cylinder to the booster. Pull the master forward off the studs and let it rest against the fender area, lines attached. Move it gently — flex the hard lines as little as possible, and don't let the master hang by the lines. Do not open any fittings.
  4. Move inside the cabin. Get under the dash on the driver's side with a flashlight. Find where the booster pushrod meets the brake pedal arm: a clevis pin secured by a retaining clip.
  5. Pop the retaining clip off the clevis pin and pull the pin. Needle-nose pliers or a small pick work. The clip is spring steel and launches into the darkest corner of the footwell if you let it — cup a hand over it.
  6. Remove the four 13mm nuts holding the booster to the firewall. This is the fight. A deep socket, 16 inches or more of extension, and a swivel joint reaching up past the pedal assembly is the combination that works. Penetrating oil helps on rust-belt trucks.
  7. Pull the booster out from the engine bay. Angle it around the master cylinder and lines. Compare it side-by-side with the replacement: stud pattern, pushrod length, and check valve location should match.
  8. Set the new booster onto the firewall with the pushrod through into the cabin. Start all four nuts by hand, then torque to 29 ft-lb (1997–2001 FSM spec; earlier manuals list 23 ft-lb — use your year's figure and don't gorilla them, the studs clamp sheet metal).
  9. Reconnect the pushrod to the pedal. Clevis pin through, retaining clip fully seated. Give the pedal a few strokes by hand and watch the pushrod — it must move freely with no bind and return fully. A booster pushrod that doesn't return drags the brakes.
  10. Reinstall the master cylinder on the booster studs. Torque the nuts to 17–19 ft-lb. Reconnect the vacuum hose to the check valve until it clicks home.
  11. Test before you drive. Engine off, pump the pedal to deplete vacuum — it should go hard. Hold pressure and start the engine — the pedal should sink noticeably as boost arrives. No hiss under the dash at rest or while holding the pedal. Then test at walking speed in the driveway before the street.

Why it works

  • No brake bleeding required — the hydraulic side never opens
  • Reman units with core exchange keep the cost reasonable
  • Same access work sets you up for a dual-diaphragm upgrade if the rig has bigger brakes coming

Trade-offs

  • The four under-dash nuts are genuinely miserable on a tall-socket day — budget patience, not strength
  • Bendix ABS trucks (1989–1991) need a full system conversion, not a booster swap
  • A reman booster is only as good as its diaphragm — buy from a vendor that honors the warranty

Tools required

  • 13mm deep socket, 3/8-drive ratchet, and at least 16" of extensions
  • Swivel / universal joint for the under-dash nuts
  • 13mm socket or wrench (master cylinder nuts)
  • Needle-nose pliers (pushrod retaining clip)
  • Flashlight or headlamp
  • Torque wrench (ft-lb range)
  • Small pick or flat screwdriver (clevis pin clip)
