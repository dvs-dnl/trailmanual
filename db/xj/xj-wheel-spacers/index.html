Jeep Cherokee XJ / Wheels & Tires

Wheel Spacer Safety on the XJ — Hub-Centric vs. Lug-Centric

Difficulty 2/51–2 hrs$79–1851984-1990, 1991-1995, 1996, 1997-2001
Short answer

A hub-centric bolt-on spacer with the correct 71.5mm register on both faces is safe on an XJ; a lug-centric slip-on spacer stacked on stock studs is the version that loses wheels.

The XJ runs a 5x114.3 (5x4.5") bolt pattern, a 71.5mm hub bore, and 1/2"-20 wheel studs on every year from 1984 through 2001 — imperial threads, not the M14x1.5 metric studs used on later JK and JL Jeeps. Get that wrong when you order extended studs or replacement lug nuts and nothing threads on. Spacers show up on an XJ build for two reasons: a lifted rig where the tire still kisses the fender lip at full lock, or a set of wheels bought at a good price with 4.5" backspacing when 3.75" was the target. Both are legitimate. What gets people hurt is not the spacer itself, it's picking the wrong style and running out of thread.

Hub-centric vs. lug-centric — the part that matters

The XJ hub has a machined register — a raised ring 71.5mm across — that the wheel's center bore drops onto. That register carries the wheel's weight and holds it concentric. The five studs are clamping hardware; they are not designed to locate the wheel. A lug-centric spacer has an oversized or non-existent center bore, so the wheel hangs on the studs and finds center only as you tighten the nuts in a star pattern. It will feel fine in the driveway and shake at 60 mph, and every rotation cycles the studs in bending rather than pure tension.

There's a second trap most listings gloss over: a spacer can be hub-centric on the inner face and lug-centric on the outer. The inner bore fits the XJ's 71.5mm register, but the outer face has no raised lip for your wheel to center on. That spacer is half of what you paid for. Confirm the product states a 71.5mm bore and a 71.5mm outer hub lip. Any spacer without both is a lug-centric setup wearing a hub-centric label.

Material matters too: forged or CNC-machined 6061-T6 billet aluminum only. Cast spacers exist at the bottom of the market and crack at the stud bosses.

Slip-on vs. bolt-on, and the thread engagement math

Slip-on (single-sided) spacers slide over your existing studs; the wheel then mounts on those same studs. Every millimeter of slip-on spacer subtracts a millimeter of thread engagement. The accepted rule is engagement at least equal to stud diameter — for a 1/2" stud at 20 threads per inch, that's 0.5" of engagement, or ten full threads inside the lug nut. Stock XJ studs have roughly that much spare length and no more, which means slip-on spacers are viable up to about 5-10mm and nowhere near 1.25". If you want a slip-on thicker than 10mm, press in extended 1/2"-20 studs first. That is not optional and it is where most failures start.

Bolt-on (double-sided) spacers solve this properly. The spacer bolts to the hub with its own set of lug nuts, and carries pressed-in studs on the outer face for the wheel. Every quality 1.25" and 1.5" XJ spacer is this style. You end up with two torque values and two sets of hardware to re-check — accept that as the cost of doing it right.

Installation

  1. Break the lug nuts loose while the wheel is on the ground.
  2. Raise the vehicle and support it on jack stands under the axle tube. Never under the pinch weld on a unibody XJ.
  3. Pull the wheel and wire-brush the hub face and register down to bare metal. Rust scale between the spacer and hub is the number one cause of runout on a rusty XJ, and two minutes here saves a wheel-vibration chase later.
  4. If pressing extended studs: drive the old studs out with a socket stack, pull the new 1/2"-20 studs in with a stack of washers and a lug nut, seating each one fully against the flange.
  5. Bolt-on spacers: seat the spacer on the hub register and torque its inner lug nuts in a star pattern to the manufacturer's spec — commonly 85 ft-lb. Do not substitute a guess here; the inner nuts sit inside the spacer and nobody ever checks them again.
  6. Mount the wheel on the spacer's outer studs. Torque in a star pattern to the XJ factory range of 80-110 ft-lb (the 1995 FSM calls out 109-150 N-m); 95 ft-lb dry and clean is a good working number for steel wheels, 85 for aluminum. Torque wrench, not impact gun.
  7. Repeat on all four corners, then re-torque both sets of nuts after the first 50-100 miles and after any trail run that got the wheels hot.

XJ-specific consequences

Moving the wheel outboard lengthens the moment arm on the front unit bearings, and XJ unit bearings are already a consumable — expect to shorten their life, more so at 1.75" than 1.25". The same geometry change increases scrub radius, which shows up as heavier steering feel and more feedback through the wheel over washboard. Ball joints see the same added load. None of that makes a 1.25" spacer a bad idea on a mild build; it does mean a 2" spacer on 33s is borrowing against parts you'll be replacing anyway.

Never stack two spacers to reach a thickness. Never run a spacer with visible corrosion pitting on the mating face. And check your state's fender coverage law before going wide — several states require the tread to sit under the bodywork.

Why it works

  • Cheap fix for minor rubbing without buying a new set of wheels
  • Correctly specified hub-centric bolt-on spacers run vibration-free at highway speed
  • Fully reversible — pull them and the rig is back to stock geometry
  • Corrects excess backspacing on a wheel you already own

Trade-offs

  • Slip-on spacers over 10mm need extended studs, and that step gets skipped constantly
  • Adds unsprung weight and shortens front unit bearing and ball joint life
  • Two sets of fasteners to torque and re-check instead of one
  • Not a substitute for buying the right backspacing when you're choosing wheels anyway
  • No-name lug-centric spacers are dangerous on a street-driven vehicle, not merely low quality

Tools required

  • floor jack
  • jack stands
  • torque wrench
  • socket set
  • breaker bar
  • wire brush
  • dial indicator (optional)
