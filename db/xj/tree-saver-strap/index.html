Jeep Cherokee XJ / Recovery

Tree Saver Strap — When and How

Difficulty 2/50–1 hrs$40–1401984-1990, 1991-1995, 1996, 1997-2001
Short answer

A tree saver is the short, wide, non-stretch strap that stands between your winch line and the anchor — it keeps the line off the bark, keeps the load spread, and keeps the rope from being wrapped back onto itself, which is the single most common way winch lines get destroyed.

Every winch pull needs something to pull against, and on most trails that something is a tree. Wrapping the winch line straight around the trunk does two kinds of damage at once: the line saws through the bark and cambium — often killing the tree over the following seasons — and the tree's bark grit saws back into the line. Then, to close the loop, people hook the winch hook onto the line itself, which side-loads the hook and crushes the rope at a single point.

The tree saver solves all of that with one piece of webbing and one shackle. It is the cheapest item in a winch kit and the one that decides whether the rest of the kit works as designed.

Flat webbing, typically 3 in wide and 8 ft long, with a reinforced sewn eye at each end and no hardware attached. Common ratings run from about 20,000 lb to 35,000 lb minimum breaking strength — far above what an XJ-sized winch can generate, because the strap gets loaded across corners and edges where a straight-line rating does not tell the whole story.

The defining property is that it does not stretch. Warn calls it a non-elastic strap, and that is the whole point: a winch pull is a slow, controlled, high-tension operation, and any stored energy in the anchor is energy waiting to be released if something parts. This is why a kinetic strap or recovery rope is not a substitute. Nylon kinetic gear is engineered to stretch 20 to 30 percent and store energy on purpose; putting it at the anchor end of a winch pull turns a static rigging problem into a loaded spring.

Width matters as much as strength. Three inches spreads the load over enough bark to keep the strap from cutting in, which is what "tree saver" refers to. A 2 in recovery strap pressed into service will bite deeper, and a chain will bite deeper still.

Diameter first. Look for a live, well-rooted trunk at least 8 to 10 in across measured where the strap will sit — bigger if the pull will be hard or repeated. Deep cracks, fungal shelves, insect boring, or a hollow sound when you knock on it all mean walk away. Dead trees and snags are out entirely; they fail without warning and they fail toward you.

Root conditions decide as much as trunk size. A 14 in trunk in saturated streambank soil will lever its own root ball out of the ground, while a 10 in hardwood in packed dirt will not move. Hardwoods like oak and maple hold better than fast-growing softwoods.

Height is leverage. Rig as low on the trunk as the strap will go — at the root flare if you can reach it. Every foot higher multiplies the bending moment on the trunk and on the root system.

Angle is the last check. You want the straightest line you can get between the fairlead and the anchor. A pull that comes in at a sharp angle side-loads the winch drum, piles the rope to one side, and puts a bending load into the tree instead of a straight tension load. If the only good anchor sits off-axis, a snatch block redirects the line cleanly — that is what snatch blocks are for.

Line wrapped directly around the trunk. Kills the tree, wrecks the line, and leaves no clean attachment point. This is the failure the tree saver exists to prevent.

Hook back onto the winch line. Warn flags this repeatedly and it is worth repeating: the hook crushes the rope at one point and loads in a direction it was never designed for. Synthetic line fails there first; wire rope kinks permanently.

Choking the strap on itself. Halves the effective bearing area, abrades the webbing against its own weave, and cuts into bark harder than a properly bridged strap. Use the shackle.

A kinetic strap or recovery rope used as the tree saver. Adds stretch to the one part of the system that should have none, and the narrower webbing cuts bark. Keep them in separate bags if you have to.

Winch line extension. When the anchor sits a few feet beyond reach and you would otherwise spool down to the last wrap, an 8 ft tree saver shackled inline buys the length. Keep the damper on the longest span.

Rock and stump anchors. The same strap works around a solid boulder or a well-rooted stump, but rock edges cut webbing far faster than bark does. Pad every contact point with a sleeve, a floor mat, or a section of old fire hose.

Bridle between two anchors. Two marginal trees can share a load through a pair of straps and a shackle, which also lets you steer the pull direction between them. Neither tree carries the full load, and the geometry is adjustable.

The XJ has no frame, so everything on the vehicle end of a winch pull lands in the unibody rails. That makes the anchor-side rigging worth getting right: a clean, low, straight pull puts a steady load into the winch plate, while a high or angled pull adds a twisting component the rails handle poorly.

An XJ's low weight is an advantage here. A Cherokee at roughly 3,200-3,600 lb stuck in mud rarely demands the peak output of an 8,000-9,500 lb winch, so a correctly rigged tree anchor is usually pulling well under the strap's rating. The margin gets eaten by bad geometry, not by the Jeep's mass.

Fairlead height on most XJ winch plates sits low, which helps — the line runs close to horizontal to a low tree anchor, which is exactly the geometry you want. Check that the line clears the bumper and any light bar mounts before loading it; a line that rubs an edge under tension is a line that fails there.

Rinse after muddy or sandy days and dry in shade. Grit driven into the weave abrades fibers internally where inspection will not find them, and UV degrades webbing over seasons regardless of use.

Retire the strap for cuts, pulled or fuzzed stitching at the sewn eyes, glazed or melted-looking fibers, chemical staining, or any spot where the weave has gone thin. The sewn eyes carry the load and they are where failures start, so inspect them first every time. Webbing is a consumable — replacing a $40 strap is cheaper than every alternative.

Steps

  1. Dig and air down first. Clear the tires and undercarriage, and drop to 15-18 psi on 31s. Half the winch pulls on a trail never needed to happen.
  2. Walk the anchor. Confirm trunk diameter, live wood, solid root ball, and a workable pull angle before any gear comes out of the bag.
  3. Put on heavy leather gloves. Used winch line — wire rope especially — develops barbs that will open a bare hand.
  4. Pass the tree saver around the trunk at the lowest point you can reach, with both sewn eyes meeting on the vehicle side.
  5. Bring both eyes together and connect them with one rated bow shackle or soft shackle. Do not pass one eye through the other and choke it — choking concentrates the load into a fraction of the webbing and grinds the strap against itself under tension.
  6. Attach the winch hook to that shackle, hook opening facing up so it cannot roll off under slack. With a screw-pin shackle, thread the pin fully, then back it off half a turn so it cannot seize once loaded.
  7. Free-spool the line out to the anchor — do not power the winch out to full length under load. Leave at least 5 wraps of line on the drum at all times.
  8. Lay the damper across the line roughly midway between the fairlead and the anchor. A line blanket, a heavy jacket, or a loaded gear bag all work.
  9. Clear everyone out of the line's path and out of the cone behind the anchor. Say out loud where nobody is to stand, and confirm it before touching the controller.
  10. Take up slack in short bursts until the line is taut, then stop and inspect: strap seated low and flat on the trunk, shackle aligned, rope spooling evenly, no bark crushing, no root lifting.
  11. Pull in short pulls with pauses. Watch the tree, the strap, and the line rather than the stuck vehicle. Winch pulls run for minutes, not seconds, and the winch motor needs the cool-down.
  12. Stop and reassess if the tree moves, the strap shifts up the trunk, or the line starts stacking against one drum flange. Re-rig rather than pulling through it.

Why it works

  • Prevents the two most expensive winching mistakes at once: a destroyed line and a killed tree
  • Cheapest item in a winch kit, and nothing else in the kit works correctly without it
  • Non-elastic construction keeps stored energy out of the anchor end of the pull
  • Doubles as a winch line extension, a rock anchor, and half of a two-tree bridle
  • Makes a legitimate anchor out of trees that would otherwise be off-limits on managed land

Trade-offs

  • Useless without a rated shackle, so it is really a two-item purchase
  • Needs a suitable tree; open desert, sand, and snowfields give you nothing to wrap
  • Webbing is a consumable that ages from UV and grit whether or not you use it
  • Adds a rigging step that gets skipped under pressure, which is exactly when skipping it costs the most
  • Some land-management areas restrict or prohibit tree anchors — check local rules before rigging

Tools required

  • Rated bow shackle, 3/4 in, stamped with a working load limit — or a soft shackle
  • Winch line damper or line blanket
  • Heavy leather gloves
  • Shovel
  • Edge protection (sleeve, rubber mat, or floor mat) for rock anchors
