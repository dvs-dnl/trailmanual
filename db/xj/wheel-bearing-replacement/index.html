Front Wheel Bearing Hub Replacement — XJ Cherokee

Difficulty 2/51.0–2.5 hrs$65–1401990-2001

Front wheel bearings on the XJ are sealed unit hub assemblies — when they go, you swap the whole hub. One side takes about an hour if the hardware cooperates.

The 1990–2001 XJ Cherokee uses a sealed, non-serviceable unit bearing on the front Dana 30. There's no packing, no adjustment — the whole assembly presses onto the stub axle and bolts to the steering knuckle face with three bolts. When the bearing fails, the fix is full hub replacement.

Most XJ front bearings die somewhere between 100,000 and 150,000 miles, though trail use and water crossings accelerate that timeline. The failure is usually audible before it's dangerous, but a badly worn front hub can contribute to steering wander and has been linked to feeding existing death wobble symptoms, so don't treat it as a low-priority item once you've confirmed the bearing is bad.

1984–1989 XJ note: Earlier models used a different bearing configuration — some years had serviceable tapered roller bearings rather than a unit assembly. This guide covers the 1990–2001 hub assembly. If you're on an '84–'89, confirm your bearing type before ordering parts.

Replace both sides together. If one front bearing is worn enough to make noise, the other has similar miles and similar stress history. The second hub costs $70 and saves you another hour under the truck a few months later.

A failing front hub sounds like a low growl or rumble that varies with vehicle speed, not engine RPM. The definitive test: change pitch when you gently swerve left and right at 40–50 mph. Loading the wheel (turning toward it) will quiet a bad bearing; loading the opposite side will amplify it. Bad right bearing = noise increases when turning right. Bad left bearing = noise increases when turning left.

With the truck on jack stands, grab the tire at 3 and 9 o'clock and rock it side-to-side. Any perceptible lateral play — not smooth rotation — points to the bearing. Also check 12 and 6 o'clock for up-and-down play, which is more likely a ball joint. On ABS-equipped XJs, a failing hub tone ring can also trigger the ABS light.

The XJ community has learned this the hard way: cheap off-brand hub assemblies fail fast. Bearings rated for 75 mph highway loads that see trail flex and water immersion need quality steel and proper sealing. Timken (HA590032) and SKF (BR930295) are the go-to choices. Figure an extra $15–20 over parts-counter generics for a unit that will actually last. If a supplier's hub comes in under $40, plan to do this job again in 12 months.

Steps

  1. Chock the rear wheels and break the front lug nuts loose before lifting. With the tire on the ground, crack each nut a quarter turn. The 30mm axle nut also benefits from a pre-soak with penetrating oil at this point — spray it down and give it a few minutes while you get set up.
  2. Raise the front axle and support it on jack stands under the axle tubes, not the control arms. The axle is the safe support point. Remove the wheel and set it aside.
  3. Remove the brake caliper and bracket. Two bracket bolts (13mm) hold the entire assembly. Hang the caliper on the coil spring or a hook — never let it dangle by the brake hose. Slide the rotor off; if it's rust-bonded to the hub, tap it with a dead-blow between the studs to break it free. Do not pry against the studs.
  4. Disconnect the ABS sensor if your XJ has one. There's a single mounting bolt and an electrical connector. The sensor tip sits above the hub tone ring. Pull the connector, unbolt the bracket, and tuck the sensor out of the way.
  5. Remove the cotter pin from the axle nut. Straighten both legs and pull it out with needle-nose pliers. Discard it — always use a new cotter pin on reassembly.
  6. Remove the axle nut. This is a 30mm prevailing torque nut torqued to 175 ft-lb from the factory — you need real torque to break it. An impact wrench handles this with no drama. If you're working with a breaker bar, have a helper press the brake pedal hard to lock the axle while you apply force. Keep the breaker bar straight to the nut to avoid rounding it. The axle nut is technically single-use due to its prevailing torque design — replace it with a new nut at reassembly rather than reusing it.
  7. Remove the three hub-to-knuckle bolts. These 13mm bolts pass through the knuckle from the back side, with the bolt heads behind the knuckle face. The rotor being off gives you access. Use a 13mm socket with a medium extension and a flex/universal joint adapter — the angle is awkward but manageable. Remove all three. If they're seized, hit them with penetrating oil and wait before fighting them. They will snap if you rush it.
  8. Slide the hub assembly off the axle stub shaft. On a truck with moderate mileage, it comes off with hand pressure. On an XJ that's seen rust or never had the hub off, it may be seized to the stub shaft. Do not hammer the axle shaft end — you'll damage the axle. Instead, work the hub side to side while pulling, or use a gear puller threaded to the hub face. Light PB Blaster on the stub shaft helps. Give it time.
  9. Clean the knuckle mating surface. Wire-brush the face where the new hub seats. Any rust or grit here causes hub runout, which leads to rotor pulse and a job that immediately needs redoing. Clean the stub shaft too.
  10. Install the new hub assembly. Slide it onto the stub shaft with the sensor hole (if ABS) in the correct orientation — facing up or toward the sensor bracket location. Start all three knuckle bolts by hand first, then snug them down evenly before torquing.
  11. Torque the hub bolts to 75 ft-lb. Work in a star pattern across the three bolts to seat the hub evenly against the knuckle face.
  12. Install a new axle nut and torque to 175 ft-lb. Lock the axle the same way you loosened it — brake pedal or impact. Once torqued, check that the cotter pin hole in the stub shaft aligns with the castellations on the nut. If it doesn't align at exactly 175 ft-lb, advance the nut slightly (never back it off) until the holes line up.
  13. Install a new cotter pin. Thread it through, bend both legs in opposite directions to lock it, and trim any excess with wire cutters.
  14. Reconnect the ABS sensor (if equipped). Plug in the connector, bolt the bracket, and route the harness clear of any rotating parts.
  15. Reinstall the rotor, brake caliper and bracket. Torque the caliper bracket bolts to 77 ft-lb. Reinstall the wheel and torque lugs to 85 ft-lb.
  16. Lower the truck and check the work. Before driving, pump the brake pedal several times until it firms up — the caliper pistons may have shifted slightly during removal. Drive cautiously for the first stop or two to ensure the brakes are bedded and the hub is quiet.

Why it works

  • Unit bearing design means no repacking, no adjustment — replacement is the only service required
  • Dana 30 hub assemblies are inexpensive and widely stocked
  • One side takes under two hours for most builders
  • Addresses both bearing noise and ABS sensor issues in one job (on ABS-equipped XJs)

Trade-offs

  • Seized hubs on high-mileage or rust-belt XJs can turn a straightforward job into a multi-hour fight
  • Hub bolts are in an awkward access position behind the knuckle — budget extra time on your first attempt
  • Quality matters — cheap bearings are false economy on a solid-axle rig with trail exposure
  • Axle nut requires significant torque; a proper impact wrench or a helper and a long breaker bar is required

Tools required

  • Floor jack and jack stands
  • 19mm socket or impact (lug nuts)
  • 30mm deep socket and breaker bar or impact wrench (axle nut)
  • 13mm socket, extensions, and flex/universal joint adapter (hub bolts)
  • Torque wrench (50–200 ft-lb range)
  • Needle-nose pliers (cotter pin)
  • Wire brush and rags
  • Penetrating oil (PB Blaster or equivalent)
  • Rubber mallet or dead-blow hammer
