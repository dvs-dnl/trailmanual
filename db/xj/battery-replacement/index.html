Battery Replacement and Terminal Cleaning

Difficulty 1/50.5–1.0 hrs$130–2801984-1990, 1991-1995, 1996, 1997-2001

Pull the old battery, clean every contact surface down to bright metal, drop the new one in, and torque the terminals. Most XJs run a Group 58 (pre-1998) or Group 34 (1998-2001) top-post battery. Plan an hour the first time, half that once you've done it.

A weak battery is the most common reason an XJ won't crank in the morning. Lead-acid batteries lose capacity gradually for two to four years, then drop off a cliff — usually on the first cold night below 40°F. The symptoms are a slow lazy crank, dim headlights at idle, dash warnings that flicker as accessories load the system, or a clicking starter solenoid that won't engage. By the time the no-start happens, the battery has been failing for weeks.

The job itself is short. The complication on an XJ is corrosion. The factory negative cable on the 4.0L runs from the battery to a 10mm bolt on the cylinder head, and that connection corrodes under the intake manifold heat. The positive cable bolts to the starter solenoid and to the fender-mounted PDC. Any of these contact points can grow a film of green or white oxidation that adds resistance — enough to mimic a dead battery even with a brand-new unit installed. Cleaning every terminal back to bright metal is what separates a battery swap that fixes the problem from one that hides it for another month.

Group sizes matter for fitment. Pre-1998 XJs came with a Group 58 battery and a shorter tray (about 9.5 inches). 1998-2001 XJs got a Group 34 in a slightly larger tray. A Group 34 won't drop into a pre-98 tray without modification — you either swap to the later tray (Crown 55155022, junkyard pull, or any 98+ XJ/WJ donor) or trim the front lip of the old tray. The popular upgrade is a Group 34/78 dual-post, which gives you side posts in addition to top posts — useful for hooking up winches, dual batteries, or auxiliary fuse blocks without stacking ring terminals.

Steps

  1. Park on a flat surface, set the parking brake, and let the engine cool. Open the hood and locate the battery on the driver's side of the engine bay.
  2. Put on safety glasses and gloves. Battery acid causes burns and ruins clothing on contact. Keep open flames and sparks away from the battery — venting hydrogen gas is flammable.
  3. Optional but smart: Plug a memory saver into the cigarette lighter or OBD-II port before disconnecting anything. This keeps the radio presets, clock, and ECU adaptive memory alive during the swap. Without one, the ECU re-learns idle and fuel trims over the next 50-100 miles — not fatal, but the XJ may idle rough for the first few days.
  4. Disconnect negative first. Loosen the 10mm nut on the negative (black) terminal clamp. Wiggle the clamp side to side and pull it straight up. If it's stuck, use a battery terminal puller — do not pry with a screwdriver, which can crack the case or short to the positive post. Tuck the negative cable to the side so it can't fall back onto the post.
  5. Loosen the 10mm nut on the positive (red) terminal clamp the same way. Pull the clamp off and tuck it aside.
  6. Remove the battery hold-down. The XJ uses a J-bolt and a small clamp bar across the front lip of the battery. Loosen the 13mm nut at the top of the J-bolt several turns — you don't need to remove it fully. Lift the clamp bar off the battery and swing the J-bolt out of the tray.
  7. Lift the old battery straight up and out. A Group 34 weighs about 45 pounds — lift with your legs, not your back. Set it on cardboard, not on the driveway directly.
  8. Inspect the battery tray. Look for cracks, deep corrosion holes (the acid eats through the metal floor over time), or rusted-through mounting points. A corroded tray will eat a new battery in two years. If it's bad, replace it now — drop the old battery off at the auto parts store and order a Crown 55155022 (98-01) or scrub and POR-15 the original.
  9. Wipe out the tray with rags. If there's white or green corrosion, mix a tablespoon of baking soda in a cup of water and pour it on the affected area — it will fizz as it neutralizes the acid. Wipe clean and dry thoroughly.
  10. Clean the cable terminals. This is the step most people skip and the one that determines whether the new battery actually solves your starting problem. Use a wire brush or a dedicated battery terminal cleaner tool (the brass cone style with internal and external bristles) to scrub the inside of each clamp until you see bright metal. Both clamps — positive and negative.
  11. Trace the negative cable back to the cylinder head. There's a ring terminal bolted to the head with a 10mm bolt, usually near the rear of the intake manifold. Loosen the bolt, pull the ring terminal off, and clean both the ring terminal and the bolt threads. Heavy corrosion here causes slow crank, dim dash lights, and random electrical gremlins. Reinstall with the bolt torqued to 84 in-lb (7 ft-lb).
  12. Trace the positive cable to the starter solenoid (on the bell housing) and to the PDC (the black plastic box on the driver's-side fender). Check both terminations for corrosion. If either is heavily corroded, loosen, clean, and reinstall — but be aware the starter cable is live to the battery side at all times, so this work happens with both battery clamps already off (which they are at this point).
  13. Set the new battery into the tray with the positive post toward the front of the vehicle. Verify orientation against the cables before you start tightening anything — if the cables won't reach, you have it backward.
  14. Reinstall the hold-down. Swing the J-bolt back into its slot at the front of the tray, set the clamp bar across the front lip of the battery, and snug the 13mm nut. The battery should not move when you push on it side to side. Do not overtighten — crushing the case cracks the plastic and ruins the battery.
  15. Optional anti-corrosion step: Slide a felt anti-corrosion washer over each post before installing the cable clamps. These washers are pre-soaked with neutralizing compound and dramatically slow the green crud that grows on top-post terminals.
  16. Reconnect positive first. Slide the positive clamp onto the post, work it down until it bottoms out, and torque the 10mm nut to 80 in-lb (about 7 ft-lb). Hand-tight plus a quarter-turn with a short wrench is close — using a torque wrench prevents stripping the soft lead post.
  17. Reconnect the negative clamp the same way. Torque to 80 in-lb. Confirm both clamps are seated all the way down on the posts.
  18. Spray both terminals with battery terminal protectant or coat with dielectric grease. This blocks oxygen and moisture, which is what creates the corrosion in the first place.
  19. Verify the install. Headlights on bright, then crank the engine. The crank should be quick and strong with no hesitation. Dash lights should hold steady — not dim noticeably during cranking.
  20. With the engine running, set a multimeter to 20V DC and probe across the battery posts. You should read 13.8 to 14.6 V, confirming the alternator is charging the new battery correctly. If you read 12.6 V or less with the engine running, the alternator isn't charging — diagnose that before driving.
  21. Take the old battery to any auto parts store (AutoZone, O'Reilly, Advance, NAPA) for free recycling. Most will refund the $15-20 core charge if you bought the new one there.

Why it works

  • Solves no-start and slow-crank problems immediately when the battery is the actual fault.
  • Cleaning the cable terminations and head ground while you're in there cures a long list of XJ electrical gremlins — dim dash lights, slow power windows, intermittent gauge issues, even rough idle on 4.0L Renix engines (1987-1990) that are sensitive to voltage drop.
  • A modern Group 34 with 800+ CCA gives noticeably stronger cold-weather cranking than the original 500-550 CCA Group 58, especially on high-compression rebuilt engines or with winter-grade oil.

Trade-offs

  • A pre-1998 XJ won't fit a Group 34 without modifying or replacing the battery tray. Plan for that upfront rather than discovering it at the parts counter — measure the existing tray (about 9.5 inches long is the small one, 10.25 inches is the 98+ tray) or check the group size sticker on the old battery.
  • The negative cable head bolt is awkward to reach. On 4.0L engines it sits behind the intake manifold runners, and a long extension plus a swivel makes the difference between a five-minute job and a knuckle-busting hour. Don't skip it — that one connection causes more mystery problems than the battery itself.
  • Disconnecting the battery wipes the radio presets, the clock, and (on 1996+ OBD-II XJs) the ECU's learned fuel trims. Idle will be slightly off for the first 50-100 miles as the ECU relearns. Use a memory saver to avoid this entirely.
  • If the new battery cranks the same as the old one, the battery wasn't the problem. Check the starter draw, the alternator output, and every ground connection before assuming you got a bad new battery. The battery/alternator/starter triangle exists for a reason.

Tools required

  • 10mm socket and ratchet
  • 13mm socket and ratchet
  • 8-inch socket extension
  • battery terminal puller (recommended)
  • wire brush or battery terminal cleaner tool
  • small flat-blade screwdriver
  • rags
  • baking soda and water (for neutralizing corrosion)
  • safety glasses
  • nitrile or rubber gloves
  • memory saver (optional but recommended)
