Beadlocks — Do You Actually Need Them?

Difficulty 2/54–8 hrs$1200–24001984-1990, 1991-1995, 1996, 1997-2001

True beadlocks bolt the tire's outer bead to the wheel so you can run single-digit pressure in the rocks without spinning a tire off the rim — and if you're not running single-digit pressure, you don't need them.

Here's the problem beadlocks solve: at 12-15 psi a standard wheel holds its bead fine. Drop to 5-8 psi for a technical rock trail and the sidewall folds, the tire squirms on the rim, and a hard side-load — a ledge, a rut wall, a spotter's "crank it hard left" — can pop the bead off the wheel. Now you're re-seating a bead with a ratchet strap and someone's compressor halfway up a trail. A true beadlock ends that: an outer ring bolts through the wheel and mechanically clamps the outer bead so it cannot walk off, no matter how low you air down.

Be clear about what's actually clamping. "DOT beadlock-style" wheels with fake bolt rings are cosmetic — they hold nothing. Bead-retention designs like Method's Bead Grip use a grooved bead seat to grip harder than a plain wheel; they're street-legal, cheaper, and a real middle ground for the 10-12 psi crowd, but they are not a mechanical clamp. Internal beadlocks (Hutchinson, Stazworks) clamp both beads and are DOT-compliant, at roughly double the price. Classic outer-ring beadlocks are not DOT-approved, which most states tolerate and some technically don't — know your local enforcement before bolting them on a daily driver.

The XJ angle: most built XJs live on 15s with a 5x4.5 bolt pattern, and 15" true beadlocks are a smaller market than 17s — Trail Ready's HD15 and Raceline's Monster line are the proven options, while the popular Method 105 only comes in 17". Factor wheel weight too: a beadlock wheel plus ring adds roughly 8-12 lb per corner of unsprung mass over a comparable aluminum wheel, which the XJ's 4.0L and (likely worn) suspension will feel on the street.

  1. Mount the inner bead conventionally — lube it and push it over the drop center. On stubborn tires, have a shop seat the inner bead; only the outer bead belongs to the bolt ring.
  2. Set the outer bead flat on the wheel's clamp surface, then place the ring over it. Hand-start four bolts at the 12, 3, 6, and 9 o'clock positions before starting the rest.
  3. Snug all bolts in a criss-cross pattern, then torque in stages — Method's guide for its wheels runs 50 in-lb, 100 in-lb, 150 in-lb criss-cross, then around the circle to a final 221 in-lb (about 18 ft-lb). Trail Ready and Raceline publish their own numbers; use your manufacturer's spec, not a forum's.
  4. Never run bolts down in one pass and never use an impact — an uneven ring warps, pinches the bead, and can let go under pressure.
  5. Air up to street pressure and check the ring gap is even all the way around. An uneven gap means the bead is pinched — deflate and restart.
  6. Re-check ring bolt torque after the first drive, after the first trail day, and every few wheeling trips after that. Loose-ring failures are a maintenance failure, not a design failure.

For an XJ that runs 33s or larger on rock trails at 5-8 psi, beadlocks earn their cost the first time the trail gets off-camber. For overland, forest roads, sand, and mostly-dirt use at 12-15 psi, they're overkill — a standard wheel with proper backspacing holds fine at those pressures, and the money goes further in tires, gears, or armor. If you're between those camps, a bead-retention wheel like the Bead Grip design covers the 10-12 psi range without the ring maintenance or the legality question.

Why it works

  • Air down to single digits with the outer bead mechanically clamped
  • Rings are rebuildable and replaceable after rock damage
  • Lets a rock-crawling XJ use the full footprint of its tires

Trade-offs

  • Not DOT-approved (outer-ring style) — a legality gray zone for street use
  • Ring bolts need staged torque at install and periodic re-checks forever
  • $250-$450 per wheel, plus added unsprung weight the street manners pay for
  • 15" true beadlock options for the XJ's 5x4.5 pattern are limited

Tools required

  • Torque wrench with inch-pound range (beadlock ring bolts)
  • Torque wrench with foot-pound range (lug nuts)
  • Tire mounting lube or soapy water
  • Valve core tool
  • Air compressor
