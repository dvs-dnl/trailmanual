The 2012–2013 JK Cylinder Head Failure: How to Spot It, and What It Costs

On a 2012–2013 Wrangler JK with the 3.6L Pentastar, a tick from the driver’s side of the engine combined with misfire codes on cylinders 2, 4 or 6 points to the known left cylinder head failure — premature valve guide and valve seat wear — and not to spark plugs or coils. Those three cylinders are all on the left bank. Three same-bank misfires is the signature, and it is the reason a lot of owners spend $600 on ignition parts before anyone says the word “head.”

The part-level test is the Julian date code stamped into the head casting: below 2062 — day 206 of 2012 — is the original design, and 2062 or higher is the revised one with hardened guides and seats. Model year is only a rough screen, because Chrysler phased the new head into production partway through 2012, so two JKs built weeks apart can carry heads from either side of the change. And the warranty answer, in 2026: the 10-year/150,000-mile extension has run out on every affected vehicle, so you are paying for this one.

September 28, 2026 · 10 min read
The direct answer

Driver’s-side tick plus P0302, P0304 or P0306 on a 2012–2013 JK means check the left head before you buy ignition parts. Confirm with a leak-down test — 25% or more leakage on an affected cylinder is the number Chrysler’s own procedure used. A head stamped 2062 or higher is the revised casting and not the failure. Reman left head with camshafts runs around $725; a shop doing the whole job quotes roughly $2,800–$3,200. Warranty coverage expired years ago.

Why only the left head, and only those three cylinders

The 3.6L Pentastar is a 60-degree V6 with three cylinders per bank. Cylinders 1, 3 and 5 sit on the right; 2, 4 and 6 sit on the left, which on a left-hand-drive JK is the driver’s side. Early left heads had a thermal hot spot near the exhaust valve guides. The guides and seats wore faster than they should have, the valves stopped sealing against the seat, compression on that cylinder dropped, and the cylinder began to misfire. Chrysler described the cause as a combination of rarely occurring factors including fuel quality and drive cycle, which is corporate for “we know where it happens but not reliably why.”

Understanding the mechanism does most of the diagnostic work for you. Worn guides on one bank cannot produce a misfire on the other bank. So if your codes are P0302, P0304 or P0306 — alone or in combination, often alongside P0300 for a random misfire — the fault is confined to the left bank, and the head is a live suspect. If you are throwing P0301, P0303 or P0305, you are looking at something else entirely.

Worth keeping in proportion: Chrysler put the affected population at roughly half a percent of early Pentastars — on the order of 5,000 engines out of the first million built. This is a real, documented, expensive failure, not a certainty on every early JK. The 3.6L in a 2012 Wrangler is not a grenade. It is an engine with one known casting problem that you can test for.

Three different noises get called “the Pentastar tick”

Conflating these is the single most expensive mistake owners make on this engine, because two of the three are not the head and one of them is not a failure at all.

What it is Years How it presents
Left head valve guide/seat wear 2012–2013 JK Tick from the driver’s side, then misfire codes on 2, 4 or 6. Low compression on the affected cylinder. Gets worse, never better.
Rocker arm / lash adjuster tick 2014–2018 JK and later Ticking that can come and go with oil temperature. A collapsed rocker will set a misfire too, but it is a valvetrain part, not the casting. Correct oil viscosity and a disciplined change interval matter here.
Normal engine noise All Injector and exhaust manifold noise, loudest at cold idle, unchanged for years, no codes, no performance loss. Not a fault.

The sorting question is whether there are codes and whether they cluster on one bank. A tick with no check engine light and no misfire history on a 2015 JK is almost certainly the third row. A tick with three left-bank misfire codes on a 2012 is the first.

How to confirm it, in the order that costs least

Work from cheap to expensive and stop when you have an answer. Do not start by buying parts.

1. Pull the codes and write down which cylinders. A $25–40 OBD2 reader that shows live data and per-cylinder misfire counters does everything you need here. You do not need a dealer-level scan tool for this diagnosis. If you already own a basic reader, use it. Our JK OBD2 diagnostics entry covers the procedure.

2. Listen at cold start, from the fender. Start it cold, stand at the driver’s-side front wheel, and listen. The head failure ticks loudest cold. And note this: no check engine light is not proof of a healthy head. Guides can wear far enough to tick well before the misfire counter crosses the threshold that sets a code.

3. Move a coil to prove the fault stays put. Swap the coil from the misfiring cylinder onto a healthy one and clear the codes. If the misfire follows the coil, you found a coil. If it stays on the original cylinder, the ignition side is ruled out and the fault is mechanical. This costs nothing but your time and it is the step most shops skip.

4. Compression, then leak-down. This is the confirmation. A compression tester tells you the affected cylinder is down; a leak-down tester tells you where the pressure is going. Chrysler’s own procedure used 25% or more leakage on the affected cylinder as the threshold for replacing the head. Air escaping out the exhaust is a burnt or unsealed exhaust valve, which is exactly the failure mode. Low compression on 2, 4 or 6 with good plugs and coils and 25%-plus leakage is your answer.

Reading the head: the 2062 date code

Chrysler engineered hardened valve guides and seats into a revised left head and phased it into production during 2012. The dividing line is a Julian date stamped into the casting — the three-digit day of the year plus the last digit of the year. 2062 means day 206 of 2012, roughly July 24. Below that number is the original design; 2062 or higher is the revised one.

Identifier Original (at risk) Revised (fixed)
Julian date stamp Below 2062 2062 or higher
Part number suffix Ends AA through AG 05184445AH and later (AI, AJ, AN, AO)
Service assembly number — 68141353AC / RL141353AC and higher also circulate for the left head

Two honest caveats about using the stamp. First, head date is not vehicle build date. The change went into production mid-2012, and a JK assembled anywhere near that window could have received a head from either batch. Nearly every article on this subject gets that backwards and tells readers to check the year. Check the casting. Second, on a fully assembled engine in the vehicle the stamp sits behind heat shielding and plumbing, and plenty of owners cannot get a clean read on it without removing parts. If you cannot see the stamp, the misfire pattern and a leak-down test will tell you what you need to know, and a dealer parts department can cross-reference your VIN for what shipped on the vehicle.

The warranty is almost certainly gone — ask anyway

Chrysler covered this failure under service bulletin 09-002-14 and warranty extension X56, which stretched coverage on the 3.6L left cylinder head from the base 5-year/100,000-mile powertrain term out to 10 years or 150,000 miles from the in-service date, whichever came first. For the Wrangler the covered years were 2012 and 2013. Some sources cite an earlier, shorter extension of 7 years/70,000 miles; the 10/150 figure is what appears on the warranty bulletin filed with NHTSA.

Either way, the arithmetic in 2026 is unkind. A 2013 JK sold new in 2013 aged out of a 10-year term in 2023, and most of these vehicles crossed 150,000 miles long before that. The top search results for this problem still tell readers to check whether they are covered, which was good advice in 2019 and is close to useless now. Run your VIN past a dealer regardless — it costs a phone call, records of prior goodwill repairs do exist, and reimbursement provisions applied to owners who paid out of pocket before learning of the extension. Do not build your budget around a yes.

What it costs when you pay for it

Two numbers matter: what a shop charges to do the whole job, and what the parts cost if you do it yourself.

Shop, whole job. RepairPal’s Jeep Wrangler cylinder head replacement estimate currently runs $2,795–$3,199. Treat that as a floor rather than a quote: it is a model-level Wrangler figure, not Pentastar-specific, and a left head on this engine carries labor a generic estimate does not capture. Owners who have had both heads done at independent shops have reported bills near $6,000.

Parts, doing it yourself. A remanufactured left head with camshafts, rockers and lifters installed lists at around $725 from one specialist supplier, with a two-year warranty and no upfront core charge. Prices move and suppliers vary in what they include, so get two or three quotes and confirm the unit carries an AH-or-later casting before you buy. Add gaskets, bolts, coolant and oil.

The ruling-out parts, for context. A shop spark plug service on a JK runs about $410–$524 and ignition coil replacement about $262–$370. Both are worth doing on a high-mileage 3.6L on their own merits, and both are far cheaper than a head — which is exactly why they get thrown at this problem first. Spend that money deliberately, not as a guess.

If you are shopping for a 2012–2013 JK

Add two items to the test drive. Start the engine cold yourself — if the seller has it warmed up when you arrive, that is worth noticing — and listen at the driver’s front fender. Then plug in a reader and check stored and pending codes plus misfire counters, not only whether the light is on. A seller who has cleared codes before your visit leaves the counters behind.

If it checks out clean, this is not a reason to walk. On a $16,000–24,000 Pentastar JK an uncovered head job is a meaningful fraction of the purchase price, which makes it a negotiating fact rather than a disqualification — provided the asking price already reflects the risk and the rest of the Jeep stands up. If it presents with the tick and the codes, price the repair in at the shop figure and be prepared for the seller to disagree. Our buyer’s playbook covers how to hold that line. And if you are weighing a newer platform instead, the JL’s 3.6L does not carry this defect — see the JL used buyer’s guide.

If you already own one

Confirm before you commit. Codes, coil swap, then leak-down — in that order. Once it is confirmed, be clear-eyed about the repair itself, because this is where the honest answer diverges from the encouraging one. Replacing a left head on the 3.6L means pulling the timing cover and re-timing the engine. That requires cam-locking and chain-retention tools, a service procedure followed exactly, and patience with torque sequences that do not tolerate improvisation. Get the timing wrong and you turn a $725 part into a new engine.

Home mechanics do this job and do it well. Nobody does it in an afternoon. Plan a full weekend minimum with the engine down, budget for the specialty tools or a rental set, and read the full service procedure start to finish before you loosen the first bolt. If that reads as more than you want to take on, paying a shop is a defensible call and roughly a $2,000 premium over parts. Either way, you now know what you are dealing with, which is the part most owners are missing when they hand over a credit card for their third set of coils.
