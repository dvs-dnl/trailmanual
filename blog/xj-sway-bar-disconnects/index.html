XJ Sway Bar Disconnects: When They Help, When They Don’t

The front sway bar on your XJ is doing two jobs that fight each other. On pavement it keeps the body flat through corners. On the trail it ties the front axle to the frame and stops it from flexing, which is exactly what you don’t want when one tire is climbing a rock and the other is dropping into a hole. Disconnecting the bar frees the axle to articulate — up to roughly 30% more travel — and keeps tires planted where a connected bar would be lifting them off the ground. Whether you should spend money on quick disconnects, though, depends entirely on how the truck gets used.

July 29, 2026 · 7 min read
The direct answer

Quick disconnects are worth it if your XJ drives to the trail and wheels hard enough to lift a tire. They make disconnecting the front bar a 60-second pin-pull instead of a wrench session, and reconnecting for the drive home takes the same. They are not worth it on a street-only XJ (the bar should stay connected anyway), and a trail-only rig can unbolt the factory end links for free. Expect $60–$180 for a quality kit matched to your lift height. The hard rule: reconnect before pavement, every time — an XJ without a front sway bar leans hard and can lose control in an emergency swerve.

What the front sway bar actually does

A sway bar — anti-roll bar, if you want the engineering term — is a torsion spring connecting the left and right sides of the front axle to the frame through a pair of end links. When both wheels move together over a bump, the bar rotates freely and does nothing. When one wheel moves relative to the other — body roll in a corner, or one tire climbing while the other drops — the bar twists and resists. On the street that resistance is what keeps a tall, softly sprung XJ feeling composed through a freeway ramp. On the trail, the same resistance fights the axle every time the terrain gets uneven, unloading tires that need weight on them for traction.

This is why the XJ’s front suspension flexes so much better with the bar out of the equation. Pull the end links on even a bone-stock XJ and the difference on an RTI ramp or a crossed-up section of trail is dramatic — commonly quoted at up to 30% more articulation, and consistent with what stock-height owners report. The coil-sprung front axle has real travel available; the bar is the main thing holding it back. The leaf-sprung rear is a different story — most XJs have no rear bar at all, and the leaf packs themselves limit rear flex, which makes front articulation matter even more.

When disconnects earn their money

The case for quick disconnects is one specific truck: an XJ that is driven on the road and wheeled regularly. That truck needs the bar connected on pavement and disconnected on the trail, which means swapping states at the trailhead twice per trip. You can do that with the factory end links and a couple of wrenches — the hardware is a bolt at each end — but the factory bushings fight you, the bolts corrode, and after the fifth trip you will stop bothering. A disconnect kit replaces the end links with links that separate at a pull-pin: pull two pins, swing the bar up, secure it with the supplied straps or lanyards, and drive on. Sixty seconds each way. The kit is not buying you capability the wrenches don’t have — it is buying you the certainty that you will actually do it, in both directions.

If that is your truck, buy once. Match the link length to your lift height first — too short or too long and the links bind or the bar sits at a bad angle. Common fitments start around 2–3 inches of lift, with dedicated 4–6 inch versions for taller trucks.

Which kit

The budget pin-style kits from Rough Country run cheap — often under $60 — and they do disconnect with a pin pull. The honest caveat from long-term owners: the joint design has a reputation for developing slop within a year of regular use, at which point they clunk. If the truck wheels a few times a year, they are serviceable. If it wheels monthly, spend more once instead.

JKS Quicker Disconnects are the long-standing answer for a truck that gets used: heavier-gauge steel, a sturdier hanger design, and greasable fittings so the joints stay tight instead of wearing loose. The RockJock/Currie CE-9141 (about $177) is the other buy-once option — machined from 7/8-inch solid steel, greasable Dura-Thane bushings, stainless lynch pins, sized for XJs with 4–6 inches of lift. Either will outlast the truck’s next three owners. Whichever you pick, the lift-height fitment matters more than the brand.

When to skip them

A street-only XJ has no use for disconnects — the bar should never come off, so there is nothing to disconnect. Money better spent on tires or the cooling system. A stock-height truck that sees mild forest roads is close to the same case: graded dirt and two-track don’t cross the axle up enough for the bar to cost you traction, and the factory end links are fine.

A trail-only rig — trailered to the trailhead, never driven on pavement — also doesn’t need a disconnect kit, because it never needs to reconnect quickly. Unbolt the factory end links, zip-tie the bar up, and put the kit money elsewhere. Some dedicated trail XJs delete the front bar entirely; that is a defensible choice for a rig that genuinely never sees the street, and only for that rig.

The rule that isn’t negotiable

Reconnect before pavement. Every trip, no exceptions. An XJ is tall, softly sprung, and short of wheelbase, and without the front bar it leans dramatically in any quick maneuver. Around town at parking-lot speeds you might not notice much — which is exactly how people talk themselves into leaving it disconnected. The problem shows up in the one moment you can’t schedule: a swerve at road speed to miss a stopped car or something in the lane. Experienced XJ owners are unanimous on this, and the physics backs them: body roll that would be caught by the bar instead keeps building, and the truck can go over. The pull-pin convenience of disconnects exists precisely so this rule costs you nothing to follow. Make reconnecting part of airing up, and it becomes automatic.

Quick reference
  • What you gain disconnected: up to ~30% more front articulation; tires stay planted on crossed-up terrain
  • Worth buying if: the XJ drives to the trail and wheels regularly — the kit makes the swap a 60-second pin pull
  • Skip if: street-only (bar stays on), mild dirt roads only, or trail-only (unbolt the factory links for free)
  • Fitment: match link length to lift height — common kits fit 2–3″+ of lift; CE-9141 fits XJs at 4–6″
  • Budget pin kits: work, but joints commonly wear loose within a year of regular use
  • Buy-once kits: JKS Quicker Disconnects or RockJock CE-9141 (~$177) — greasable joints, heavy steel
  • Rear axle: most XJs have no rear bar; leaf springs limit rear flex — front articulation is where the gains are
  • Hard rule: reconnect before pavement, every time — no front bar means severe body roll and rollover risk in an emergency swerve

The honest verdict

Sway bar disconnects are one of the rare mods where the honest answer is “it depends” and the dependency is clean: they exist for the truck that lives in both worlds. If your XJ commutes during the week and crawls on the weekend, a quality disconnect kit is one of the best dollars-per-trail-improvement purchases you can make — more flex, planted tires, and no temptation to leave the bar off for the drive home. If your truck lives in only one world, street or trail, the kit solves a problem you don’t have.

And if you do buy, buy the greasable kit once instead of the pin-and-slop kit twice. The parts are visible from the driver’s seat of every trail line you pick — you will use them more than almost anything else you bolt to the truck.
