Is the Bronco Sasquatch Package Worth It? The Honest Math

This is the option box people agonize over on the configurator, and the answer is not a matter of taste. It comes down to one question you can answer before you ever sit in the truck: are you going to run 35-inch tires? Everything else — the price, the fuel economy hit, the ride, the resale argument — follows from that.

August 3, 2026 · 9 min read
The direct answer

If you intend to run 35s, buy Sasquatch from the factory. The 4.70 gears, the front electronic locker and the stronger Dana M210 front axle cost more to add afterward than the package costs new — replicating the content in the aftermarket lands somewhere around $9,700 to $18,000 with labor, against a factory price of roughly $4,180 to $8,460 depending on trim. If you are staying near stock tire size and the truck mostly sees pavement, skip it. You will pay for 35-inch rugged-terrain tires and 4.70 gearing every mile in fuel, noise and steering effort, and you will not get the capability back.

What is actually in the box

Sasquatch is not a tire and wheel package with a badge. It is a drivetrain and suspension configuration, and understanding which parts of it are expensive to replicate is the whole argument:

  • 35-inch tires — 315/70R17 rugged-terrain rubber, Goodyear Wrangler Territory MT on current builds
  • 17-inch beadlock-capable wheels — matte black alloy, so you can air down hard without unseating a bead once you fit the optional rings
  • Front and rear electronic locking differentials — the front locker is the piece that matters most
  • 4.70 final drive ratio — matched to the 35s from the factory
  • Position-sensitive Bilstein monotube dampers — firmer at the end of travel, compliant in the middle
  • High-clearance suspension and high-clearance fender flares
  • Dana M210 front axle in place of the M190 used on base and mid trims

That last line is the one buyers skim past and regret. The front axle upgrade and the locker that lives inside it are not a bolt-on afternoon. A front M210 assembly with 4.70 gears and a locker is available as a standalone unit, but by the time you have bought it, matched the rear ratio to it, and paid someone to set up both, you have spent more than the option cost and you have done it without a factory warranty on the setup.

The pricing, and why it swings so much

Sasquatch does not cost the same on every trim, which confuses people comparing notes online. Recent model-year pricing runs roughly:

  • Base: around $8,460 — the highest number, because on Base the package is carrying the most content the trim does not already have
  • Big Bend: around $4,180, but the Black Diamond package is a prerequisite, so the real out-the-door delta is larger than the sticker suggests
  • Badlands and Outer Banks: around $6,475
  • Badlands Wildtrak package: Sasquatch is included — four-door only

Treat those as the shape of the pricing, not gospel. Ford moves option content between trims almost every model year, and the 2026 lineup already shuffled the Wildtrak back in as a Badlands package and restructured what sits above and below it. Pull the current order guide for the exact configuration you are building before you commit — and when you compare against a non-Sasquatch build, compare the delta, not the option price. A Badlands already has a rear locker and a disconnecting front sway bar, so on that trim you are really paying for the front locker, the M210, the 4.70s, the Bilsteins, and the wheel and tire package.

The case for: the aftermarket math does not work

Say you buy a non-Sasquatch Bronco with 3.73 or 4.46 gears and decide two years in that you want 35s. Here is what that actually costs, in the ranges shops quote:

  • 35-inch tires and wheels: $2,500–$4,000
  • Re-gearing front and rear: $1,500–$3,000 in parts and setup, and $3,000 is the number people who have done it quote more often than $1,500
  • Front locker: $1,500–$3,000 installed, more if the front axle needs upgrading to carry it
  • Shocks: $1,200–$2,000
  • Lift or leveling components, calibration and labor: $3,000–$8,000 depending on how deep you go

That is $9,700 on the low end and closer to $18,000 built properly, spread over months of shop appointments. Against $6,475 rolled into the finance term with a factory warranty behind it, the factory package is not close. There is also the mistake people make when they try to split the difference: running 35s on 3.73 gears without re-gearing. It is the most common rookie error in this hobby. You lose throttle response, the transmission hunts, and fuel economy gets worse than it would have with correct gearing — because the engine spends its life outside the range it was calibrated for.

The case against: what it costs you every day

Sasquatch is not free capability, and the honest version of this article says so plainly.

Fuel economy. The 35-inch rugged-terrain tires add rotating mass and rolling resistance, and 4.70 gears raise cruise RPM against the 3.73 or 4.46 in a non-Sasquatch truck. Owners regularly report highway averages in the mid-teens. One commonly cited pattern: mid-teens on the highway against high-teens around town, with a drop of three to four miles per gallon between a 70 mph cruise and an 80 mph cruise. Aerodynamics does most of that, but the tires and gearing make the penalty steeper than it would otherwise be. If you commute long highway miles, run the numbers on your actual annual mileage before you decide the option is cheap.

Noise and road manners. A 315-wide rugged-terrain tire is loud at speed and it tramlines on grooved pavement. Steering effort at parking speeds goes up. None of this is a defect — it is the cost of the tire — but people who test drove a non-Sasquatch truck and ordered a Sasquatch are sometimes surprised on delivery day.

Wheel and tire choices narrow. The Sasquatch fender flares and factory wheel offset are a matched set. Aftermarket wheels for a Sasquatch truck are typically specified at 17x9 with roughly zero offset for exactly this reason. Fit a wheel with meaningfully different backspacing and you buy rubbing at the flares or the front control arms at full lock. It is workable, but it is not the open field of options non-Sasquatch owners have.

Consumables cost more. Four 315/70R17 rugged-terrains, replaced on a normal wear cycle, are a materially larger bill than the 32s or 33s on the trim below. Add the spare when it eventually ages out.

Decide in thirty seconds
  • Buy it if 35s are the plan, now or within a couple of years
  • Buy it if 37s are the eventual plan — 4.70 already suits them and the front locker is already there
  • Buy it if you want a front locker at all; that is the hardest single piece to add later
  • Skip it if the truck is mostly a highway commuter with occasional dirt roads
  • Skip it if you are staying at or near the stock tire size — a Badlands without Sasquatch is still a capable truck with a rear locker and a disconnecting front sway bar
  • Skip it if you already have a specific aftermarket suspension architecture in mind that replaces the Bilsteins and flares anyway; you would be paying twice for half of it

What about 37s?

Sasquatch is the cheapest entry point to a 37-inch build, but do not read that as bolt-on. The 4.70 ratio is already in the right neighborhood for a 37, which removes the single most expensive obstacle. What remains is clearance: the flares and the factory offset were sized around a 35, so 37s generally need a leveling or lift component and often a different wheel to keep the tire off the flare and the front control arm at full lock and full compression. Owners who went this route consistently describe it as a fitment project, not a tire swap. Starting from a non-Sasquatch truck, you have that fitment project plus a re-gear and a locker.

Buying a used Sasquatch: verify, do not trust the listing

Sasquatch content is priced into the used market, which means listings claim it liberally. Wheels and tires are the easiest thing in the world to swap onto a truck that never left the factory with the package, and the parts that matter are the parts you cannot see from the driveway.

Check the front axle. Every sixth-generation Bronco runs an M220 rear, so the rear tells you nothing. The front is the split: M190 on base and mid trims, M210 on Sasquatch-equipped and Badlands trucks, and the M210 is what carries the front locker. Then confirm the axle ratio rather than taking the seller's word — the window sticker by VIN is the fastest route, and counting driveshaft turns per wheel revolution is the free one. If a truck has 35s and a 3.73 or 4.46 ratio, someone fitted tires to a non-Sasquatch truck and did not finish the job, and you are looking at a re-gear bill you did not budget for.

The honest verdict

Sasquatch is one of the few factory off-road packages where the content is worth more than the price. That is unusual and worth saying plainly — most packages of this kind are trim and badges with a token capability item buried inside. This one is gears, a front locker, a heavier front axle and real dampers, and every one of those items is a five-figure conversation once the truck is out of the showroom.

But that only holds if you use it. Buy it because you are going to 35s, because you want a front locker, because you intend to air down and put the truck somewhere that requires it. Do not buy it because the wheels look right in the configurator. If the honest answer is that this truck is going to spend ninety percent of its life on pavement, the money is better spent on the trim above, or kept — and the non-Sasquatch Badlands will still take you further than most people ever ask a vehicle to go.
