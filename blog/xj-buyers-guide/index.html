XJ Cherokee · Buyer’s Guide

Buying a Used XJ Cherokee: What to Inspect Before You Pay

Rust decides an XJ purchase and almost nothing else does. The Cherokee is a unibody — there is no separate frame, so the stamped rails running the length of the floor are the structure holding the suspension to the vehicle. Rot at the rear leaf spring hangers, the front lower control arm mounts, or the steering box mount is structural, expensive, and usually terminal.

Everything else you will find on a thirty-year-old Cherokee — a weeping rear main seal, a tired AW4, a cracked exhaust manifold, worn ball joints, a dead blend door — is a repair with a known price, and it is a number you negotiate with. So run the inspection in that order: get under the vehicle with a flashlight and a screwdriver before you ever pop the hood. A clean rust-free XJ with a blown head gasket is a good buy. A mechanically perfect XJ with holes in the rear rails is scrap with good parts on it.

September 21, 2026 · 11 min read · How we source guides
The direct answer

Inspect the unibody rails first, mechanicals second. Rust at the leaf spring hangers, front control arm mounts or steering box mount is the only true deal-breaker. Target 1997–1999 for the refreshed interior and the 4.0L High Output six. Budget $9,000–$15,000 for a rust-free driver, and walk from anything where a screwdriver goes through a rail.

Why rust is the whole decision on an XJ

A TJ or a Tacoma has a ladder frame with a body bolted on top. Rot the body and you have a cosmetic problem. The XJ does not work that way. When Jeep launched the Cherokee in 1984 it was one of the first unibody SUVs on the market, and that is a large part of why it weighs around 3,200 lb and drives better than anything else of its era. The trade is that the structure and the bodywork are the same pressed-steel assembly. The front lower control arms, the track bar, the steering box and the rear leaf springs all bolt directly into that assembly.

So when an XJ owner says “frame rust,” they mean rust in a boxed section of sheet metal that is holding a suspension mounting point. Perforate it and the mount is no longer reliably attached to the vehicle. There is no bolt-on replacement frame. The fix is cutting the rot out and welding in new steel — either fabricated patches or one of the laser-cut reinforcement kits sold for exactly this failure. That is real work: a competent shop will quote four figures for a pair of rear rails, and doing it yourself means a welder, a way to support the vehicle safely, and a weekend or several.

None of which makes it impossible. Plenty of XJs have been saved this way and are on the trail today. But you should decide to take on a rust repair deliberately, with the price reflecting it, not discover it two months after you bought the thing.

The ten-minute rust crawl, in order

Bring a bright flashlight, an old flat-blade screwdriver, and clothes you do not care about. Tap and prod — looking is not enough. Sound steel rings and resists; rotten steel gives, crumbles, or goes straight through. Work this order:

1. Rear rails at the leaf spring hangers and shackles. This is the number one XJ killer. Go to the very back, find where the rear leaf spring shackle bolts into the rail, and prod the rail above and around it. If a trailer hitch is bolted to the underside of the rear rails, look harder — hitch brackets trap salt and moisture against the steel and hide what they are doing.

2. Front lower control arm mounts. Under the front floor, where the lower control arms pivot into the body. Rot here lets the mount move under load. Check both sides.

3. The steering box mount. Driver-side rail, behind the front wheel. The XJ steering box is famous for cracking and tearing its mounting area, which is why several companies sell weld-on reinforcement plates for it. Have someone rock the steering wheel side to side with the engine running while you watch the rail for flex.

4. Rockers and floor pans. Pull the carpet back at the front footwells and behind the front seats. Damp carpet is a warning; soft or perforated pan is a repair. Floor pans are patchable and not a deal-breaker on their own, but a rotten floor usually means the rails underneath have been sitting in the same water.

5. Cowl, under the windshield. Leaves and water collect here, rot the cowl, and then leak into the passenger footwell. Check for staining and damp under the dash.

6. Tailgate bottom, rear quarters and wheel arches. Cosmetic in isolation, but they tell you how the vehicle lived. A bubbling tailgate on a car with clean rails usually means a leaky rear glass seal, not a rotten body.

Geography is the strongest single predictor. A Cherokee that spent its life in Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico or west Texas is in a different category from an identical car from Ohio or upstate New York, and a dry-climate XJ is worth paying a real premium for. Many people buy a southwestern XJ sight-unseen and have it shipped rather than fight rust locally — and when the alternative is a four-figure welding bill, that math often works.

Which years to target

The XJ ran from 1984 to 2001 in the US with two meaningful dividing lines and one cautionary note.

1987–1990 — Renix 4.0. The 4.0L inline-six arrived for 1987 with Renix fuel injection, around 173 hp, and a notably fat low-end torque curve that many owners genuinely prefer on the trail. The catch is the electronics: more sensors, more wiring, an EGR valve, a smaller throttle body, and no self-diagnostic mode. Parts are still available but thinner on the ground, and diagnosis takes more knowledge. A good one is a good Jeep; it is not the one to learn on.

1991–1996 — High Output. The HO injection upgrade lifted the 4.0 to roughly 190 hp and brought far better parts availability and on-board diagnostics. Mechanically this is the XJ most people should want. The interior is the older design and 1991–1995 cars are OBD-I.

1997–1999 — the sweet spot. The 1997 refresh brought a new dash, restyled front and rear fascias, standard dual airbags and body improvements, while keeping the HO 4.0. OBD-II means a $30 code reader tells you what is wrong. If you are buying one XJ to drive and build, start here.

2000–2001 — the 0331 head years. Identical in most respects to a 1999, but every 4.0 in these two years left the factory with the 0331 cylinder head casting, made from a bad batch of iron and prone to cracking between the valve springs of cylinders three and four. Not all of them crack — some are past 200,000 miles, and others let go under 70,000. Do not treat this as an automatic pass. Treat it as an inspection item and a negotiating lever.

Also worth knowing: the 2.5L four-cylinder exists and is durable, but it is slow with anything larger than stock tires and it makes a lifted build unpleasant. The 2.8L V6 in 1984–1986 cars is best avoided entirely. For a build, you want a 4.0.

Engine inspection: the 4.0 checklist

The 4.0 is one of the most durable engines ever put in a mass-market vehicle, and 250,000 miles is unremarkable. Neglect still catches up with it.

Coolant contamination. Pull the oil cap and the dipstick. Tan or milky emulsion means coolant is getting into the oil — head gasket or cracked head. Check the overflow bottle with the engine warm: persistent bubbling is combustion gas in the cooling system. On a 2000–01 car, this is the 0331 test.

Head casting number. On 2000–01 cars, the casting number is stamped on the driver side of the head above the number four exhaust port. If it has already been replaced with a good aftermarket casting, that is a genuine plus — ask for the receipt.

Exhaust manifold. Listen for a tick at cold start that quiets as the engine warms. The 4.0 manifold cracks, usually at the rear. It is a known repair and a fair price adjustment, not a walk-away.

Oil leaks. Expect some. Valve cover and oil filter adapter seals are minor. A wet bellhousing means rear main seal, which is a transmission-out job and a legitimate several-hundred-dollar deduction.

Hot restart. Drive it until fully warm, shut it off, wait ten minutes, and restart. A no-start or stumble is the classic crankshaft position sensor failure — cheap to fix, but you want to know before it strands you.

Temperature behavior. The gauge should settle around the middle and stay there, including at idle in traffic with the A/C on. A needle that climbs in slow traffic and drops at speed is a cooling system on borrowed time.

Drivetrain: what is under there

AW4 automatic. The Aisin-Warner four-speed is the durable choice and routinely outlives the body. It should shift crisply and hold each gear. Flaring between shifts, a shudder, or delayed engagement from a stop means it has been run on old fluid. Check the fluid: pink and clear is right, brown and burnt is a warning.

AX15 manual. Fine gearbox. Check for grinding into second and third, and for a clutch that engages right at the top of the pedal travel.

Transfer case. Most XJs have the NP231 Command-Trac part-time case; the NP242 Selec-Trac full-time case was an option and is genuinely nicer on the road. Shift into 4-Hi and 4-Lo on a flat surface, both should engage and release without a fight. A case that will not go into low range is a real cost.

Axles. Dana 30 front on everything. Rear is either a Dana 35 or a Chrysler 8.25 — check, because the 8.25 is meaningfully stronger and worth seeking out. The Dana 35 is fine behind a stock 4.0 on tires up to about 31″. Past that, or with a locker, it becomes the weak link. Look for fluid seeping from the axle tube ends and listen for whine on a steady-speed drive.

Steering and suspension

Ask the seller directly whether the Jeep has ever had death wobble. The answer matters less than how they answer it — death wobble on an XJ traces to worn track bar ends, ball joints, tie rod ends or a loose steering box, all of which are fixable. A seller who denies any steering issue while the track bar bushing is visibly destroyed is telling you something about the rest of their answers.

Track bar: grab it and try to move it at both ends. It should be rigid. Any play is a worn bushing or joint.

Ball joints: with the front wheel off the ground, rock it at 12 and 6 o’clock. Movement means worn joints. Rock at 9 and 3 to check wheel bearing play.

Steering box: with the engine running, small wheel inputs should move the front tires immediately. An inch of dead play at the rim is a box that is worn or in need of adjustment.

Rear leaf springs: look at them from the side. Flat or reverse-arched springs mean a saggy, bottomed-out rear end — common, and worth pricing in if you want the vehicle to sit level.

What a modified XJ tells you

Modifications add value only when the work was done properly, and the quality of the work is a reliable proxy for how the rest of the vehicle was treated. A lift installed with the correct geometry corrections, an alignment afterward, and a receipt is an asset. A stack of used parts bolted on over a weekend with the steering geometry left where it fell is a liability you are paying extra for.

Specifically:

Lift over 3″: ask about the slip yoke eliminator and driveshaft. Test drive at 55–70 mph. Vibration that builds with speed is a driveline angle problem, and fixing it properly is not cheap.

Big tires, stock gears: a 33″ tire on stock 3.55 gearing behaves like roughly 3.10 — drivable, sluggish, and hard on the transmission. Ask the ratio and do the math. A re-gear is $1,200–$1,800 per axle.

Wiring: lift the hood and look at how the lights, winch and stereo were wired. Unfused runs, taped splices and wire routed against hot or sharp surfaces are a fire risk and a sign of how everything else was done.

What it should cost in 2026

XJ prices have firmed up considerably as the platform has crossed into collectible territory, and they vary more by region and rust than by mileage. Broad ranges:

$1,500–$4,000: Project. Rust, high miles, unknown history, or not currently running. Only worth it if you have the tools and want the project.

$4,500–$8,000: Running driver. 180,000-plus miles, cosmetic rust, a list of deferred maintenance. The most common XJ on the market.

$9,000–$15,000: Genuinely rust-free, under about 150,000 miles, with service history. This is what you should aim for if you plan to build it.

$18,000–$30,000+: Low-mileage survivors, clean Limiteds, and documented restorations. Classic.com tracks an average XJ sale near $13,000 and a top recorded sale of $42,000 for a 2001 Limited — but that data comes from curated auction listings, not the private-party market where most Cherokees still trade well below it. Do not let an auction comp anchor what you pay for a driver.

Here is the unvarnished version: the most expensive XJ mistake is buying a cheap rusty one because the price looked good. The gap between a $4,000 rusty Cherokee and a $10,000 clean one is smaller than the cost of making the rusty one structurally sound, and you will spend that difference whether you planned to or not.

Price adjustment for what you find

Rough 2026 figures to negotiate against. Parts-only costs assume you do the work:

Front ball joints: $150–$350 in parts, plus a press. Track bar: $90–$200. Wheel bearing hub assembly: $70–$150 per side. Exhaust manifold: $200–$400 in parts, $600–$900 at a shop. Head gasket: $250–$400 in parts, $1,000–$1,800 at a shop. Complete head replacement on a cracked 0331: $1,500–$2,800 installed. Rear main seal: $400–$800 at a shop. Radiator and water pump together: $250–$450 in parts. Blend door repair: cheap part, miserable job, budget $500–$900 at a shop because the dash comes out. Re-gear: $1,200–$1,800 per axle. Rear unibody rail repair: $1,200–$3,000-plus at a fabrication shop, and more if it has spread.

Two or three items off that list do not make a Cherokee a bad buy. They make it a car with a number attached. Go in knowing the numbers and the conversation gets a lot easier.

The walk-away list

Short and non-negotiable:

  1. A screwdriver goes through a unibody rail — especially at a leaf spring hanger, control arm mount or the steering box area.
  2. Coolant in the oil plus a seller who says it is nothing — the engine is a known quantity; the honesty is not.
  3. Salvage or rebuilt title on a unibody vehicle — you cannot see what was straightened, and the structure is the body.
  4. A build with no receipts and visible corner-cutting — you are buying someone else’s shortcuts at full price.
  5. A seller who will not let you get underneath it — there is exactly one reason for that.

Everything else is a conversation. Run the VIN through a history service before you drive out — $40 to confirm the title status and spot a flood history is the cheapest part of this whole process. Then take an hour, take the flashlight, and take your time. There are still good XJs out there, and the people who end up with them are the ones who were willing to walk away from three bad ones first.
