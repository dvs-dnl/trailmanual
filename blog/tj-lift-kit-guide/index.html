TJ Wrangler Lift Kit Guide: Short Arm, Long Arm, and Everything That Goes With It

The TJ's coil-sprung suspension makes lift kits cleaner than the leaf-sprung generation before it — but lift still has a cascade of requirements. Here's what you need for each tire size, what the geometry tradeoffs actually mean, and how to do it right the first time.

May 2026 · 11 min read
The direct answer

For 33-inch tires: 3–3.5 inches of lift with a quality short arm kit, adjustable track bars, and a slip yoke eliminator plus CV rear driveshaft. Budget $2,000–$3,500 in parts and 8–12 hours of work. For 35s: 4–4.5 inches with all of the above and ideally a long arm or adjustable-arm kit. Budget $3,500–$5,500. Don't start with the lift — start with the tires you want to run and work backward.

Why lift height is about tires, not ground clearance

Every inch of lift you add exists to create clearance for a larger tire. The TJ on 31-inch tires at stock height has adequate trail ground clearance for most situations — the stock fender openings are the limit, not the suspension. When you go to 32 or 33-inch tires, the tires start rubbing the fenders and the lower control arms under full compression and full lock. Lift creates the space the larger tire needs to move freely.

The relationship is roughly: 2 inches of lift clears 31–32-inch tires. 3–3.5 inches clears 33s. 4–4.5 inches clears 35s. Beyond 4.5 inches with 35s, you're in long arm territory and the geometry demands become significant. Don't lift higher than your tires require — each inch adds weight to the front end, raises the center of gravity, and introduces another set of geometry corrections.

Short arm vs. long arm: what the difference actually means on trail

The TJ uses four control arms per axle to locate it — two upper and two lower at the front, two upper and two lower at the rear. These arms are the geometry. As you add lift, the arm angles change: the lower control arms point more steeply downward from the frame to the axle, which rotates the axle forward through the suspension arc and reduces caster (the front axle's steering geometry angle). Drop enough caster and the TJ steers vaguely and is susceptible to death wobble.

Short arm kits retain the stock control arm pivot location on the frame. To recover lost caster, they use adjustable control arms or offset control arm cam bolts. Good short arm kits (Rubicon Express Super-Flex, Old Man Emu, Metalcloak Game-Changer) do this correctly. Budget coil spacer kits ignore caster entirely — which is why cheap lift kits have a reputation for causing death wobble that good kits don't deserve.

Long arm kits move the control arm pivot point rearward on the frame, which flattens the arm angle at lift height and brings the geometry much closer to stock. The result is better ride quality, more wheel travel, and more stable steering at higher lift heights. The cost: long arm kits require welding new frame brackets onto the unibody, they're expensive ($2,500–$4,000 just for the kit), and they're not reversible. This is the right answer for a dedicated 4.5-inch+ build running 35s or larger on serious terrain. It's not necessary — and arguably not justified — for a 3.5-inch street-and-mild-trail TJ.

The slip yoke eliminator: not optional on lifted TJs

The NP231 transfer case outputs to the rear driveshaft through a slip yoke — a splined shaft that telescopes in and out as the driveshaft moves. At stock ride height, this works fine. At 3 inches of lift and above, the rear driveshaft operating angle at the slip yoke becomes steep enough to create vibration at highway speeds. You'll feel it as a rhythmic shudder at 55–70 mph that gets worse under acceleration.

The fix is a slip yoke eliminator (SYE) kit, which converts the NP231 output to a fixed yoke and requires a custom CV (constant velocity) rear driveshaft to replace the factory driveshaft. Budget $600–$900 for the SYE kit and custom driveshaft together. Advance Adapters and Tom Wood's Custom Driveshafts are the two common sources. Plan the SYE as part of the lift installation — doing it afterward requires pulling the transmission again.

What a complete 3.5-inch TJ lift actually includes

A correct 3.5-inch lift on a TJ is not just new springs and shocks. Here's the full list:

Coil springs (all four corners): Rate them for your expected weight. A front steel bumper and winch add 80–120 lb up front — under-sprung and the front will sag. $300–$600 depending on brand.

Shocks: New springs with old shocks is a waste. Match shock length to lift height. Bilstein 5100s are the benchmark for value. Fox 2.0 for better off-road performance. $300–$600 for four.

Adjustable track bars (front and rear): At 3+ inches, the axles shift laterally without track bar correction. Adjustable bars bring the axles back to center. $150–$280 per bar.

Adjustable control arms: Needed to correct caster after lift. Most quality short arm kits include these. $400–$800 if buying separately.

SYE + CV rear driveshaft: See above. $600–$900.

Extended sway bar links: Stock links are too short at 3+ inches of lift. $60–$120.

Extended brake lines: Required at 3+ inches. Stock lines pull tight at full droop. $60–$100.

Four-wheel alignment: Non-negotiable after any lift. $100–$180 at a shop that knows Jeeps. Verify they can set TJ caster, not just toe and camber.

Kit recommendations by budget

Budget (under $800 for lift components): Rough Country 3.5-inch short arm kit. Gets the Jeep up, includes everything needed, and will be functional. Shocks are the weak point — expect to replace them within 30,000 miles of trail use. Fine for a first Jeep that isn't doing serious wheeling yet.

Mid-range ($1,200–$2,000 for lift components): Rubicon Express Super-Flex, Old Man Emu 2.5-inch with Nitrocharger shocks, or Metalcloak 3.5-inch Game-Changer. Better bushing quality, better shocks, better geometry correction. These kits ride better, wheel better, and last longer. This is where most serious TJ builds end up.

Premium ($2,500+ for lift components): Metalcloak Game-Changer 4.5-inch, Currie long arm, Clayton Off Road long arm. For dedicated trail rigs that are going to 35s and beyond. The long arm kits require welding — budget for professional installation if you don't have a welder.

What to do about re-gearing

Stock TJ gears are 3.07 or 3.73 depending on year and trim. On 33s, 3.73 gears are adequate for daily driving and moderate trails but feel sluggish under load. On 35s, 3.73 gears will wear out your clutch or torque converter faster than necessary. The correct gear ratio for 33s is 4.10, and for 35s is 4.56. If you're already pulling the axles for other reasons (locker install, rear axle swap), re-gear at the same time — the labor overlaps significantly.
